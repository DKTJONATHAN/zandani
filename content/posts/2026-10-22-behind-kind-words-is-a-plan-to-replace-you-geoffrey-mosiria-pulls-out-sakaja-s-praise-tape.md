Geoffrey Mosiria is not leaving City Hall quietly; he is leaving with receipts.

Hours after his exit from the Nairobi County executive, the former Chief Officer went into the digital archives to unearth recorded footage of Governor Johnson Sakaja lavishing praise on his stewardship of the county health docket. The specific clip Mosiria shared on Thursday, October 22, 2026, captured Sakaja commending him for pulling off a major administrative feat: delivering a functional intensive care unit without requesting a single extra shilling from county coffers.

For Mosiria, that old compliment was not shared as a sentimental goodbye. It was presented as exhibit A in an unexpected workplace post-mortem.

According to Mosiria, setting up specialized medical infrastructure on a lean budget did not cement his tenure under the city canopy. Instead, he argued, it signed his professional death warrant by making him appear too visible, too effective, and ultimately uncomfortable to have on the roster.

“My hard work is what has cost me my job,” Mosiria wrote on his Instagram page. “When you see your boss praising you for working hard, just know that behind those kind words may be a plan to replace you.”

### The Anatomy of an Archive Drop

The move marks a theatrical pivot from a bruised bureaucrat into an aggrieved corporate whistleblower. Rather than contesting the procedural technicalities of his dismissal, Mosiria framed his removal as a universal corporate tragedy: the diligent operative discarded precisely because his output outshone the boardroom.

By recirculating Sakaja’s own glowing words about the ICU project, Mosiria neatly flipped the dismissal narrative. If the governor had once publicly attested to his fiscal prudence and operational speed, Mosiria’s public rebuttal went, any subsequent dismissal could not credibly be blamed on incompetence.

Instead, Mosiria offered his experience as an unsolicited survival tip for professionals navigating delicate institutional hierarchies, warning that excessive enthusiasm can backfire when leadership feels eclipsed.

### Courting the 23,000-Strong Workforce

Having framed his exit as punishment for diligence, Mosiria immediately pivoted his public grievance into an open pitch for political elevation. His target audience was neither the Nairobi elite nor the traditional party barons, but the sprawling, often demoralized ranks of City Hall’s internal bureaucracy.

Directing his message to Nairobi County’s estimated 23,000-member workforce, the former chief officer presented himself as one of their own—a fellow staffer battered by unpredictable administrative weather.

“Nairobians will take away my shame next year!” Mosiria declared, setting his sights firmly on the August 2027 gubernatorial ballot. “I will teach my boss an important lesson: the best employees are supported, not fired for working hard.”

He pledged that if county voters promote him from ousted officer to governor, his administration will eliminate political coercion and protect workers from what he described as systemic executive failures. Mosiria told staff that he already possesses “a plan to end their suffering from August next year,” arguing that front-line workers have long borne the blame for blunders manufactured on the upper executive floors.

### The Road to August 2027

Mosiria’s retaliatory bid adds a volatile internal dynamic to Nairobi’s upcoming electoral cycle. Where outsider candidates typically campaign against City Hall from the fence line, Mosiria brings intimate knowledge of the administration's internal friction, having sat through the cabinet sessions and operated the dockets.

His declaration that voters will vindicate him converts a routine administrative reshuffle into an ideological duel over workplace loyalty and political threat management. By insisting that his reform mission remains alive, Mosiria has signaled that every past executive praise clip in his camera roll could become campaign trail fodder over the next ten months.

Whether his pitch resonates with Nairobi’s electorate remains to be seen, but inside the corridors of City Hall, Mosiria has ensured that Sakaja’s past words will continue echoing well past the dismissal letter.
