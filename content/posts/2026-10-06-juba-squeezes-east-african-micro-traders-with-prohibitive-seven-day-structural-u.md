East African micro-traders operating in South Sudan’s capital face an immediate threat to their livelihoods following an aggressive municipal directive that combines targeted trade bans with a prohibitive seven-day infrastructure ultimatum. Juba County’s sudden policy shift effectively prices out informal operators by requiring roadside and open-air traders to rapidly transition to concrete structures equipped with electronic signage or face demolition.

The directive, announced on Monday, October 5, by Juba County Caretaker Commissioner Kalisto Lado, targets traders from Kenya, Uganda, Ethiopia, Eritrea, and Burundi. In a swift tightening of municipal regulations, the county has declared that small-scale retail and informal transport sectors are now exclusively reserved for South Sudanese citizens.

Specifically, foreign nationals are prohibited from operating roadside chapati stalls, running boda boda (motorcycle taxi) services, or selling household essentials such as tomatoes, onions, and charcoal. Lado stated that foreign operators must either exit the retail economy entirely or pivot to wholesale operations and larger capital investments that employ local citizens.

"The retail business is only for South Sudanese. If you are a foreigner and want to invest, you do wholesale so that our people can buy from you, or you can employ South Sudanese," Lado said during a visit to the Kuburi Haboba market in Juba.

### The Seven-Day Compliance Hurdle

For roadside and open-stall operators who do not fall under the immediate outright bans, the county has set a strict timeline to meet complex municipal standards. To escape demolition, these informal traders must construct permanent concrete stalls, acquire formal business licenses, and display their business names on electronic signs.

While Lado framed the measures as necessary steps to improve sanitation, maintain order, and secure commercial areas against criminal activity, the suddenness of the directive has caught thousands of cross-border traders off guard. 

"We are here to protect lives. We are here to maintain order. We are here for development and service delivery. We want this place clean and organised to prevent criminals from taking your things," Lado added, emphasizing that business operations in Juba must strictly align with domestic law.

![Kalisto Lado, Juba County Caretaker Commissioner, at a past event.](https://i.ibb.co/7xGdhkx5/4fc994ed9ed3.webp)

However, the county government has not specified how it expects small-scale traders to mobilize the capital required for permanent concrete construction and electronic displays within a single week.

### A Regional Trend of Protectionism

This micro-retail crackdown in South Sudan mirrors a growing wave of economic protectionism across the East African Community. It comes just weeks after Kenyan President William Ruto issued a similar directive ordering a crackdown on foreign nationals engaged in hawking and small-scale retail trade in Kenya. Although Ruto later clarified that Kenya remains open to law-abiding foreign investors and warned against harassing foreigners, the initial pronouncements triggered diplomatic friction and raised concerns about reciprocal measures from neighboring states.

Juba's move highlights the fragile nature of cross-border commerce within the region, where municipal and national administrative decisions frequently override regional treaties promoting the free movement of goods, services, and labor.

The immediate challenge for affected Kenyan, Ugandan, and Ethiopian traders in Juba is the lack of transitional support. The municipal government has not yet clarified how the retail bans will be policed, or what legal recourse remains for foreigners who have already invested in Juba's informal markets. As the seven-day countdown begins, roadside traders must now decide whether to attempt a costly, high-speed structural upgrade or liquidate their stock before the demolition crews arrive.
