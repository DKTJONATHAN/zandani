Gospel singer Size 8 has laid bare the extensive neurological damage and physiological crises that marked her journey to motherhood, detailing how consecutive high-risk pregnancies escalated into partial paralysis and speech loss before doctors intervened to deliver her second-born child.

Speaking in an in-depth conversation alongside her husband DJ Mo and fellow gospel artist Daddy Owen, the singer, born Linet Munyali, revisited the severe medical complications that accompanied the arrival of their son, Muraya Junior. While public attention often centers on the glamour of celebrity family life, Size 8 ed the private medical reality that saw her body repeatedly shut down under maternal distress.

During her pregnancy with Junior, the singer experienced debilitating neurological complications that crippled the left side of her body. The nerve damage became so acute that she temporarily lost the ability to speak, requiring specialized maternal-fetal surveillance just to sustain the pregnancy.

Medical specialists monitored the high-risk condition around the clock, managing to nurse the pregnancy to the 28-week mark before determining that keeping the baby in utero posed an immediate threat to both mother and child.

"Hiyo pregnancy ilibombelezwa hadi ikafika 28 weeks," Size 8 noted, recalling the delicate interventions required to push her gestation to the seventh month.

### The Dual-Magnesium Delivery Crisis

When doctors initiated the emergency delivery, the complications multiplied rapidly inside the theatre. Junior was born severely preterm, weighing just 1.7 kilograms, and was in immediate danger of respiratory and systemic collapse.

Both mother and newborn had to be administered magnesium sulfate intravenously—a critical treatment deployed to manage severe hypertensive emergencies, neurological instability, and prevent fatal neonatal outcomes.

“When I gave birth to Junior, niliweka Magnesium, ata Junior aliwekewa, alikuwa anakufa juu nilimzaa preterm 1.7 kgs,” Size 8 explained.

The urgency in the labour ward reached a point where medical personnel ordered DJ Mo to maintain an uninterrupted bedside vigil. Because falling asleep carried the real danger of slipping into unconsciousness from which she might not recover, medical staff tasked her husband with keeping her awake by any means necessary, even as he managed his own private panic.

“I remember it was traumatising. Nikiwa labour ward, DJ Mo aliambiwa anikeep company nisilale, juu nikilala nitakufa. Alikuwa anakeep company na bado amepanic,” she said.

DJ Mo recalled the disorienting pressure of manufacturing conversation and forced humor under acute hospital stress: “Umepanic na hafai kulala, na kwa kumwamusha huwezi mwimbia; lazima ucrack jokes na ufanye kitu; lazima ataamka.”

### A Pattern of Consecutive Maternal Trauma

Junior’s delivery was not an isolated crisis. It was the culmination of three successive reproductive emergencies that took an compounding emotional and physical toll on the couple.

Their road began with the birth of their firstborn daughter, which ended in an emergency Caesarean section and the newborn being rushed straight into the Intensive Care Unit (ICU). A subsequent pregnancy ended in a tragic miscarriage, deepening the domestic grief before she conceived Junior.

By the time Junior arrived at 28 weeks, the repeated hospitalizations and near-fatal emergencies had left deep psychological scars on the household.

For Size 8, recounting the nerve damage, the 1.7-kilogram preterm battle, and the bedside intervention serves as a frank record of the physical cost that shadowed her public career, highlighting a survival story that extended far beyond the stage.
