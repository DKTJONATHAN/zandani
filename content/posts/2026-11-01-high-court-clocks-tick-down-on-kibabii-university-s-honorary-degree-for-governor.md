A fast-moving legal challenge is putting the spotlight on how public universities award high-level recognition to sitting political figures, just weeks before a scheduled academic ceremony in Western Kenya.

Lady Justice Emily Ominde has certified a petition filed by the Consumer Federation of Kenya (COFEK) as urgent, compressing the timeline for respondents to reply to questions surrounding Kibabii University's plan to confer a Doctor of Humane Letters on Bungoma Governor Kenneth Lusaka. The court has directed that the petition be served within three days, giving respondents a five-day window to file their responses ahead of a scheduled mention on November 12.

![Bungoma Governor Kenneth Lusaka](https://i.ibb.co/prfBRKJK/31c3576cacbf.webp)

The friction sits directly between the institution's statutory framework and consumer oversight. Kibabii University issued a formal notice relying on Section 9(1)(c) of the Kibabii University Charter, 2015, and Statute XLIV of its 2021 Statutes, citing Lusaka's role in public service, devolved governance, and his historical backing of the university's 2015 Charter acquisition and academic program development.

COFEK, however, is asking the High Court to scrutinize whether the distinction is an independent academic honour or an administrative entanglement tied to Lusaka's executive office. Central to the lobby group's petition is whether the university adhered to the Commission for University Education (CUE) guidelines, which demand transparent, objective, and fair selection metrics for honorary conferments.

![Undated photo of students outside Kibabii University in Bungoma](https://i.ibb.co/675w4Sqy/759d70c0deb9.webp)

While COFEK Secretary General Stephen Mutoro emphasized that the federation does not oppose acknowledging leaders for public service contributions, the legal challenge targets the timing, process, and institutional relationship underpinning the award.

With the university's 11th graduation ceremony slated for November 20—just eight days after the court mention on November 12—the judicial review will test whether external oversight can alter the final stages of a senate-backed academic recommendation.
