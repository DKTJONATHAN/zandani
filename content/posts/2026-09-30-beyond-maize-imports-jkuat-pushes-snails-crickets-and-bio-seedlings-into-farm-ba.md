As state authorities prepare to import over one million bags of maize from Zambia to cushion against poor rainfall performance, public researchers are attempting to pivot smallholders toward fast-maturing alternative proteins and tissue-culture fruit crops.

Speaking at the Nairobi International Trade Fair on Wednesday, September 30, Jomo Kenyatta University of Agriculture and Technology (JKUAT) Vice Chancellor Victoria Wambui outlined a commercialization strategy focused on micro-livestock and specialized plant propagation designed to diversify smallholder income.

### Micro-Livestock Protocols at the Trade Fair

Rather than presenting edible insects and mollusks purely as emergency relief rations, the university framed cricket and snail production as commercially viable ventures for peri-urban and rural farmers.

JKUAT’s exhibition stand demonstrated harvesting, processing, and culinary techniques for visitors, with university officers offering step-by-step guidance on how to establish small-scale units.

"We are proposing the use of crickets and snails as food, and if you visit our stand, you'll be able to learn how you can even cook some of these foods," Wambui told attendees.

![An undated image of Jomo Kenyatta University of Agriculture and Technology (JKUAT) students with an insert of snails](https://i.ibb.co/cXvYkQVG/fdd20221aa36.webp)

The Vice Chancellor stressed that integrating non-traditional protein farming provides double utility: providing immediate household nutrition while opening high-margin local market niches.

While insect and snail farming maintain established commercial footprints across markets in Asia, Europe, and West Africa, domestic adoption in Kenya has remained localized. The university's initiative seeks to lower technical barriers by pairing direct farmer training with standardized breeding protocols.

### Commercializing Bio-Tech Seedlings

Alongside insect husbandry, JKUAT launched commercial distribution of specialized seedlings developed in its plant tissue-culture laboratories.

The institution has established standardized tissue-culture protocols for plantain and dragon fruit—two high-value horticultural options selected for their climatic resilience and quick turnaround times.

Seedlings developed under these sterile lab conditions are being sold directly to farmers attending the trade fair to monitor field growth rates and fruit output across different agro-ecological zones.

![A bag of dried crickets.](https://i.ibb.co/JwT6sTR8/6e9c8d6bf918.webp)

"We have come up with very good protocols in the area of tissue culture for things like plantain and dragon fruit, and therefore these are other seedlings that we are selling to Kenyans," Wambui noted.

By supplying clean, pathogen-free planting material alongside extension training, the university aims to move academic research out of laboratories and directly onto practical farm balance sheets.

### Macro Crisis Versus Micro-Farming

The academic push coincides with escalating national food security pressure. Failed seasonal rains and forecasted October rainfall volatility have forced the national government to rely on regional grain purchases to plug supply deficits.

While macro-scale grain imports address immediate shortfalls in urban centers, JKUAT's focus on micro-farming target structural resilience at the farm gate.

By pairing high-density protein farming like crickets with high-value horticultural crops like dragon fruit, researchers are testing whether smallholders can generate steady cash flow during seasons when conventional staple crops fail.

The long-term viability of the university’s initiative will depend on how effectively its training programs transition curious trade fair visitors into active, commercially producing farmers over the coming planting season.
