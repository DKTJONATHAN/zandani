Dr. Neema Rusibamayila Kimambo stepped into the room with an uncompromising message for those calling to seal Kenya’s borders: shutting official gates will not stop Ebola—it will only blindfold the doctors trying to catch it.

Speaking on Wednesday, October 14, 2026, during a High-Level Media Engagement and National Outbreak Reporting Workshop, the World Health Organization (WHO) Country Representative to Kenya took direct aim at the mounting public pressure to lock down points of entry. Rather than protecting the country, Dr. Kimambo warned, blanket closures would immediately backfire by pushing travelers into the shadows.

“Our borders are very porous. If we close the borders, people will use unofficial borders, and you will not be able to screen,” Dr. Kimambo stated, laying out the ground reality facing surveillance teams. “Better use official borders so that you can do the screening and pick up those at risk.”

### The Illusion of Sealed Crossings

The public debate over border controls escalated following the confirmation of Kenya’s first imported Ebola case on October 6, 2026. The patient, a Kenyan citizen who had been living in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), undertook an extended journey across regional lines. He moved by road through Kampala, Uganda, before boarding a commercial flight to Nairobi on October 3. Upon arrival, he was isolated in a hospital facility and passed away on October 5.

The fatality ignited immediate chatter and calls on social platforms demanding total travel restrictions to shield the country from further exposure. But Dr. Kimambo used her podium to explain why reflexive closures offer a false sense of security while creating far greater epidemiological blind spots.

According to the WHO country boss, closing designated checkpoints does not stop movement across extensive, unmanned regional frontiers. Instead, it strips health workers of the chance to catch symptomatic arrivals, effectively eliminating the early warning mechanisms needed to contain transmission.

### Keeping the Watchtowers Open

Dr. Kimambo maintained that operational border stations remain Kenya’s most vital line of defense. By keeping official checkpoints functional, the Ministry of Health can enforce systematic thermal screenings, review required health declaration forms, and isolate suspect travelers before they disappear into general circulation.

Driving cross-border movement underground, she argued, would instantly compromise contact tracing, leaving health desks unaware of who is entering the country, where they traveled from, or what symptoms they might be managing.

Beyond public health surveillance, Dr. Kimambo underscored the necessity of preserving economic stability. Blanket border shutdowns threaten essential trade corridors between Kenya and its inland neighbors, risking commercial disruptions and public panic without delivering medical protection.

For the WHO representative, containment rests on rigorous detection at existing ports of entry rather than attempting to enforce an unworkable regional blockade. As surveillance teams maintain active screening protocols at entry points, health authorities continue to lean on structured monitoring over isolationist policy to manage border risks.
