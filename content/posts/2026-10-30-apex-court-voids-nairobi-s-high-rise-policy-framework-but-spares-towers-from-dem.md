The Supreme Court has stripped Nairobi City County of its legal basis for approving high-rise developments over several years, establishing that City Hall issued construction permits under an draft policy that never received County Assembly approval.

Yet, while the apex court ruled that the county acted without statutory authority, it stopped short of ordering the tearing down of contested multi-storey towers in Westlands. The decision leaves dozens of completed and ongoing high-rise developments in a regulatory holding pattern, shifting the responsibility back to the Court of Appeal to determine whether City Hall's newly gazetted June 2026 planning framework can retrospectively or legally resolve the planning vacuum.

### The Assembly Approval Gap

In a judgment delivered on Friday, October 30, a five-judge bench chaired by Chief Justice Martha Koome found that the county government systematically bypassed legislative oversight by relying on the draft Nairobi City Development Control Policy 2021 to grant building approvals.

Because the 2021 document was never submitted to or passed by the Nairobi County Assembly, the Supreme Court ruled that the county possessed no legal mandate to use it as an evaluation tool for development applications.

![An aerial view of Nairobi City](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_style/public/images/media/nairobi%20city%20%281%29.jpg.webp?itok=qZ8h1-60)

"The absence of the County Assembly approved plan suspended the 2nd respondent’s ability to determine development applications on their merits," the bench observed, finding that City Hall had functioned without a valid spatial planning policy.

The legal challenge originated from a petition filed by the Rhapta Road Residents Association. Homeowners in the Muthangari area of Westlands disputed the authorization of high-rise residential towers scaling between 17 and 22 floors. The residents maintained that existing local zoning guidelines capped building heights at four floors, warning that unauthorized high-density developments were overwhelming local roads, water connections, and sewerage lines.

### Judicial Fault Lines Across Lower Benches

The apex court also faulted lower courts for repeatedly attempting to substitute technical planning decisions with arbitrary judicial height limits. 

Both the Environment and Land Court (ELC) and the Court of Appeal had previously evaluated the Muthangari dispute using the same unapproved 2021 policy. The ELC had capped the disputed developments at 16 floors, a limit the Court of Appeal later raised to 20 floors.

![Judiciary](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_inner/public/images/media/supreme.jpg.webp?itok=DtfHmFGA)

The Supreme Court clarified that courts lack the administrative and technical competency to draw zoning boundaries or set floor limits. Such determinations, the judges held, require formal infrastructure impact assessments, density studies, and statutory public participation conducted by legally mandated planning authorities.

### Demolition Standards and the 2026 Remittal

Despite declaring the county’s approval framework illegal, the apex court refused to issue demolition orders against the Westlands high-rises. 

Defining demolition as an "exceptional and most intrusive remedy," the judges stated that structural tear-downs require clear evidence of direct environmental degradation, severe statutory violations, or imminent risk to human life. The court ruled that the Rhapta Road residents had failed to present sufficient technical evidence demonstrating environmental harm or constitutional breaches attributable directly to the constructed towers.

Instead of ordering physical enforcement, the Supreme Court directed the case back to the Court of Appeal to resolve the outstanding regulatory compliance questions.

During the proceedings, City Hall disclosed that it had enacted a new framework—the Nairobi City County Development Control Policy 2026—which was adopted on June 18 and gazetted on June 19. Because the legal validity and retroactive applicability of the 2026 policy had not been cross-examined during the original appeal, the Supreme Court tasked appellate judges with determining whether the newly gazetted rules legally cure the city's long-standing planning deficits.
