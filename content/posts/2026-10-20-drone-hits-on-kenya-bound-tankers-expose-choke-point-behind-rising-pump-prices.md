Kenya’s vulnerability to international maritime warfare was laid bare this week when two commercial tankers transporting petroleum products to the country were struck by drones while traversing the Strait of Hormuz.

The disclosure, delivered before the National Assembly's Special Funds Accounts Committee, confirms that the geopolitical friction between Iran and Saudi Arabia has moved from an abstract global risk to an immediate physical threat along the maritime shipping lane that feeds East Africa’s largest economy.

Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA) Director General Edward Mwirigi Kinyua told lawmakers that although both vessels sustained structural damage to their propulsion machinery, their hazardous cargo remained intact.

“There has been escalating tension between Iran and Saudi Arabia and, as a result, two vessels destined for Kenya were hit by drones,” Kinyua testified, confirming that the fuel consignments aboard were preserved despite the strikes knocking out ship engines.

### Pressure on the Landed Cost Formula

![tanker-drone-fuel.jpg](https://i.ibb.co/zh5Qkf9q/c92a82fa5a56.webp)

The maritime security breach arrives at an exceptionally sensitive juncture for Kenya's energy pricing architecture. EPRA’s October 15 to November 14 pricing cycle pushed domestic retail tariffs to punishing heights, reflecting rising international procurement charges.

In Nairobi, Super Petrol escalated by Ksh12.93 to trade at Ksh226.96 per litre, while diesel climbed by Ksh9.90 to Ksh227.76 per litre. Kerosene recorded the sharpest jump, rising by Ksh28.49 to settle at Ksh219.87 per litre. At the coast, Mombasa motorists are paying Ksh223.81 for petrol, Ksh224.61 for diesel, and Ksh216.71 for kerosene.

Under Kenya's regulated petroleum pricing model, retail figures are tethered directly to the landed cost of imported cargo. That metric bundles benchmark crude quotes with ocean freight charges, maritime war-risk insurance premiums, and demurrage penalties incurred when ships suffer transit delays.

When commercial carriers sustain engine damage or require offshore emergency towing through contested waterways, shipping lines routinely reassess corridor risks, passing the inflated transit premiums and delayed docking schedules down the supply chain to final consumers.

### Diplomatic Hurdles in Doha

Efforts to establish a security cordon around commercial transit in the Persian Gulf remain bogged down by historical grievances, despite tentative diplomatic contacts.

Addressing delegates at the Global Security Forum in Doha, U.S. Ambassador to Türkiye and Special Envoy for Syria Tom Barrack acknowledged that negotiations aimed at establishing a maritime ceasefire in the Strait of Hormuz face deep mutual suspicion.

![Ugandan oil consignment docks at the Port of Mombasa on July 3, 2024.](https://i.ibb.co/0RBq9J5F/95f7ecb5c58c.webp)

“America wants a peace deal. What’s happened from the beginning of these discussions and negotiations is a lack of trust,” Barrack said in remarks reported by Al Jazeera. “IRGC does not trust the U.S. The U.S. does not trust the IRGC,” he added, referring to Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps.

Barrack indicated that any viable diplomatic framework would require balancing competing state interests, positing that an armistice in the Strait of Hormuz could serve as the bedrock for broader regional de-escalation.

### The Route to Mombasa

For Kenya, the waterway is not an academic security theater. A significant portion of the country's refined petroleum imports originates in Middle Eastern refineries, requiring supertankers to navigate the narrow passage between the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman before entering the open Indian Ocean toward the Port of Mombasa.

Any sustained threat to navigational safety along this passage leaves energy regulators with few alternative logistics routes. While EPRA confirmed that the cargo on the two targeted tankers was secured, the operational reality of managing damaged vessels introduces immediate scheduling friction for local discharge terminals.

Until maritime safe-passage arrangements can be established and verified across the Gulf corridor, domestic fuel prices will remain hostage to the security conditions of a waterway thousands of nautical miles away.
