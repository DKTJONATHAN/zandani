When Benson Mutua Muia, widely known as Bensoul, lost his voice to tuberculosis, he did not just lose his instrument—he lost his immediate livelihood. Stuck in his mother’s home in Embu, unable to perform or even speak properly, the singer-songwriter had to pivot from being a stage performer to a backroom writer out of pure survival. The songs he wrote during those six months of silent isolation were never meant for his own albums; they were drafted as assets to be sold off to other artists for quick cash while he recovered.

During an interview with media personality Nick Kwach, Bensoul detailed how a life-threatening health crisis in Nairobi’s Kayole estate dismantled his early career momentum and forced a total retreat to his roots.

"Kuna time niligonjeka TB, nilikua tu hii Nairobi, tukiishi Kayole," Bensoul recalled. "Afya yangu ikakua mbaya sana mpaka ikabidi nirudi home Embu."

The transition from the high-energy hustle of Nairobi's music scene to the quiet of Embu was sudden. Unable to afford private care in the capital as his health collapsed, Bensoul took advantage of public treatment programs in his hometown, noting that his medical care was completely free.

However, the medical road to recovery demanded a complete halt to his career. After arriving in Embu, a consultation with his mother and medical guidelines led to a strict prescription: six months of absolute isolation from the entertainment industry.

"So nikapigia mama yangu simu, so vile nilikua Embu, nikaambiwa I need to stay away from everything for 6 months. Like kuimba, ma gigs," Bensoul explained. "Walisema first 3 months nisitoke."

The hardest hit was to his primary tool. Tuberculosis severely affected his vocal cords, leaving him physically incapable of hitting notes or holding a tune. With no stage performances, no club gigs, and no immediate studio sessions possible, the financial reality of a young musician in recovery set in.

Rather than letting the silence stall him, Bensoul turned to his pen. Unable to vocalize his thoughts, he poured his energy into songwriting. The objective was transactional: write as many songs as possible, package them, and sell them to other active Kenyan artists to generate some income while he remained bedridden.

"I had lost my voice, sikua naweza hata kuimba. So nilikua naandika mawimbo mob with the hope nawea uza," he said.

This period of forced, silent focus became an accidental incubator. Without the distractions of the Nairobi social scene or the demands of a hectic performance schedule, Bensoul built a massive catalog of melodies and lyrics.

By the time his lungs cleared and his vocal cords healed, the landscape of his career had shifted. The songs he had written out of financial desperation felt too personal, too valuable to simply sign away to the highest bidder. Having survived the physical toll of the illness, Bensoul chose to hold onto his intellectual property.

"Vile nilipona, I decided to give out most of the songs. I decided to do most of them," he said, indicating his shift from a desperate songwriter-for-hire to the primary custodian of his own recovered voice.

The six-month exile in Embu, which threatened to end his career before it fully took off, ultimately re-established Bensoul as a self-sufficient artist with a deep reservoir of unreleased material—a catalog born entirely out of a silent room.
