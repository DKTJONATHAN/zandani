A legislative hold-up over Kenya’s proposed free university education framework has stranded thousands of incoming first-year students in financial limbo, triggering urgent calls for emergency cash disbursements to cover immediate campus living expenses.

President William Ruto’s administration had previously pledged a total overhaul of higher education financing under the Tertiary Education, Placement and Funding Bill, 2026. The proposed law promises full state funding for tuition and living stipends, deferring repayment until graduates secure employment.

However, with the draft legislation still undergoing parliamentary scrutiny during the ongoing September 2026 admission cycle, the Ministry of Education has been forced to fall back on the previous financing structure—without immediately releasing the underlying cash allocations to institutions and loan boards.

The resulting funding gap has left newly registered freshmen without funds for off-campus housing, food, or basic course materials during their initial weeks on campus.

### Pressure for Interim Grants

![University students accessing HELB services.](https://i.ibb.co/27QPLd1g/3fedbb2104b5.webp)

Speaking to journalists on Sunday, September 20, the Elimu Bora Working Group pressed the Ministry of Education, the Higher Education Loans Board (HELB), and the Universities Fund to release grant allocations without further administrative delays.

The advocacy coalition urged the national government to set up an emergency stipend framework so that students who have already completed university registration can survive on campus while lawmakers finalize the main bill.

"First things first, let HELB and University Fund be given their grants so that they immediately disburse resources to those who have registered," the group stated. "An immediate funding be made available, probably an interim amount for their upkeep, to help them acquire academic materials, and also to help them get accommodation."

### The Ministry's Stopgap Strategy

The financial uncertainty stems from an interim directive issued by Education Cabinet Secretary Julius Ogamba earlier this month. 

Addressing education stakeholders during a visit to the proposed Coast Region Educational Assessment and Resource Centre in Mombasa on September 4, Ogamba clarified that the government could not execute the promised free-funding structure until Parliament formally enacts the Tertiary Education, Placement and Funding Bill, 2026.

![cs-ogamba.jpg](https://i.ibb.co/M5tXwbxB/b7b38cc57ba2.webp)

In the interim, the Ministry instructed public universities and tertiary colleges to admit the September intake under the existing variable funding model.

"During this time, the first years will be admitted using the existing model," Ogamba said. "Once the new model becomes operational, as at the time the Bill becomes law, we will transition all our tertiary students, from first years to sixth years at universities, KMTC, TVETs and Teacher Training Colleges, into that model."

### The Friction Between Promise and Delivery

While the government's long-term plan envisions absorbing all post-secondary learners—spanning university undergraduates, TVET trainees, Kenya Medical Training College (KMTC) students, and Teacher Training College candidates—into a unified state-funded system, the immediate transition has created friction on the ground.

Families who expected full state backing based on executive pronouncements now find themselves forced to manage unexpected upfront costs for tuition deposits and private rentals. For lower-income households, the gap between the public pledge and the parliamentary timetable has turned the first month of the academic year into a scramble for basic survival funds.

Parliamentary debate schedules for the education bill and the subsequent cash release schedules from the National Treasury will determine how fast HELB can process upkeep loans before mid-term assessments begin.
