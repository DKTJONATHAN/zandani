Maurice Ogeta was never just standing watch outside doors; he was reading rooms when everyone else was looking at monitors. In the final, fragile moments of Raila Odinga’s life inside an Indian mission hospital, the veteran bodyguard took on an unexpected role: gatekeeper between a cautious medical team and a family on the brink of devastating loss.

Speaking publicly during a television interview on Sunday, October 11, 2026, Ogeta pulled back the curtain on the quiet panic that gripped the hospital room, revealing how he stepped in to block clinical protocol the moment attending physician Dr. Olunya began arranging grief counselors for Raila's daughters, Winnie and Ruth Odinga.

The medical team had already deployed an electric defibrillator in a desperate bid to stabilize the former Prime Minister. But it was what came immediately after that prompted Ogeta to draw a hard line with the lead physician.

### The Altar by the Bedside

For Ogeta, the alarming shift had not come from the sound of medical monitors, but from silent housekeeping inside the treatment room. While staff moved swiftly around the bed, he spotted attendants setting up an unexpected arrangement nearby.

“By the time they brought the electrical shock, I was like, no,” Ogeta recounted during the broadcast. “Within a very short time, I saw some place there being prepared. They brought a stand where they put a Bible on top… then I saw a Bible and candles and a certain cloth.”

The presence of liturgical dressings inside an active critical-care ward set off immediate alarms for the long-time aide, who directly confronted the medical team on duty.

“I was like, ‘No, Doc, are you sure we are okay? What are all these for?’” Ogeta said.

Dr. Olunya attempted to calm his rising anxiety, explaining that because the institution operated as a mission hospital, clergy routinely conducted prayers during intense medical procedures. Yet the reassurance fell flat moments later when a priest arrived, performed hurried prayers over the bed, folded his Bible, and abruptly walked out. To Ogeta, the sudden exit confirmed the worst: the staff was no longer simply treating a patient; they were preparing for an inevitable outcome.

### Blocking the Counselors

The turning point came when Dr. Olunya began taking steps to break the news to the family. Winnie and Ruth Odinga were steps away, praying inside the hospital chapel, unaware of the sudden turn in their father’s room.

Rather than walking straight to the chapel with the medical reality, Dr. Olunya began making inquiries about room availability for specialized grief counseling before delivering the prognosis. Ogeta intervened on the spot, shutting down the procedural buffer and insisting on unvarnished honesty.

“I told him, ‘If it has happened, there is no need for counselling,’” Ogeta said. “‘I know these people better; they will not accept. Just tell them what has happened, then we see what we will do.’”

Having spent years managing high-stakes situations and navigating the deeply personal dynamics of the Odinga household, Ogeta understood that clinical diplomacy would backfire. He pushed the medical team to abandon procedural formalities, strip away the psychological buffers, and look the daughters in the eye.

### An Unvarnished Inside Glimpse

Ogeta's televised account offers an intensely human, unvarnished look into an event that brought Kenya to a standstill in late 2025. While public memory focused on the state ceremonies, political eulogies, and mourning that followed the opposition leader's passing, the private scene thousands of miles away in India was defined by quick instincts and tense whispers.

For years, Ogeta’s public identity was synonymous with physical security—flanking Raila through teargas, chaotic campaign podiums, and tense political standoffs across Kenya. His account makes clear that in the quiet corners of an overseas hospital room, his ultimate assignment was safeguarding the dignity of the room and ensuring the family received the truth without hesitation.
