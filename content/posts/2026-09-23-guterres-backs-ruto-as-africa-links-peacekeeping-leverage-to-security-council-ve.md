The structural contradiction of African nations supplying the bulk of United Nations peacekeeping contingents while remaining entirely shut out of top-tier decision-making has drawn a sharp rebuke from the global apex leadership.

United Nations Secretary-General António Guterres threw his institutional weight behind President William Ruto’s campaign for permanent African representation, declaring the current makeup of the UN Security Council both unjust and obsolete. Speaking in New York, Guterres noted that maintaining a 21st-century security architecture without permanent seats for the continent fails to meet the basic demands of legitimacy and effectiveness.

![President Ruto addressing the High-Level Reflection on the Pact for the Future, convened by Namibia, Zambia, the African Union, and the Economic Commission for Africa in New York o](https://i.ibb.co/YFY5bzQ8/0e22745067c2.webp)

The intervention directly reinforces Nairobi’s multi-year diplomatic push. President Ruto has consistently argued that the collective will of 54 African nations cannot be subordinated to an exclusionary structure where continental peace, security, and development policies are decided in rooms where local perspectives are absent.

At the core of the Kenyan position is a demand for at least two permanent seats equipped with full rights—including the veto—alongside two additional non-permanent positions. State House officials maintain that reforming the 15-member body is no longer a diplomatic courtesy to the continent, but an institutional survival imperative for the United Nations itself, particularly given that Africa dominates the Council’s operational agenda.

![United Nations Secretary-General Antonio Guterres](https://i.ibb.co/b5XrFkvK/0c6d1416fbf7.webp)

The Security Council currently comprises five permanent members with veto powers—China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States—alongside ten rotating members elected by the General Assembly for two-year terms. While African states regularly cycle through non-permanent slots, they remain structurally locked out of permanent status and veto privileges.

Guterres’ backing shifts the debate from a regional grievance to a broader multilateral consensus, placing direct pressure on existing permanent members to address the governance imbalance before international bodies lose further credibility across the Global South.
