Kenyan businesses and households that invested in solar systems to escape high grid costs are facing a double blow as regulatory authorities erect new financial barriers to protect the national power grid.

In a newly gazetted amendment to the 2023 tariff schedule, the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA) has cut the compensation rate for independent renewable energy generators under the net-metering program. Consumers who export their excess solar power to the Kenya Power and Lighting Company (KPLC) network will now receive credit for only half of the electricity they supply. 

, the regulator has introduced a penalty for "dumping"—defined as feeding electricity into the national grid without formal approval. Anyone caught doing so will actually be billed for that injected power at the prevailing base tariff, turning what was once a technical bypass into a direct financial liability.

### The Battle Over Grid Stability

![An image showing electricity connectivity for the Last Mile Project.](https://i.ibb.co/DHKwRDx7/2da560c8136c.webp)

These adjustments come as Kenya Power moves defensively against the rapid expansion of solar and wind generation. The utility is currently slowing down the connection of new utility-scale wind and solar projects, citing severe grid stability concerns and rising consumer costs. 

Variable renewable energy now represents over 21 percent of Kenya’s total grid capacity, with wind and solar supplying up to 34 percent of peak daytime demand. Because these sources fluctuate based on weather conditions, Kenya Power has had to rely on expensive thermal backup generation to manage grid frequency. To mitigate this, the utility is now requiring all new wind and solar developers to bundle their projects with battery storage systems capable of providing three to four hours of backup power, while prioritizing stable baseload sources like geothermal and hydroelectricity.

### Thermal Costs Passed to Consumers

The ongoing reliance on fossil-fuel backups is reflected in the immediate power bill hikes facing consumers in September. EPRA has adjusted pass-through costs upward by Ksh 4.16 per kilowatt-hour (kWh), driven largely by the high cost of keeping thermal plants like Kipevu III and Rabai running.

The surcharges, which apply to all meter readings taken in September 2026, are broken down into three components:

*   **Fuel Energy Cost Charge:** Increased by Ksh 3.00 per unit to cover the diesel and heavy fuel oil consumed by thermal generators.
*   **Foreign Exchange Fluctuation Adjustment:** Set at Ksh 1.14 per unit to buffer power utilities against shilling fluctuations on Ksh 1.32 billion in foreign-denominated exchange costs.
*   **Water Resource Management Authority (WRMA) Levy:** A minor charge of 1.48 cents per unit, levied because major hydro plants like Gitaru, Kiambere, and Turkwel utilize public water resources to generate power.

Under this formula, a typical household consuming 100 units of electricity a month will see an extra Ksh 416 added to their bill for these three pass-through levies alone.

### Restructuring the Consumer Bands

To better segment the market amid shifting consumption patterns, EPRA has also restructured domestic customers into three distinct billing bands. Classification will now be calculated based on a rolling three-month average usage: the lowest tier covers up to 30 units, the middle tier spans 30 to 100 units, and the high-volume tier covers 100 to 15,000 units.

Separately, the regulator has formalized special rates for the emerging electric vehicle sector. Electric vehicle charging stations will now be billed at a flat rate of Ksh 16 per unit during peak hours, dropping to a highly discounted rate of Ksh 8 per unit during off-peak hours to encourage night-time charging when overall grid demand is low.

These structural shifts highlight a widening policy friction in Kenya’s energy landscape. While the state continues to promote electric transition and green generation on international stages, the domestic regulatory regime is actively adjusting to protect the financial viability of Kenya Power and the technical integrity of a grid that remains heavily shackled to costly thermal backups.
