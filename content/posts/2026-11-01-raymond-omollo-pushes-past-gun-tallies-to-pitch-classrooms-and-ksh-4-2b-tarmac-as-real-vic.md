Internal Security Principal Secretary Raymond Omollo is trying to break a long-running habit in Kenya's security reporting: counting the rifles and ignoring the chalkboard.

While state briefings on the volatile North Rift typically lean on dramatic tallies of seized firepower and rescued herds, Omollo used his latest review of multi-agency operations to flip the public script. Recovering 2,600 illegal firearms and returning 17,000 stolen livestock makes for eye-catching data, but the administrative chief insisted that gun hauls alone will never prove that peace has arrived.

Instead, Omollo placed his scorecard squarely on whether children can sit in renovated classrooms without ducking for cover and whether pastoralists can trade at local markets without military escort.

### The Shift from Seizures to Classrooms

For decades, government operations in northern Kenya have cycled through familiar motions: a wave of raids, a surge of boots on the ground, and a press table covered in surrendered Kalashnikovs. Omollo, who has overseen both Operation Maliza Uhalifu since its February 2023 rollout and the subsequent disarmament push under Operation Rudisha Bunduki, made a calculated point to steer attention toward social recovery.

He pointed specifically to Ngarachi Comprehensive School in Laikipia. There, Kenya Defence Forces engineers swapped combat gear for masonry tools to repair and rebuild damaged classrooms. For the administration, getting pupils back behind desks serves as more durable evidence of pacification than a line of confiscated assault rifles parked at a police headquarters.

“The true measure of success will be found not merely in the number of firearms recovered or roads constructed, but in the lives people are finally able to lead,” Omollo stated, arguing that guns disappear only when functional clinics, water supply lines, and schools fill the void left by decades of state absence.

### Ksh 4.2 Billion in Asphalt and Access

Beyond classrooms, Omollo put hard currency against the security footprint, confirming that the government has poured more than Ksh 4.2 billion into security-related infrastructure across conflict-prone belts. 

That capital outlay has so far funded the construction and grading of over 520 kilometres of roads. The logistical logic is two-fold: giving rapid-response security convoys direct pathways into previously inaccessible hideouts, while simultaneously integrating isolated grazing outposts into mainstream commercial supply chains.

By turning cut-off valleys into drivable corridors, the state is betting that faster movement of farm produce and livestock to regional trading hubs will dismantle the isolation that has historically shielded bandit networks.

### Hunting the Checkbooks Behind the Raids

Omollo also signalled a tactical pivot in how law enforcement targets the cattle-rustling supply chain. While foot-level bandits have borne the brunt of kinetic operations, the PS highlighted that state intelligence is zeroing in on the commercial middlemen.

Operations are being widened to disrupt the financiers of organized raids and the cartels that facilitate the illicit transport and wholesale butcher of stolen herds. Striking at the financial beneficiaries, rather than just chasing youths carrying arms across valleys, marks a necessary adjustment if disarmament campaigns are to hold.

To lock down grassroots monitoring, Omollo underscored the role of Nyumba Kumi elders and National Government Administration Officers working in tandem with regular police units. He accompanied that call with a reminder on operational discipline, noting that internal security units must investigate citizen abuse claims and respect civilian rights to maintain community trust.

### The Post-Eldoret Momentum

The renewed messaging follows closely on the heels of the 2026 Mashujaa Day celebrations, which were jointly hosted across Uasin Gishu, Nandi, and Elgeyo Marakwet counties, with the main stadium showcase anchored at Kipchoge Keino Stadium in Eldoret on October 20.

With national attention pulled toward the Rift Valley's economic potential, Omollo is determined to ensure that the security ledger is judged by civilian normalcy rather than frontline bulletins. For the communities watching between Baringo, Turkana, and Samburu, the benchmark will remain simple: whether the quiet in the valleys outlasts the official press statements.
