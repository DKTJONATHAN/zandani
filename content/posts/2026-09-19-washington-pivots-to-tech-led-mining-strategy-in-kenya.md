The United States is recalibrating its involvement in Kenya’s mining sector, signaling a move away from the traditional role of a direct resource extractor. Instead, Washington is positioning itself as a provider of advanced extraction and refining technologies, a strategy it hopes will align with Kenya’s push for domestic value addition.

Frank Garcia, the U.S. Assistant Secretary of State for African Affairs, outlined this shift during a discussion at the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) on Friday. According to Garcia, the U.S. has spent over a decade developing specialized mineral processing technologies that it now intends to deploy across the African continent.

“The United States made a decision early on that we weren’t necessarily going to be the miners, but we were going to be the technology leaders in going after the minerals,” Garcia said.

This approach marks a departure from the capital-heavy, equity-based models often seen in African mining, where foreign entities hold primary control over extraction sites. By focusing on technology transfer, the U.S. aims to address a common point of contention: the export of raw ores for processing in third-party nations. For Kenya, which has been vocal about moving up the mineral value chain, the promise of American refining tech-specifically systems capable of extracting multiple commercially viable minerals from a single source-could prove significant.

However, the strategy hinges on the viability of deploying these technologies within Kenya’s existing regulatory and infrastructure landscape. Garcia noted that the U.S. is looking to support African partners in capturing more value locally, a goal that requires not just the machinery, but the supporting industrial base to process those materials. While he cited instances of U.S. firms bidding on technology-heavy tenders elsewhere in Africa, the specific mechanisms for how these technologies will reach Kenyan operators remain under negotiation.

President William Ruto’s recent discussions with U.S. officials underscore the urgency of this partnership. As both nations move toward a formal critical minerals agreement, the debate is shifting from whether the U.S. will participate in the sector to what form that participation will take.

For the Kenyan government, the challenge remains balancing the influx of foreign technology with the need for domestic ownership and sustainable labor practices. While the U.S. offers a pathway to avoid the 'extract-and-export' model, the success of this plan will ultimately depend on whether this technology can be integrated into local operations without creating a new layer of dependency on foreign proprietary hardware and technical support.
