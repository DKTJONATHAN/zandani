Dr Raymond Omollo walked straight onto the shoreline of Marenga Beach in Bunyala, trading Nairobi’s guarded corridors for the damp landing sites of Lake Victoria to make a personal pitch on grassroots governance. The Internal Security and National Administration Principal Secretary arrived to showcase state-backed modernization, only to find himself receiving an unfiltered brief on the cross-border hazards that stalk everyday fishermen on the open water.

The town-hall session brought together Bunyala’s Beach Management Units (BMUs), fish traders, and local transport operators at Busia County’s busiest fish landing point. Omollo framed his visit around a clear administrative doctrine, insisting that top officials under President William Ruto must step out of air-conditioned offices and defend their operational records directly to the people.

Yet while the lakeside audience readily acknowledged visible changes on the shore, the gathering quickly turned into a reality check on the territorial perils beyond the surf.

### Shoreline Upgrades and the Local Ripple

For the fishermen who tie their vessels at Marenga, the presence of the Kenya Coast Guard Service has injected tangible stability along coastal shallows. Fisherfolk reported a marked drop in domestic gear theft since patrols increased, giving crews the confidence to launch night expeditions without the constant dread of engine theft.

One veteran boat owner, who noted he had worked the lake through three successive national regimes, pointed out that the current government has backed its words with physical infrastructure. Speedboats handed to BMU teams, dedicated fish-handling cold points, access roads leading into the beaches, and direct power connections have significantly upgraded handling standards at the water's edge.

That logistical lifeline extends far beyond the men casting nets. At Marenga, catches feed a bustling micro-economy where boda boda riders secure daily fares ferrying fresh tilapia to rural trading centres, while roadside vendors take up the retail slack.

“Sisi wote tunafaidika,” a resident told the delegation, pointing out how the ecosystem keeps families afloat across the sub-county.

### The Shadow of Cross-Border Patrols

Yet beneath the applause for onshore assets lay unresolved tensions that modern speedboats have not cured. BMU representatives laid bare the volatile dynamic playing out near the international boundary line, pointing to routine confrontations with Ugandan security personnel patrolling the lake.

Fishermen recounted harrowing encounters on the water, detailing instances where foreign officers intercepted Kenyan vessels, impounding boats and seizing expensive commercial nets. In several cases, crew members watched their fishing tackle set ablaze or confiscated entirely, leaving families carrying crippling financial debt with zero legal recourse.

Compounding the friction is an ecological crisis inside Kenya's own waters. Local leaders warned Omollo that rogue operators harvesting undersized fingerlings are systematically exhausting fish nurseries, threatening to collapse breeding grounds even as infrastructure expands.

### Answering to the Shoreline

Rather than brushing aside the friction points, Omollo used the forum to affirm that public service must stomach uncomfortable feedback. He credited the presidency with demanding direct community engagement, arguing that relying purely on sanitized departmental memos leaves national security planners blind to frontline vulnerabilities.

Omollo pledged that the state would continue expanding water-patrol cover while addressing the security vacuum that leaves Bunyala's crews vulnerable to harassment. With gear loss mounting and border friction unresolved, Marenga’s fishing community made it clear that while speedboats and electricity on the sand are welcome, their real survival hinges on lasting protection out on the open lake.
