In Busia County, small-scale producers Miriam Ojakol and Rebecca Odalo are turning agricultural waste into operational leverage through a closed-loop system that links poultry coops, fish ponds, and vegetable patches on tight acreage.

The setup bypasses conventional single-crop overheads by operating on a biological exchange model. Poultry manure goes straight into enriching fish ponds, while nutrient-heavy water siphoned from those same ponds is recycled to irrigate adjacent vegetable gardens. For Ojakol and Odalo, the method trims down the physical footprint required to run multiple enterprises while cutting out the heavy fertilizer and feed bills that usually squeeze smallholders.

Behind these backyard plots sits institutional backing from the Lake Basin Development Authority (LBDA) and the Wezesha Agribusiness Programme. Over the past two years, the initiative has pushed climate-smart training to at least 5,000 young people, attempting to bridge the gap between traditional subsistence farming and structured agribusiness.

LBDA Managing Director Wycliffe Ochiaga noted that the authority is leaning into modern poultry setups alongside local animal feed development to drive down the primary cost driver for small producers. Complementing the Busia field operations, a poultry technology transfer centre in Kisumu has also been established, churning out 300 trays of eggs daily for the surrounding market.

While state programs frame the push around national food security metrics, the everyday reality on the ground in counties like Busia rests on how tightly farmers can manage their resource loops before the next input cycle begins.
