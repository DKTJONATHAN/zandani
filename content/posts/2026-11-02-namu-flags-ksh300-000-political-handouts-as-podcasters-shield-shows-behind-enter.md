Political operatives preparing for the 2027 General Election have begun channeling cash directly into Nairobi’s digital podcast studios, prompting investigative journalist John-Allan Namu to warn that undisclosed payments threaten to institutionalize brown-envelope practices across Kenya’s fastest-growing media sector.

The alarm centers on payments reportedly ranging between Ksh200,000 and Ksh300,000 handed to online content creators under the guise of event sponsorship and production support. Speaking at the Open Africa Tribune Media forum and elaborating in follow-up remarks on Sunday, November 1, Namu cautioned that accepting unvetted political financing strips digital creators of their claimed editorial independence.

“A certain podcaster receiving mountains of cash from a politician, supposedly for an event, right. And was raising money from pillar to post, from all sorts of unsavoury characters, you know,” Namu said. “Easy money, which is not clean. And allowing that to pass as normal behavior and journalism is part of the problem.”

Namu argued that disguising politically backed content as spontaneous, candid digital interviews deceives audiences who are increasingly abandoning conventional television and radio broadcasts for long-form online shows.

### The 'Not a Journalist' Shield

The scrutiny has triggered immediate pushback from studio operators who argue that traditional editorial ethics do not govern commercial digital production.

Defending his business model following the remarks, one prominent host maintained that digital channels operate as corporate production houses focused on comedy, scripted productions, and lifestyle entertainment rather than news gathering.

"I'm not a journalist. I do have my company. It's a media company that focuses on just productions," the creator said, adding that interviewing political figures does not convert an entertainer into a reporter, even if the studio retains trained journalists for separate, dedicated assignments.

This defense highlights a deepening accountability divide in Kenya’s public square. While mainstream newsrooms operate under formal statutory frameworks, code of conduct requirements, and commercial advertising declarations, digital creators function in a commercial grey zone where paid appearances can be presented as organic conversations.

### The 2027 Distribution Prize

![A podcast set up.](https://i.ibb.co/RGsSVLsx/f36bfcc670f9.webp)

The sudden influx of political capital reflects the immense distribution power Kenya's independent creators now control.

Data compiled by a Nairobi-based media research and analytics firm underscores why political strategists are bypassing legacy newsrooms. Former Tahidi High actor Abel Mutua leads Kenya’s podcast rankings with an average of 306,500 views per video. Digital creator Lynn Ngugi ranks second, pulling an average of 248,100 views per release.

High-output entertainment channels wield substantial cumulative influence as well. Obinna TV averages 65,600 views across an archive of 521 videos, while its companion platform, Obinna TV Extra, logs 60,200 views across 820 uploads.

For politicians planning nationwide campaigns for 2027, those audience aggregates offer direct, uncritical access to youth demographics without the hostile cross-examination typical of veteran political reporters.

With three years remaining before the next general election, the practice leaves audiences navigating a media environment where six-figure transactions can quietly purchase political airtime, insulated by creator claims that entertainment platforms owe the public no editorial disclosure.
