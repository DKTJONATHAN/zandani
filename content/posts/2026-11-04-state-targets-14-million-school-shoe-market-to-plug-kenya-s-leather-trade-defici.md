Government is attempting to correct a persistent structural imbalance in Kenya's livestock economy by turning the country’s 14 million school-going children into a guaranteed domestic market for locally processed leather.

Speaking at the Innovation Summit inside the Kenyatta International Convention Center (KICC) on Wednesday, November 4, President William Ruto instructed footwear manufacturers to scale up production to supply every learner nationwide. The directive extends an executive strategy first tested within Kenya’s disciplined forces, moving from discretionary public purchasing to mandated local procurement.

"We will now move a step further," President Ruto told delegates at the summit. "We want you to manufacture all the shoes for our school-going children, and we have 14 million of them."

### Scaling from Boots to Classrooms

![An image of a school child, displaying his black leather shoes.](https://i.ibb.co/nq8cNzNJ/4a7ff00db154.webp)

The policy shift mirrors an earlier executive intervention in the security sector. In June, the administration mandated that all police and military footwear be sourced strictly from domestic manufacturers. By October, during a town hall meeting at Jamhuri Park, executive officials confirmed that security agencies had transitioned entirely to locally produced boots, enforcing a total ban on imported combat footwear.

The government is now attempting to replicate that import-substitution model on a far broader scale. While security forces represent tens of thousands of uniformed personnel, the school market anchors steady, repeatable annual demand across 14 million primary and secondary learners.

This demand-side guarantee addresses a long-standing economic contradiction in Kenya's agricultural and manufacturing sectors. Data from the World Bank and the Kenya Leather Development Council places Kenya's livestock herd at approximately 80 million animals—ranking 11th globally and third in Africa.

Yet despite holding one of the continent's largest raw-material reserves, Kenya remains a net importer of finished leather goods. Official trade metrics show Kenya ranks 87th out of 208 nations in leather apparel exports, generating just Ksh68 million ($527,000) annually. Concurrently, the country ranks 101st among 223 importing nations, spending Ksh285 million ($2.2 million) each year on foreign leather goods—spending over four times more on finished leather imports than it earns from exports.

### Industrial Infrastructure and County Logistics

To bridge the gap between raw hide availability and finished footwear assembly, state capital is being deployed to centralize modern processing before devolving manufacturing outward.

On September 2, Deputy President Kithure Kindiki unveiled the Ksh5.8 billion Kenya Leather Industrial Park in Kenani, Machakos County. Designed as a specialized industrial zone, the Kenani hub provides mechanized tanneries, treatment plants, and standardized quality-control facilities intended to raise raw Kenyan hides to commercial footwear standards.

Rather than concentrating finished boot and shoe assembly solely within Nairobi industrial hubs, the administration's stated plan involves distributing lower-tier assembly across county-level facilities. Under this model, primary processing occurs at sites like Kenani, while localized workshops and regional tanneries supply nearby schools.

The policy’s success now depends on whether domestic tanneries and shoe manufacturers can rapidly expand operational capacity without triggering localized supply shortages or price distortions for parents. As local manufacturers begin preparing production lines, the transition from state military orders to nationwide school procurement represents the largest formal test of Kenya's leather industrial policy to date.
