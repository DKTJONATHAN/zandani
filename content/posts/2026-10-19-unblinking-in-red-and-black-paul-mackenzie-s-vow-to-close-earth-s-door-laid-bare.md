Clad in a casual red-and-black striped T-shirt, Paul Mackenzie sat in the Mombasa dock on Monday morning with the unblinking, flat composure that has come to define his public appearances. Around him, a dense wall of security personnel stood watch, cameras from local and foreign press corps clicked in rapid bursts, and Director of Public Prosecutions Renson Ingonga observed from the bar. 

Yet across the bench, as High Court Judge Diana Kavedza-Mochache began leafing through an extraordinary 1,000-page judgment, the preacher was confronted with the verbatim mechanics of the apocalyptic theater he had orchestrated deep inside Malindi’s scrubland.

At the center of Justice Mochache’s reading was the profound psychological hold Mackenzie exerted over his flock within the 4,860-acre Shakahola forest tract. According to witness testimonies detailed by the court, Mackenzie had framed their agonizing withdrawal from society not merely as a spiritual escape from a "condemned world," but as an orderly procession to heaven where he appointed himself the supreme gatekeeper. 

He had assured his congregation that every single follower would cross over before him—even if entering paradise meant enduring violent physical suffering, described plainly in testimonies as *"mangumi na mateke"* (kicks and blows). Mackenzie, the court heard, pledged that he would be the very last living human to depart, promising he would personally "close the door on earth" once the final follower had ascended.

Inside the makeshift tented courtroom erected at the Mombasa Law Courts to accommodate the massive trial, the details laid bare the tragic innocence of those who believed him. Some children were dressed in pristine, brand-new clothes in joyful expectation of meeting their maker in the skies. Other young victims, physically broken by deliberate starvation, had reached the point of pointing out to their relatives the precise patches of dirt where their earthly remains ought to be buried. Mothers looked on helplessly as their infants starved, and siblings watched one another wither into nothingness.

The scale of that promised ascension forms the crux of the 191 counts of murder facing Mackenzie and his 30 co-accused. Of the 429 bodies recovered from mass graves dug throughout the forest compound, 191 victims are central to this specific murder trial—and 190 of them were children aged 16 and below.

The reading of the monumental ruling proved an endurance test of its own. Beginning promptly at 9:40 a.m., Justice Mochache systematically traversed witness accounts, forensic records, and doctrinal assertions without interruption for more than four hours. As the afternoon heat settled heavily inside the canvas structure, the physical strain on the packed gallery prompted the judge to call a brief recess at 2:00 p.m., granting Mackenzie, his legal team, and the prosecution a 30-minute respite before proceedings resumed at 2:30 p.m.

Throughout the marathon delivery, Mackenzie offered no visible reaction to the devastating portrait of his ministry. The man who had once commanded absolute authority over hundreds of believers who starved themselves in wilderness clearings listened in unbroken silence, anchored to the dock by the overwhelming paper trail of his own prophesied apocalypse.
