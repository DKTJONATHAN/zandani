Even as President William Ruto took the global stage in New York to lobby multilateral lenders for cheaper capital, he turned the spotlight inward, warning that African governments must aggressively tighten their own tax collection machinery to break the cycle of external borrowing.

Speaking on Tuesday, September 22, 2026, during a High-Level Reflection on the Pact for the Future, the Kenyan leader argued that high debt-servicing expenses are actively starving national budgets of the funds needed for jobs, healthcare, and infrastructure. Convened by Namibia, Zambia, the African Union, and the United Nations Economic Commission for Africa, the session served as a platform for African heads of state to push for structural changes in international financial systems.

Yet, alongside his demands for expanded credit guarantees and risk-sharing instruments designed to lower artificial sovereign risk premiums, the President laid out a strict domestic mandate for African treasuries.

“We need reforms that lower Africa’s cost of capital, expand guarantees and risk-sharing instruments to unlock investment, and enable countries to mobilise more of their own resources,” President Ruto told world leaders, adding a sharper local caveat: “Money Africa can rightfully tax is money Africa need not borrow.”

The dual approach highlights the administration's tightrope walk between seeking external relief from high-cost commercial debt and enforcing internal fiscal discipline. While African nations helped shape the broader Pact for the Future adopted in 2024, the President noted that global financial institutions must now speed up the conversion of those diplomatic commitments into measurable economic outcomes.

The high-level gathering concluded with the ongoing push for multilateral lenders to bridge the continent’s development financing gap, leaving local economic planners to weigh how domestic revenue mobilization will scale alongside shifting international credit terms.
