Inspector General Douglas Kanja found himself walking into a high-stakes courtroom showdown on September 28, 2026, where his personal freedom hinged on convincing a judge that the National Police Service would finally take court orders seriously.

The police chief did not send a legal representative. Instead, Kanja walked into the dock alongside senior police commanders to face mounting contempt proceedings after officers repeatedly defied judicial bans on barricading public roads during public demonstrations.

Faced with the immediate prospect of custodial sentencing, Kanja presented an operational plan detailing how field officers will adhere to court directives during assemblies. He gave explicit assurances that unauthorized road blockades—which the court noted directly infringe upon citizens’ constitutional freedom of movement—would cease.

“Noting that you have attended court in person, appreciating that you have undertaken and given assurance that this will not happen again, I shall pardon you,” the presiding judge ruled.

While the pardon spared the police leadership from an immediate jail term, the court drew a sharp, unyielding line. The judge emphasized that contempt proceedings were vital for asserting judicial authority and protecting the rule of law against arbitrary security tactics.

“I appreciate that you have emphasised to your officers that court orders must be complied with,” the court added, delivering a stern warning that any future violations will trigger personal accountability and direct imprisonment for the service's leadership.

The detailed written judgment outlining the specific statutory duties of the National Police Service in maintaining public order and protecting constitutional rights is set to be released by the court.
