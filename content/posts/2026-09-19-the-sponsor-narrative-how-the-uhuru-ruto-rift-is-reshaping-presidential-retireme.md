Former President Uhuru Kenyatta has signaled a shift in his public engagement, citing a climate he describes as "too dangerous" for off-the-cuff commentary. Speaking at a funeral in Kiharu, Murang’a County, on Friday, the former president adopted a cautious tone, explicitly linking his decision to remain scripted to the risk of having his words misconstrued by a political environment currently dominated by the "sponsor" narrative.

This label, deployed repeatedly by President William Ruto and his allies, frames Kenyatta as the primary financier behind opposition maneuvers, including those of Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna. For Kenyatta, the term has become a shorthand for the ongoing friction between the current administration and his own political legacy. By adopting the label himself-closing his remarks with, "That is all from the sponsor"-Kenyatta appeared to be attempting to neutralize the political sting of the accusation through irony.

However, the rhetoric from the current administration suggests that the rift is far from symbolic. President Ruto’s recent tour of Homa Bay County underscored a deeper, more structural grievance: the collapse of a purported political agreement. Ruto publicly revisited his tenure as Kenyatta’s deputy, asserting that he had invested political capital in four of Kenyatta’s election campaigns with the expectation of a reciprocal arrangement.

According to the President, the two had reached an understanding of "10 for you, 10 for me," referring to a two-term power-sharing model that ultimately failed to materialize during the 2022 transition. While Ruto acknowledged that Kenyatta held the democratic right to pivot away from that agreement, the public airing of these private pacts marks a departure from the traditional decorum observed during previous presidential successions in Kenya.

The escalation highlights a significant evolution in the relationship between the two leaders. What was once a partnership defined by shared campaigns and constitutional cooperation has hardened into a recurring public debate over broken promises and the financial backing of political dissent. For the current administration, the narrative of the "sponsor" serves as a convenient lens through which to view opposition activity, effectively blaming the former president for the challenges faced by the current government.

As Kenyatta limits his public appearances to scripted statements, the question remains whether this public confrontation will continue to dominate the political discourse or if the two leaders will find a way to navigate their post-power relationship without further eroding the norms of presidential transition.
