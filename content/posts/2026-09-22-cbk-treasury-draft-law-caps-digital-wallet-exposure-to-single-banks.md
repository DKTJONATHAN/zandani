The National Treasury and the Central Bank of Kenya have jointly proposed legislation that will legally ring-fence customer deposits held by fintechs and force digital wallet operators to split their cash reserves across multiple commercial banks to prevent systemic collapse.

Under the draft National Payment System Bill, 2026, electronic money issuers and mobile payment providers will no longer be permitted to warehouse their entire float within a single preferred financial institution. The bill stipulates that no commercial bank may hold more than Ksh500 million or 25 percent of a payment provider's total trust funds, whichever figure is higher.

The structural shift primarily insulates millions of mobile money users and retail depositors from institutional contagion. By isolating client balances in dedicated, ring-fenced trust accounts completely divorced from an operator's operational capital, the framework ensures customer balances remain untouchable should a fintech firm slide into insolvency or operational distress.

At the same time, spreading custodial cash across the banking sector addresses the exposure created when large non-bank platforms concentrate billions of shillings in liquidity within single anchor lenders.

### Mandatory Authorisation and Capital Standards

The draft statute seeks to repeal the 15-year-old National Payment System Act of 2011, replacing an outdated supervisory regime with an expanded licensing framework. All non-bank corporate entities operating payment systems or clearing digital transactions will be required to seek formal licensing from the banking regulator.

Traditional financial institutions—including commercial banks, microfinance lenders, and building societies—will not require separate payment provider licenses. However, they will be required to obtain formal authorization from the CBK and demonstrate strict compliance with capital adequacy standards equivalent to those imposed on non-bank competitors.

To enforce pricing transparency, the bill mandates that operators explicitly disclose all transactional fees, service tariffs, operational risks, platform limitations, and contractual terms to users prior to service delivery.

### Direct Takeover Powers and Heavy Fines

Beyond technical licensing, the proposed law significantly expands the Central Bank’s enforcement arsenal, granting the regulator explicit powers to assume direct operational control or place struggling payment providers under statutory management.

![Former CBK Governor Patrick Njoroge holding the new Kenyan notes](https://i.ibb.co/HLvgC9sC/13a219162dd7.webp)

Firms that flout the regulatory provisions face initial financial penalties of up to Ksh20 million. That threshold rises to Ksh30 million for repeat infractions, alongside compounding administrative fines of up to Ksh100,000 for each day a breach persists.

The statute also introduces severe criminal penalties for individuals within payment firms. Executives or staff convicted of unlawfully manipulating or exploiting a payment system for personal financial gain face up to seven years in prison alongside personal financial liabilities.

### County Hearings Across 11 Hubs

Announcing the publication of the bill on Tuesday, September 22, the National Treasury and the Central Bank opened an 18-day public consultation window, inviting financial sector players, technology firms, and the public to submit memoranda by Friday, October 9, 2026.

To capture feedback outside the capital, the banking regulator has scheduled nationwide public participation forums between September 28 and October 9. Physical hearings are slated for Nairobi, Mombasa, Kisumu, Nakuru, Eldoret, Nyeri, Meru, Kisii, Kitui, Kitale, Garissa, and Nandi before the final text is forwarded to Parliament for formal debate.
