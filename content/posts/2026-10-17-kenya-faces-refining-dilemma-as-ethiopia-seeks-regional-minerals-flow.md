According to statements issued by Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed, regional miners across East Africa are being lobbied to route unrefined gold through Addis Ababa’s newly launched National Precious Metals Refinery. The overture positions Ethiopia as the primary beneficiary of regional bullion flows, presenting Kenyan trade officials with a critical policy choice between exporting raw precious metals to neighbouring hubs or accelerating domestic processing.

The regional push follows the October 10 commissioning of Ethiopia’s $50 million (Ksh6.48 billion) facility, established jointly by state enterprise Ethiopian Investment Holdings and Dubai-based Sam Precious Metal. Operating with a targeted annual capacity of 600 tonnes of gold and silver, the plant was pitched by Prime Minister Abiy as a continental buffer against value loss overseas.

"It enables neighbouring countries and regional mining companies to bring their gold to Ethiopia for advanced refining, certification, value addition, and sale, eliminating the need to export it outside the continent," Abiy stated.

![ruto-abiy-ahmed-ethiopia.jpg](https://i.ibb.co/hRRvMLfT/4ee509d18a98.webp)

Industry tracking from the Africa Gold Council identifies Kenya, Uganda, and Sudan as prime feeder markets for the facility, according to council director Keith Nare. Yet Nairobi’s wider mining framework is increasingly oriented around domestic value retention—a tension currently playing out over Kwale County’s Mrima Hills deposits.

Mrima Hills holds significant volumes of niobium and rare earth elements, minerals central to global electronics, defense systems, and clean energy manufacturing. Rather than serving purely as an extraction site, the deposit has become the centerpiece of intense bilateral jockeying over who builds and operates Kenya’s internal refinery infrastructure.

Mining Cabinet Secretary Hassan Joho confirmed in September that Kenya and the United States have reached advanced discussions over a dedicated critical minerals partnership. Under the framework offered by Washington, the US would back the construction of local processing units and refineries, advancing an American objective to diversify critical mineral supply networks away from Chinese dominance.

![A photo collage of President Donald Trump (US) and Xi Jinping (China) with Mrima Hills in Kwale in the background, October 26, 2025.](https://i.ibb.co/DHbdPMDN/f7608ce2b147.webp)

That strategic pivot arrives under sharp scrutiny from Beijing. Chinese President Xi Jinping has publicly cautioned African administrations against permitting foreign powers to convert continental mineral reserves into geopolitical levers, calling instead for African states to retain direct sovereignty over their supply lines.

Simultaneously, commercial interests from Australia have made headway in the formal bidding process for the Kwale site. A consortium featuring RareX and Iluka Resources has progressed in the Mrima Hills tender with a hybrid structure: executing baseline primary processing inside Kenya, while shipping the resulting rare earth concentrate across the Indian Ocean to Iluka’s dedicated refinery in Western Australia for high-tier separation.

The Kenyan government has yet to state publicly whether it will permit local gold miners to direct raw consignments into Ethiopia's processing network, or enforce local processing requirements consistent with its stance on Kwale's rare earths. With multiple foreign proposals now testing Nairobi's value-addition policy, the Ministry of Mining faces mounting pressure to define the terms under which raw minerals cross its borders.
