Digital immortality is a lie sold to us by Silicon Valley, and Yassin Osen is the latest man to find out just how fragile his kingdom truly is.

Osen, the man behind the Mr Handsome Official Live brand, recently watched a million followers vanish into the digital ether. Most people would have packed their bags and found a desk job in Kakamega. Instead, he clawed his way back. He now sits at 250,000 followers on a fresh account. It is a comeback story that sounds heroic until you realize he is essentially rebuilding a house on land he does not own.

The digital space in Kenya is a volatile landlord. One day you are the king of the algorithm, pulling thousands of viewers who treat your sarcasm like gospel. The next day, a bot flags your content, a moderator wakes up on the wrong side of the bed, and your entire livelihood is wiped off the map. Osen survived because he has the Luhya humor that keeps people watching. He leans into the deadpan style that resonates with a demographic tired of the polished, fake lives displayed by Nairobi influencers. He is authentic. He is consistent. But he is also entirely at the mercy of a server farm located thousands of miles away from Western Kenya.

Think about the economics of the scroll. You are sitting in a matatu on Waiyaki Way, sweating through your shirt while the engine fumes drift through the window. You open TikTok to kill the boredom. You see Osen. You laugh. You tap the screen and send a virtual gift that costs real, hard-earned shillings from your M-Pesa. In that moment, you are the venture capitalist of his content, but you own none of the equity. You are paying for his hustle, for the data bundles, and for the platform’s bottom line. When the account gets nuked, your contribution disappears into the void of lost data.

The platforms are the only ones getting rich. They collect the data, they hoard the ad revenue, and they dictate the terms of engagement. They let creators like Osen build a fanbase, then they tighten the leash. When they purge an account, they do not care about the hours spent editing or the rapport built with fans. They care about their user agreement. Osen managed to pivot to Smartboy Management and keep the lights on, but his situation exposes the precarious nature of the creator economy. We are all living in a rented digital reality.

There is a strange irony in Osen looking toward Nigerian creators like Peller and Jadrolita for inspiration while finding his footing here at home. He understands that the game is about personality. It is about being a character that people recognize. When he takes to the screen from Kakamega, he is not just broadcasting; he is fighting for relevance in a landscape where the audience has a five-second attention span. If he stops, he dies. If he is banned, he has to start from scratch. There is no tenure in this profession.

We celebrate the Digital Creator Inspiration Award he picked up last month as if it is a permanent seal of approval. It is not. It is just a snapshot in time. The real test is whether he can stay relevant when the next trend inevitably pushes him to the side. The local streamers who have emerged after him are already nipping at his heels. They have the same access to the same tools and the same ambition. In this environment, the only real currency is the ability to adapt faster than the algorithm can change its mind.

The viewer is the one holding the bag. We spend our airtime and our focus on these characters, building them up until they become the face of our daily entertainment. We forget that this is a zero-sum game. Every shilling sent to a digital creator is a shilling that is not being invested in something tangible. We are subsidizing a spectacle that can be terminated by a single line of code. Osen is lucky. He has the grit to start over. Most creators who get nuked simply fade away, their "million-strong" audiences migrating to the next shiny object without a second thought.

If you are following Osen, you are watching a man who knows the fragility of his position. He is not naive. He knows that his empire is built on borrowed ground. He keeps streaming because it is his best shot at making a living in a world that offers very few alternatives for someone with his specific brand of talent. He is trading his labor for our attention, and we are trading our money for his distraction.

Take a look around the next time you are stuck in traffic. Look at how many people are locked into their screens, eyes glazed over as they consume the content of people they will never meet. The economy of influence is a pyramid where the base is composed of thousands of bored commuters and the peak is a platform that could not care less about who wins or loses. Osen is just trying to stay near the top for as long as the connection holds.

It is a grinding, exhausting way to make a living. There is no job security, no pension, and no guarantee that tomorrow will be anything other than a blank screen. Osen is a reminder that in the modern digital age, the most valuable skill is not talent, but the stubborn refusal to quit when the platform decides you are finished. Whether or not that is a sustainable way to live is a question we are all going to have to answer soon enough. For now, he is back, he is loud, and he is waiting for the next notification to see if he still has a job.
