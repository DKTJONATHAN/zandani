The National Transport and Safety Authority (NTSA) has set an ambitious November 26, 2026 target to activate its dormant instant traffic fines system, launching a high-stakes race to clear a web of active court injunctions and legislative vetoes.

While the technological infrastructure for automated ticketing is already built and ready for deployment, the regulator remains legally handcuffed. The agency is now forced to run a dual-track strategy: lobbying the Senate to resurrect three key traffic laws that were thrown out in July, while attempting to resolve the judicial challenges currently blocking the rollout.

### The Dormant Infrastructure

Speaking on Citizen TV on Monday, October 5, NTSA Director of Road Safety Andrew Kiplagat confirmed that the physical and digital frameworks for the instant fines system are fully assembled but cannot be turned on. 

![The National Transport and Safety Authority (NTSA) Director of Road Safety, Andrew Kiplagat, addressing the press in November 2025.](https://i.ibb.co/G4V1cz8P/cc4fac1467f4.webp)

"NTSA is having a programme on instant fines, which again is not happening because of legal impediments because of court cases," Kiplagat stated. He expressed optimism that these legal hurdles would be resolved in time to allow enforcement to begin by November 26, 2026, framing the system as a critical tool to curb speeding, drink-driving, and other behavioral risks on Kenyan roads.

The proposed system relies heavily on technology to bypass traditional roadside physical arrests. Kiplagat detailed a plan to reintroduce speed cameras across clearly designated speed zones. These cameras will automatically log speeding violations, generate digital fines, and transmit them directly to offending motorists. Drivers will be given a specific window to pay, failing which the regulator will trigger secondary enforcement and judicial proceedings.

### The Legislative Bottleneck

Beyond the courtroom, NTSA's broader regulatory agenda faces a significant legislative logjam. The Ministry of Transport is currently locked in intense negotiations with Senate leadership to salvage three major pieces of traffic legislation that senators annulled on July 22, 2026. 

The Senate threw out the Traffic (School Transport) Rules, 2026, the Traffic (Motor Vehicle Inspection) Rules, 2026, and the National Transport and Safety Authority (Operation of Commercial Vehicles) Regulations, 2026. The annulment was driven by findings that NTSA and the Ministry had failed to conduct adequate public participation before gazetting the rules.

![NTSA](https://i.ibb.co/TDQhGd9V/d3851e4b5ae8.webp)

This legislative setback directly stalls a planned overhaul of Kenya’s vehicle inspection regime. The regulations published in February 2026 aimed to expand mandatory vehicle safety inspections to a wider array of private passenger vehicles. Currently, the Traffic Act limits routine inspections primarily to public service vehicles (PSVs) and heavy commercial trucks.

### Expanding the Enforcement Net

Kiplagat revealed that the Ministry of Transport is using this forced pause to conduct the first comprehensive review of the Traffic Act, aiming to patch structural enforcement gaps. Part of this expansion involves bringing heavy commercial vehicles and private hauliers under the same stringent regulatory microscope as PSVs, particularly regarding speed limits and driver behavior.

However, the viability of the November 26 rollout hinges entirely on how quickly the Ministry can satisfy the Senate's demands for public participation and how fast the courts dispatch the active petitions against the instant-fines program. 

With just over seven weeks remaining before the self-imposed deadline, motorists and transport operators are left watching a regulatory clock. Whether the NTSA can successfully transition from its current posture of behavioral sensitization to automated, camera-led enforcement remains a question of judicial and legislative diplomacy.
