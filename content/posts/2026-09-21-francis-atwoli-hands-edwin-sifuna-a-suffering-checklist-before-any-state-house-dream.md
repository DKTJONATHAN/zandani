Francis Atwoli has never been one to deliver political critiques in polite whispers, and on Sunday night, he turned Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna into his latest prime-time classroom subject.

Appearing on Citizen TV’s *Sunday Live* on September 20, 2026, the Central Organization of Trade Unions (COTU) Secretary General did not just dismiss the first-term senator’s whispered ambitions for Kenya’s top seat. He handed him an unsparing, old-guard checklist of everything an African politician must endure before even daring to look toward State House.

Speaking to host Jeff Koinange, the veteran trade unionist made it clear that eloquence and packed stadiums do not grant an express ticket past the country's grueling political hierarchy.

“Sifuna, a one-term Senator, you want to become a president? Not in Africa; we must detain you, we must make you go through stages,” Atwoli said, leaning into the camera. “You must suffer looking for leadership. You must know how the economy is crafted. You must be able to understand the problems Kenyans are having. You must pay school fees for your children. You must raise money for other people. You must build churches; you must do what we have done. For now, no—capital N-O!”

### The Veteran's Hazing Ritual

Atwoli’s broadside went straight after the political profile Sifuna has cultivated as one of Nairobi’s sharpest, most outspoken opposition figures. As the convener of the countrywide Linda Mwananchi rallies, the senator has spent recent months drawing animated crowds and fueling speculation about higher ambitions.

Yet to Atwoli, Sifuna's public momentum is being mistaken for actual political capital. The union boss argued that genuine power in Kenya is built not on applause lines, but through an exacting apprenticeship that involves financial sacrifice, community patronage, and physical resilience.

By rattling off requirements like paying school fees, financing public fundraisers, and constructing churches, Atwoli effectively laid down the traditional bar of Kenyan elite politics—a system where leaders earn standing by spending personal fortunes and weathering political storms over decades.

### The Cautionary Roll Call

To drive the lesson home, Atwoli warned against mistaking large rally turnouts for an inevitable path to victory, invoking some of the most celebrated figures in Kenya's multi-party history.

He reeled off the names of Moi-era titans who held massive swathes of the country in the palm of their hands yet never crossed the threshold of the presidency: Kenneth Matiba, Paul Ngei, Jaramogi Oginga Odinga, Bildad Kaggia, and Ronald Ngala. Each of them commanded legendary devotion from thousands of supporters, but none secured the ultimate prize.

“You must build broad, nationwide alliances,” Atwoli emphasized, noting that charismatic street mobilization means little without the institutional muscle that anchors a serious campaign. 

Winning State House, he pointed out, requires locking in the quiet backing of county governors, navigating deep-pocketed business communities, and knitting together regional blocs that do not show up simply for theatrical speeches.

### The Ceiling Even Raila Faced

Even veteran opposition chief Raila Odinga was pulled into the lecture. Atwoli noted that despite spending decades cultivating intricate networks across every corner of the republic, Odinga repeatedly found the presidency just out of reach.

The comparison served as a pointed reality check: if a figure with Odinga’s institutional footprint and historical baggage faced immense hurdles, a first-term senator leaning primarily on urban rhetoric and rally excitement faces a far steeper climb.

While Sifuna has not formally thrown his hat into the ring for the 2027 General Election, his repeated public hints at higher executive ambitions have made him an inescapable fixture of ongoing succession chatter. For Atwoli, however, that conversation remains laughably premature—at least until Sifuna logs the scars, the financial burdens, and the decades of political dues the trade unionist insists the office demands.
