A foreign exchange cushion of USD 14.689 billion (about Ksh1.91 trillion) has decoupled the Kenyan Shilling from seasonal swings in diaspora remittances, maintaining the local unit below the critical Ksh130 threshold despite softening family support flows from abroad.

Data published by the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) on Friday, October 16, shows the shilling settling at Ksh129.81 against the U.S. dollar, effectively neutralizing a USD 13.3 million (about Ksh1.6 billion) month-on-month drop in diaspora receipts.

Overseas workers sent home USD 438.5 million (about Ksh56.92 billion) in September, down from USD 451.8 million (about Ksh58.5 billion) in August. The pullback dragged cumulative 12-month inflows down by 1.0 per cent to USD 5,032 million, compared to USD 5,080 million recorded across the corresponding period previously.

### Structural Floor Replaces Peak Swings

The contraction marks the third quarter's ongoing cooling from the high-water mark seen in March 2026, when remittance inflows surged to USD 450.3 million (about Ksh58.46 billion). Market analysts attributed that initial surge to emergency transfers dispatched by expatriate Kenyans responding to early volatility across major foreign economies.

By June, those emergency flows had receded, dropping monthly tallies to roughly USD 375.6 million (about Ksh48.76 billion) and triggering warnings of an annualized decline. However, the subsequent rebound across July, August, and September has established a durable floor above the USD 400 million (about Ksh51.92 billion) mark.

Rather than tracking the downward tilt of the 12-month trendline, foreign exchange desks saw the shilling maintain the narrow corridor it has occupied since mid-2024. The currency briefly slipped past the Ksh130 mark in early April 2026 before recovering and trading flat through the midpoint of October.

### Import Cover Dampens External Shocks

The ability of the foreign exchange market to absorb a 1.0 per cent cumulative slide in remittance flows rests largely on the state's external reserves. CBK recorded import cover at 5.9 months as of October 15, well clear of the statutory four-month threshold.

That war chest has insulated local import bills from elevated global crude oil prices and stubborn U.S. inflation, both of which typically spur capital flight and weaken frontier-market currencies.

With remittance receipts consistently clearing Ksh50 billion each month and official reserves maintaining nearly six months of trade liquidity, commercial banks have faced little dollar liquidity strain.

The immediate test for the currency's stability now shifts to whether the USD 400 million monthly baseline can hold through the final quarter of 2026, when corporate dividend repatriations and festive season consumer import orders traditionally accelerate commercial foreign exchange demand.
