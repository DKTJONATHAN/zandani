Mining, Blue Economy and Maritime Affairs Cabinet Secretary Hassan Joho has thrown open the curtains on a high-stakes geopolitical tug-of-war, revealing that Washington's most formidable departments are locked in advanced talks with Nairobi over Kenya's subterranean wealth.

At the center of the diplomatic and corporate chess match is the fiercely contested Mrima Hill deposit in Kwale County. With the government's strict export ban on raw minerals forcing a hard pivot toward local value addition, at least six multinational heavyweights from the United States, Australia, and China are currently circling the site to secure development rights.

CS Joho confirmed that the ongoing negotiations stretch far beyond standard bilateral handshakes, drawing direct participation from key American institutions including the U.S. Departments of Commerce, Defense, and State. The push is heavily intertwined with the ongoing bidding process for Mrima Hill's rich industrial deposits of niobium, cobalt, copper, and nickel—a race that the Ministry expects to finalize by late October 2026.

“Several departments of the U.S. government are involved in the discussion,” CS Joho noted, as Nairobi looks to lock down capital-intensive investments that can hoist the mining sector's contribution to the national GDP from a meager 1% to 10% over the coming decade.

To sweeten the pot for these deep-pocketed foreign suitors, the Kenyan administration is rolling out a heavy roster of fiscal and infrastructural concessions. Successful bidders will be handed Special Economic Zone status, alongside streamlined port access, robust transport corridors, and targeted tax reliefs designed to offset the steep costs of setting up in-country processing plants.

The current diplomatic momentum follows high-level engagements in New York between President William Ruto and U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, reinforced by public assurances from U.S. Assistant Secretary of State for African Affairs Frank Garcia supporting localized processing on Kenyan soil.

With the October deadline fast approaching, the administration faces the delicate task of balancing aggressive foreign capital against strict domestic value-addition mandates as the bidding war for Kwale's subterranean assets enters its final stretch.
