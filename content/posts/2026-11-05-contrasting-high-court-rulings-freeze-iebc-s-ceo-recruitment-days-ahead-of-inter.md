A split in judicial assessments across High Court stations has thrown the recruitment of the Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission (IEBC) Chief Executive Officer into operational limbo just one week before candidates were scheduled to appear for final panel evaluations.

On Thursday, November 5, the High Court in Malindi granted conservatory orders freezing the selection process following a petition filed by a local resident. The court certified the matter as urgent and restrained the electoral agency from proceeding with scheduled candidate interviews pending a full hearing of the petition.

The Malindi order stands in contrast to a ruling issued just 24 hours earlier at the Milimani Law Courts in Nairobi. There, Lady Justice Grace Aburili refused to certify as urgent a separate petition seeking to halt the same process, questioning why the petitioner waited until the eve of interviews to challenge procedural frameworks established months prior.

### Divergent Legal Hurdles in Malindi and Nairobi

![IEBC chairperson Erastus Ethekon addressing the media on Tuesday, January 27, 2026](https://i.ibb.co/ZRDcbRY1/ef5db2f692a9.webp)

The petition in Malindi centers on candidate qualification transparency and citizenship compliance. The petitioner alleged that the commission failed to transparently outline how applicants were shortlisted and claimed non-Kenyan nationals were included on the roster—a condition strictly prohibited by national electoral laws.

Before the Malindi court issued its stay, the commission publicly addressed the citizenship issue, stating that foreign applicants had indeed submitted applications but were disqualified during preliminary administrative screening.

“Upon verification of national ID, citizenship and academic credentials, the final shortlist contains only Kenyan citizens,” the commission stated, clarifying that all foreign applicants failed national identification checks prior to shortlisting.

On October 28, the commission published a shortlisted panel of nine candidates—seven men and two women—setting their formal oral evaluations for November 12 and 13 at undisclosed locations.

In Nairobi, however, a parallel legal challenge took a different path. National Liberal Party (NLP) leader Augustus Kyalo Muli filed a certificate of urgency at the Milimani Law Courts on November 3, accusing the commission of unlawfully delegating its constitutional functions to a private recruitment firm.

Muli argued that the IEBC Act places executive selection duties strictly on the commission and does not permit wholesale outsourcing. He further alleged that the commission concealed its evaluation metrics, scoring matrices, and financial agreements with the private consultancy.

![Undated image of the Milimani Law Courts](https://i.ibb.co/qLb0bVY3/78d7d0c839e0.webp)

Justice Aburili declined to grant emergency orders, ruling that the recruitment process had been in the public domain since applications closed on August 11, 2026.

"I am not satisfied that the application is urgent, as the petitioner has had knowledge of the process being undertaken by the 1st Respondent and is in the public domain," Justice Aburili ruled, pointing out that candidates had already undergone longlisting and shortlisting over a multi-month period.

### Operational Impact on Commission Timelines

The Malindi injunction places the electoral body in legal gridlock. While the Nairobi ruling allowed the commission to proceed regarding its administrative outsourcing choices, the Malindi court's conservatory order legally prevents any interview panels from convening on November 12.

Unless the commission secures an emergency vacation or modification of the Malindi orders upon formal inter-partes hearing, the timeline for filling the electoral body's chief executive post remains suspended.
