The National Treasury reported to lawmakers on Tuesday that local borrowing now carries 56 per cent of Kenya’s sovereign debt obligations, confirming an inversion in the national debt profile that secures high yields for commercial lenders while intensifying credit constraints for private businesses.

Appearing before the National Assembly’s Departmental Committee on Finance and National Planning on October 27, National Treasury Principal Secretary Chris Kiptoo stated that total public and publicly guaranteed debt climbed to Ksh13.01 trillion by the close of the 2025/26 financial year in June, an expansion of Ksh1.2 trillion from Ksh11.81 trillion twelve months earlier.

"If you look at the division, 56 per cent is domestic debt, while 43.7 per cent is external debt," Kiptoo told the committee. "This is the reverse of the situation before, when external debt was higher than domestic debt."

### The Local Balance Sheet Pivot

![Treasury PS Chris Kiptoo appearing before NA Departmental Committee on Finance and National Planning on October 27, 2026](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_inner/public/images/media/chris-kiptoo.jpg.webp?itok=3Yfr7wyS)

The figures mark an operational shift in how the state funds its deficit. In June 2025, domestic debt stood at Ksh6.3 trillion against Ksh5.48 trillion in external loans. Over the subsequent twelve months, the state's aggressive issuance of local paper tilted the register decisively inward.

While institutional investors and commercial banks benefit from reliable, government-backed interest yields on local bonds, members of the parliamentary committee warned that the state's growing appetite for domestic liquidity comes at direct cost to the real economy. Lawmakers raised concerns that government paper is crowding out private enterprise, leaving mid-sized firms and commercial borrowers contending with steeper financing costs or shut out of bank credit entirely.

Treasury’s recourse to domestic debt has been propelled by a heavy debt-refinancing cycle. For the 2025/26 financial year, the ministry set a borrowing target exceeding Ksh1.5 trillion, designed to bridge a fiscal deficit of Ksh901.0 billion alongside Ksh646.3 billion required solely to refinance maturing debt.

### External Servicing Drain

Even as domestic paper expands, legacy offshore debt continues to exert significant pressure on the exchequer's liquidity. Kiptoo explained that foreign obligations absorbed substantial foreign exchange over the twelve-month period.

"Consequently, external debt service obligations required substantial outflows amounting to Ksh716.1 billion, split between Ksh504.3 billion in principal repayments and Ksh211.8 billion in interest payments," Kiptoo reported.

![Ruto-Mbadi](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_style/public/images/media/mbadi-ruto.jpg.webp?itok=OfsdkMpG)

That outflow coincided with lower-than-projected tax collections. Ordinary revenue, including Appropriations-in-Aid, yielded Ksh3.1988 trillion against an annual target of Ksh3.259 trillion, leaving Treasury to absorb a Ksh60.2 billion revenue shortfall at the end of June 2026.

### Absorption Gaps and Growth Headwinds

Despite the tightening debt envelope, the National Treasury recorded an overall budget absorption rate of 93.3 per cent for the 2025/26 financial year. The department utilized Ksh118.64 billion of its Ksh127.14 billion allocation, with recurrent outlays absorbing 95.2 per cent while development programs lagged at 88.8 per cent.

Kiptoo pointed to economic resilience, noting that Kenya’s real gross domestic product grew by 5.3 per cent in the first quarter of 2026, compared to 4.9 per cent during the same quarter in 2025.

However, the Principal Secretary cautioned the oversight committee that the broader execution of the national budget faces persistent structural hurdles, led by expanding debt servicing, inflationary pressures, geopolitical friction, and the chronically sluggish absorption of donor-funded development financing.
