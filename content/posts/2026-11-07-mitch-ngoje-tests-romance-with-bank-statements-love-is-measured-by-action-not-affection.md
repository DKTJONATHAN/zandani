Mitch Ngoje is drawing a clean, unromantic line through modern courtship: if partners cannot combine money to fund their traditional dowry and wedding milestones, their declarations of affection carry little practical weight.

Speaking on the *Joyride* podcast, the husband of content creator Michelle Ngoje pushed back against the convention of keeping personal bank accounts strictly insulated during courtship. Instead, he presented shared banking as an early, necessary stress test for couples moving toward marriage, arguing that financial alignment should precede the walk down the aisle.

At the center of his argument is a distinction between emotional warmth and tangible responsibility. In an urban relationship landscape where grand gestures often obscure fundamental money problems, Ngoje challenged the idea that good intentions can substitute for an operational financial plan.

“A lot of people take love as affection, but love is measured by action,” he said during the session.

### The Pre-Ruracio Liquidity Test

![Silvia Of The Mziza Family Shocked After Dealing with Nairobi Landlords' Demands](https://i.ibb.co/cKt7f19y/b83c3c363f26.webp)

For many couples negotiating Kenya's customary and formal wedding circuits, negotiations begin long before anyone sits down with family elders. Between negotiation committees, venue retainers, wardrobe procurement, and the formal Ruracio itself, the cumulative budget frequently strains young couples without deep family subsidies.

Ngoje proposed opening a joint savings account specifically earmarked for these ceremonial steps. By turning milestone planning into an active shared balance sheet, both partners establish a mutual pool rather than defaulting to ad-hoc fundraising or quiet resentment.

The mechanism, he explained, allows partners to track real-time progress toward common milestones rather than guessing each other's financial stamina. Setting up a dedicated account forces a couple to establish clear contribution baselines early, clarifying how much each individual can afford to deposit toward upcoming rites.

### Breaking One-Sided Ceremony Burden

Beyond basic savings accumulation, Ngoje highlighted how shared accounts disrupt the traditional expectation where the financial brunt of dowry and wedding production falls onto one person—typically the groom.

![Morgan Bahati Speaks on Online Trolls, Thanks Father Bahati for Standing by Him](https://i.ibb.co/6CZMycR/8922c935be01.webp)

When a single partner is expected to shoulder the entire cost of the Ruracio process, the dynamic introduces hidden strain into the foundation of the home. Shared deposits, by contrast, distribute ownership of the milestone. Both parties arrive at the ceremony as active stakeholders who negotiated the financial terms together.

That arrangement requires couples to confront uncomfortable money discussions before exchanging vows. Agreeing on exact contribution ratios demands an audit of income, recurring debts, and realistic timelines, removing the surprise of hidden financial commitments after the event has wrapped.

### The Governance Rule: Honesty Over Novelty

Yet pooling capital before marriage carries distinct vulnerabilities. A joint facility demands complete transparency around access, sign-off rules, and fund security.

Ngoje acknowledged that creating a joint savings vehicle is not a cosmetic relationship fix. Without strict honesty and open communication regarding how the funds are deployed and monitored, shared accounts can introduce friction rather than resolve it. Mutual visibility functions only when both individuals agree on the operating rules before the first deposit is transferred.

For Ngoje, the joint account remains less about the banking product itself and more about accountability. By treating milestone funding as a joint project with shared liability, couples confirm whether their partnership can survive real-world pressures long before the wedding guests arrive.
