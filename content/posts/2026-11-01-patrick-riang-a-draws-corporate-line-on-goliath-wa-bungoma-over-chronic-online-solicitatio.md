Isaac Otesa, the towering western Kenya internet sensation known as Goliath Wa Bungoma, has run directly into the expectations of corporate sponsorship after his primary benefactor publicly called time on his persistent online appeals for cash.

Patrick Riang’a, the proprietor of Nairobi-based dealership Car Soko, openly voiced his frustration with Otesa’s continued reliance on social media fundraising, demanding accountability from a creator who had supposedly crossed the bridge from public distress into formal corporate employment.

Riang’a did not mince his words when confronting the creator’s latest round of public solicitations. “You are always begging online, yet I left you with a job,” Riang’a stated, drawing a sharp boundary between commercial brand representation and indefinite crowd-funding.

### The Corporate Transition

The dispute exposes the precarious transition facing viral figures in Nairobi’s fast-moving online creator economy. Otesa initially came into national focus because of his extraordinary height and striking physical likeness to Bradley Marongo, popularised as Gen Z Goliath.

Yet the transition from an internet novelty into self-sustaining employment proved bruising. After travelling from Bungoma to Nairobi in pursuit of opportunities, Otesa quickly ran out of resources. Stranded in the capital without a steady income or reliable shelter, his open vulnerability generated widespread sympathy across digital channels.

That viral outcry mobilised notable interventions. Public figure Geoffrey Mosiria stepped into the situation, working to connect Otesa with institutional partners capable of turning transient public interest into regular work.

Those efforts culminated in Riang’a stepping forward at Car Soko to offer a structured commercial role. Under the arrangement, Otesa was engaged as a brand ambassador tasked with pitching inventory, generating showroom foot traffic, and anchoring social media promotional campaigns across the dealership’s commercial platforms.

### Accountability Beyond the Viral Wave

For Car Soko, the ambassador contract was intended as a permanent exit ramp from crisis management into steady professional earnings. For Otesa, it offered an institutional platform to convert digital visibility into a recurring livelihood.

Yet the continuation of distress-driven financial appeals on Otesa’s personal feeds has created friction with the commercial image Car Soko sought to build around him. Rather than serving as an advertisement for personal uplift and professional stability, the recurring pleas for external assistance have instead cast doubt over how the creator manages his commercial gains.

Riang’a’s intervention shifts the narrative away from casual internet charity toward commercial accountability. In taking the issue to the public square, the car dealer has placed the onus squarely back on Otesa to treat his brand ambassador portfolio as a functioning trade rather than a supplement to ongoing digital appeals.
