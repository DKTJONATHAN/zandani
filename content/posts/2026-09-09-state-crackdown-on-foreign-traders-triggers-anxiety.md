President William Ruto ordered the closure of small-scale businesses operated by foreign nationals on September 2, sparking a nationwide scramble. The directive restricts informal retail and unskilled labor to Kenyan citizens. Following the announcement, hundreds of Burundian, Ugandan, and Rwandan traders began shuttering their kiosks and stalls, fearing immediate deportation.

Tension peaked on Monday outside the Burundian embassy in Nairobi. Young men clutching suitcases formed long queues to seek clarity on their status. According to U.N. data, approximately 16,000 Burundian refugees reside in Kenya, with many operating coffee carts or second-hand clothing businesses in city estates. Munezero Farnke, an 18-year-old trader, expressed his confusion while waiting for travel documentation. He stated that he had lived in the country for three years and lacked the resources for an immediate return to Burundi.

The Kenya National Commission on Human Rights cautioned the government against indiscriminate enforcement. They stated that migration measures must be implemented fairly and without prejudice toward specific nationalities. In response to mounting backlash, the government opened a temporary registration window for undocumented East African Community citizens. A state spokesperson noted that this amnesty allows individuals to reside legally while accessing health and banking services.

Public reaction on social media remains polarized. Senior Counsel Ahmednasir Abdullahi criticized the move, calling it a form of xenophobia that targets diligent workers. Conversely, some online users argued that the policy is a necessary enforcement of immigration law. Commentators like Dommie Alvin pointed out that the government needs to ensure all residents are properly documented to maintain security and accountability. Others, such as user Sox, questioned why the enforcement seems focused on specific groups while ignoring undocumented migrants from other regions.

### The take

The optics of young men packing their lives into suitcases outside embassies are damaging to Kenya’s image as an East African hub. While the government claims this is about protecting local jobs, it feels like a blunt instrument aimed at the most vulnerable segments of the informal sector. We have to ask why we are targeting street-level vendors while ignoring larger regulatory failures in our labor market.

For the average motorist stuck in gridlock near Westlands or Kasarani, this creates a false sense of progress. Chasing a coffee seller does not solve the structural unemployment crisis facing our graduates. If we truly want to protect local business, we should focus on providing credit to our own entrepreneurs rather than policing the survival tactics of our neighbors. By choosing this path, the state risks alienating our regional partners and hardening a xenophobic culture that serves nobody. When the dust settles, we will find that we have sacrificed our moral authority in the region for a policy that offers no real economic uplift for the local hustler.
