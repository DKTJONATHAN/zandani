The detection of falsified Lucentis (Ranibizumab) injections in the Kenyan market has surfaced a deeper, more persistent issue in the national drug supply chain: the friction between local business licensing and centralized pharmaceutical regulation. 

While the Pharmacy and Poisons Board (PPB) confirmed the seizure of counterfeit 0.3mg injections bearing batch numbers 18862110 and 18802112, the incident serves as a stress test for the current regulatory architecture. The Pharmaceutical Society of Kenya (PSK) recently signaled that county government business permits—often mistaken for operational authority—do not substitute for the strict legal licenses required to practice pharmacy or operate a facility. This jurisdictional overlap provides a convenient smokescreen for the distribution of illicit medical products.

### The Product Discrepancies

![An image of the falsified Luncentis (Ranibizumab) injection batch numbers 18862110 and 18802112.](https://i.ibb.co/5XN7VNZc/e1b2242c5eac.webp)

The falsified injections, which illicitly claimed to be manufactured by Genentech, were flagged following post-marketing surveillance. Analysts comparing the fake stock against genuine supplies from Novartis Overseas Investment AG highlighted critical differences that patients and healthcare providers can use to verify authenticity:

* **Branding:** The counterfeit product is labeled "Lucentis," whereas the registered, authentic brand in the Kenyan market is "Patizra."
* **Concentration:** The fakes are marked as 0.3mg, while the registered, legal formulation is 10mg/mL.
* **Packaging:** The illicit vials are dark amber, deviating from the clear glass vials used for the legitimate medicine.

### Accountability and Market Enforcement

![Pharmacy ,Drugs](https://i.ibb.co/KjtDwQ0c/4edecf93ce81.webp)

Beyond the physical danger to patients—who risk receiving treatments with no active ingredients or hazardous contaminants—the incident highlights the difficulty in tracking the origin of such medicine. The PPB has already secured one arrest, but investigators are now forced to trace the product’s point of entry through a complex web of distribution points that have become increasingly difficult to monitor as county and national roles continue to blur.

For facilities, the directive is to immediately quarantine any stock matching the flagged batch numbers. However, the PSK’s recent warning suggests that the problem of counterfeit entry points into the market will persist as long as premises operate under the assumption that a general county business permit grants the right to handle sensitive pharmaceuticals.

As the investigation continues, the focus for procurement agencies and hospitals remains on the strict adherence to Board-licensed supply chains. The regulator has made it clear: if a facility is not explicitly licensed by the PPB to distribute, the risk of encountering falsified medicine increases significantly, regardless of the business permits displayed on the wall.
