Fred Matiang’i is not playing along with the smiling photo-ops.

While his fellow opposition principals have spent recent weeks trying to project an effortless, airtight alliance against President William Ruto, the Jubilee Party presidential hopeful walked into a local radio studio on Tuesday, October 13, 2026, and cleanly pulled the pin on that collective daydream.

His verdict on the backroom negotiations to craft a joint ticket and parcel out state power ahead of the 2027 General Election was refreshingly, if brutally, blunt.

“Forming a coalition government is not easy. Whoever tells you it’s easy is lying,” Matiang’i said during the morning broadcast. “For now, we have not yet agreed. Talks are still ongoing.”

### Too Many Captains at the Table

For an opposition camp that routinely parades a star-studded lineup before the microphones, the confession hit a raw nerve. The roster under the United Opposition umbrella is not short on titanic egos or former state power brokers accustomed to having their calls answered on the first ring.

Seated around that negotiating table are Wiper leader Kalonzo Musyoka, Democracy for the Citizens Party (DCP) boss Rigathi Gachagua, People’s Liberation Party chief Martha Karua, Democratic Party leader Justin Muturi, and Democratic Action Party–Kenya’s Eugene Wamalwa. Each brings a substantial regional fiefdom, and each has maintained that removing Ruto requires standing behind one single candidate.

Yet as Matiang’i made clear, agreeing to run together on a poster is child's play compared to dividing the spoils of a hypothetical state house. Striking a pre-election campaign pact requires good speeches; designing an ironclad post-election governance structure demands politicians willingly trade away ministries, cabinet appointments, and running-mate slots they believe they have already earned.

By publicly acknowledging that the principals remain deadlocked, Matiang’i exposed the friction humming beneath the surface of what was supposed to be a breezy reunion tour.

### Calling Out the Keyboard Cops

Matiang’i did not limit his radio reality check to his own political partners. The former Interior Cabinet Secretary—who once ran Kenya's security machinery with an iron fist—pivoted swiftly to the state’s current policing habits, mocking what he called a bizarre sense of official urgency.

Taking aim at the contrast between street violence and digital clampdowns, Matiang’i took umbrage with authorities trailing Gen Z and outspoken online commentators while armed political disruption goes unpunished on the ground.

“How can you be unable to arrest criminals walking with machetes while at the same time you arrest young people posting on their social media?” he posed.

It was a calculated jab, tailored to connect with an online populace increasingly weary of heavy-handed cyber-surveillance, even as political rallies degenerate into weaponized disruptions without a single pair of handcuffs coming out for the instigators.

### Eyeing the Pasture

Rather than staying trapped in the capital's boardroom melodrama, the Jubilee flagbearer used the tail end of his airtime to stake out territory far from the high-society lunch tables of Nairobi.

Matiang’i signaled that while the opposition's national steering committee haggles over hierarchy, Jubilee is already locking down its ground network. He turned specifically to the pastoralist belt, flagging endemic water scarcity across Narok and Kajiado counties as a cornerstone of his personal campaign ledger.

Instead of waiting on a white-smoke summit from his coalition partners, Matiang’i confirmed his party has already selected its prospective gubernatorial candidates for both Maasai-land counties, making it clear that when the opposition finally decides who gets what, he intends to hold real cards at the grassroots.

For now, the smiling press conferences will continue. But the former CS has officially set the clock: the talks are not done, the seats are not divided, and nobody in that boardroom is getting a free pass.
