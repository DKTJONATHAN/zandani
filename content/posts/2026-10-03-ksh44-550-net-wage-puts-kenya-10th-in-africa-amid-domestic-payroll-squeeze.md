A typical formal-sector worker in Kenya takes home an estimated Ksh44,553 ($344) a month after statutory payroll cuts, securing the country a spot among Africa’s ten highest-paying labor markets while creating a wide earnings gulf with its immediate neighbors.

The figures, compiled by cross-border economic data tracker LiveGDP, place Kenya 10th on the continent and 132nd worldwide in average net monthly compensation. The evaluation calculated net remuneration across 54 African economies by measuring employee earnings after income tax and mandatory deductions, converting national figures to U.S. dollars for baseline comparison.

While the threshold keeps Kenya within Africa's top tier, the figure lands at a time of acute scrutiny over domestic pay slips. Salaried workers have seen their disposable earnings eroded by an expanding basket of mandatory payroll deductions, led by the standard Pay As You Earn (PAYE) income tax schedules, the 1.5 percent statutory Housing Levy, and the 2.75 percent deduction toward the Social Health Insurance Fund (SHIF).

### The Continental Pecking Order

At the continental peak, Djibouti registered Africa’s highest average take-home pay at Ksh367,311 ($2,836) per month, ranking 27th globally. It was followed by South Africa at Ksh164,486 ($1,270) and Seychelles at Ksh102,318 ($790).

![Kenyans trekking along Moi Avenue in Nairobi Central Business District (CBD).](https://i.ibb.co/TMxRS0xj/6a8db8f7cf4e.webp)

Kenya sits just behind Cameroon, which posted an average monthly net salary of Ksh44,683 ($345), while Ghana tracked closely in 11th position at Ksh44,035 ($340). The rest of the continental top 10 comprises Botswana, Namibia, Mauritius, Morocco, and Equatorial Guinea.

The findings highlight deep structural disparities in baseline earnings across Africa, with 35 of the 54 surveyed nations posting average monthly net earnings below Ksh25,903 ($200).

At the lowest end of the spectrum, South Sudan recorded the continent's leanest net return at Ksh9,066 ($70), trailed closely by Burundi at Ksh9,713 ($75), Sierra Leone at Ksh10,361 ($80), and Gambia at Ksh11,008 ($85). Sudan and Malawi both tied at Ksh11,656 ($90).

### East Africa’s Compensation Divide

Within the East African Community, Kenya holds a pronounced wage premium over adjacent economies, with its average take-home pay outpacing its neighbors by wide margins.

![An undated photo of jobseekers in a queuing](https://i.ibb.co/Q3BmZZTf/f1166e82cb15.webp)

Tanzania placed 20th on the continent with an estimated average net pay of Ksh25,255 ($195)—roughly 43 percent below Kenya's figure. Uganda ranked 26th with take-home compensation of Ksh20,722 ($160), while Rwanda settled in 31st position at Ksh18,780 ($145).

Further afield in the regional economic bloc, the Democratic Republic of Congo placed 33rd with Ksh18,392 ($142), and Somalia recorded Ksh15,540 ($120) to place 42nd.

### Nominal Pay Versus Cost of Living

LiveGDP noted that structural drivers—including industrialization levels, productivity benchmarks, sector-specific labor demand, tax policies, and currency volatility—explain the broad salary differences documented across borders.

However, nominal take-home rankings offer an incomplete measure of household welfare. Because mandatory payroll deductions are subtracted before the net wage is computed, the metric reflects what reaches an employee's account, but it does not account for the local prices of essential goods and services.

Variations in local costs for rental housing, food staples, commuter transit, and schooling mean a worker earning $344 in Nairobi faces markedly different living expenditure pressures than an employee earning equivalent or lower nominal wages elsewhere on the continent. The metric serves as a comparative benchmark of monetary payroll liquidity rather than an index of actual domestic purchasing power.
