The executive has effectively placed the government’s project portfolio under a high-pressure sprint, ordering line ministries to insulate flagship infrastructure and social programs from the impending exodus of officials seeking elective office.

Following a session of the Joint Cabinet Committee on the Discharge of Government Business chaired by Deputy President Kithure Kindiki, the administration moved to solidify leadership structures across key departments. The directive explicitly aims to guard against administrative paralysis as the 2027 General Election cycle begins to draw high-ranking civil servants toward the campaign trail.

### The February Bottleneck

![Deputy President Kithure Kindiki during the 5th Joint Cabinet Committee on discharge of Government Business on October 13, 2026 at his Karen Residence](https://i.ibb.co/LdD8fPZp/e290ec49104b.webp)

The central concern for the cabinet is the Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission (IEBC) requirement, which mandates that public officers seeking elective posts resign by February 10, 2027. This deadline creates a significant operational risk: the departure of primary drivers for the Bottom-Up Economic Transformation Agenda (BETA) could leave key initiatives, such as the Affordable Housing Programme and Universal Health Coverage, in a state of suspended animation just as the administration seeks to showcase its term-one success.

To mitigate this, the administration has moved to lock in progress now. A primary indicator of this new urgency is the Rironi-Mau Summit Highway expansion, where the completion target has been pulled forward by two months, from June 2027 to April 2027—effectively forcing a finish line before the electoral fervor peaks.

### Accountability and Continuity

![President William Ruto chairs a cabinet meeting on Tuesday February 10, 2026](https://i.ibb.co/5XJz0JSN/0f9051793296.webp)

Public Service Head Felix Koskei has moved to reinforce these Cabinet directives with a warning against the potential weaponization of public office during the transition. Koskei emphasized that until the February resignation date, there can be no administrative vacuum or diversion of state resources into partisan activity.

“There must be no disengagement from the core mandate, no slowing of implementation,” Koskei noted in a follow-up directive. The instruction explicitly draws a line between explaining government policy, which is permitted, and active campaigning, which is barred for those currently on the state payroll.

The administration’s strategy now relies on 'firming up' leadership within ministries. This implies a possible reshuffle or a doubling-down of oversight responsibilities to ensure that even if political aspirants depart in February, the technical continuity of flagship projects remains unbroken.

As the government enters this final stretch, the challenge will be reconciling the speed required for these projects with the inevitable distractions of the 2027 campaign calendar. Whether these ministerial oversight measures will be enough to shield high-stakes infrastructure from the high turnover of a pre-election year remains the primary test for the current Cabinet’s final year in office.
