East African Community citizens crossing into Kenya without a visa still face the full weight of local labour and commercial regulations before setting up shop or taking up formal employment, immigration authorities have reaffirmed.

### What we know
* Regional free movement guarantees frictionless border crossings, yet commercial rights remain tightly shackled to domestic regulatory and tax frameworks across member states.
* While Nairobi scrapped steep permit fees for community nationals, obtaining documentation like the Class R permit and single business licences remains compulsory.
* State enforcement agencies are actively untangling diplomatic open-door rhetoric from local market realities following periodic crackdowns on unregistered cross-border business operators.
* Cross-border traders now face municipal bureaucracy rather than border guards, making county-level compliance the true testing ground for regional integration.

The framework governing this cross-border flow rests on the East African Community (EAC) Common Market Protocol. While the treaty guarantees the free movement of persons, labour, and services across partner states, it draws an unyielding line between entering as a visitor and establishing an enterprise. Visitors from member states enter Kenya at zero visa cost for social visits or exploratory travel, but transitioning into commercial trade triggers a separate regulatory pipeline.

To legally break ground, an incoming regional entrepreneur must register a formal business entity with the Business Registration Service, acquire a Personal Identification Number from the Kenya Revenue Authority, and obtain the requisite county government trading licences. On the immigration front, the Directorate of Immigration Services routes these cases through the Class R permit, tailored specifically for EAC citizens residing in Kenya for employment, trade, or specified business activities.

The primary incentive under the regional pact is financial rather than procedural. Kenya maintains a zero-shilling fee policy for Class R permits issued to EAC citizens, removing the prohibitive statutory charges leveled against other foreign nationals. Similarly, temporary business assignments, consultancy work, and short-term professional engagements fall under the Special Pass window, which is equally issued at no cost to partner state citizens. Yet despite the zero price tag, the documentation remains mandatory.

On the ground in trading hubs across Nairobi, the distinction has frequently blurred into conflict. Confusion often surfaces when small-scale traders assume the waiver of visa requirements grants carte blanche to trade in open-air markets or manage retail stalls without local municipal clearance.

State policy has oscillated between aggressive enforcement and diplomatic reassurance. Following crackdowns on undocumented foreign traders, President William Ruto clarified that the push aimed at standardisation, not exclusion.

“Kenya is very open for business,” Ruto stated during a policy review addressing regional trade tensions. “I know there has been a lot of debate about who should work in Kenya or should invest in Kenya. Let me say this for the record. All investors have a space in Kenya. The process will be coordinated by the relevant government agencies, in consultation with the embassies concerned, to provide affected persons with a clear and structured opportunity to regularise their immigration status and business operations in accordance with the law.”

For the regional trader navigating the corridor between Kampala, Dar es Salaam, Kigali, and Nairobi, the administrative reality is settled: entry is automatic, but commerce requires a paper trail.
