A heavy industrial mobilization is underway along the corridor linking the Coast to the northwest following the arrival of a Ksh2.59 billion onshore drilling rig at Kilindini Port.

The 1,500-horsepower GW70 rig, leased by Gulf Energy E&P BV SEZ from Great Wall Drilling Company, was offloaded by the Kenya Ports Authority after arriving aboard the vessel *MV Transit Sedanka* from Duqm Port in Oman. The massive piece of equipment—previously deployed on projects for Abu Dhabi National Oil Company—forms the mechanical anchor of a tight project management schedule designed to achieve first commercial crude oil production by December 2026.

![Oil Rig Turkana](https://i.ibb.co/rRY5x4Lc/2b45e83604f2.webp)

Gulf Energy Chief Executive Officer Paul Limoh confirmed that the company has locked in a November 1 target to begin actual drilling operations in the South Lokichar Basin. To meet that window, the rig must undergo mandatory commissioning and acceptance checks before navigating the arduous overland transport route from Mombasa to Turkana County.

The deployment relies on a heavy-duty corporate service network. Gulf Energy has contracted Baker Hughes to manage integrated well services, while SLB has been brought in to supply the Early Production Facility required for the initial phase. These operations form the opening leg of a broader Ksh776.7 billion infrastructure rollout aimed at extracting an initial 20,000 barrels of crude oil per day, before scaling output to 50,000 barrels daily in a subsequent phase.

![transit-sedanka-1.jpg.jpeg](https://i.ibb.co/8nwQMXMR/beadb7736d60.webp)

Behind the rush lies a complex regional energy equation. The South Lokichar Basin, discovered by Tullow Oil at the Ngamia-1 well in 2012, holds an estimated 560 million barrels of recoverable resources from an initial volume-in-place approaching 4 billion barrels. Successful extraction in Turkana is viewed as a vital feedstock test for larger downstream ambitions on the coast, where separate private investments—including proposals for a massive multi-trillion-shilling refinery in Lamu—depend on a reliable domestic crude supply.

For now, operations rest on the immediate logistical challenge: clearing the port gates, completing technical inspections, and hauling the heavy hardware hundreds of kilometers north ahead of the November spud-in date.
