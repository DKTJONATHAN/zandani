According to Education Cabinet Secretary Julius Ogamba, a Ksh4.9 billion National Treasury allocation will transition 20,000 Junior Secondary School (JSS) intern teachers onto permanent and pensionable contracts starting January 1, 2027, securing long-sought job stability for a major portion of the country’s junior school workforce.

Yet the fiscal arrangement leaves a larger contingent behind. Testifying before the Senate on Wednesday, October 7, Ogamba revealed that more than half of the current intern establishment—numbering 24,000 educators—must remain on temporary monthly stipends until the government identifies additional money in subsequent budget cycles.

“In the financial year of 2026/2027, the National Treasury has provided funding for the recruitment of 20,000 teachers for junior schools on permanent and pensionable terms, effective January 1, 2027,” Ogamba told senators, confirming that the money has now been formalized within state spending plans.

The confirmation provides administrative backing to commitments delivered by President William Ruto during World Teachers’ Day proceedings at Kasarani Stadium on Monday, October 5. However, Ogamba’s detailed breakdown laid bare the fiscal ceiling that restricts the Ministry of Education and the Teachers Service Commission (TSC) from absorbing the entire junior secondary teaching bench in a single sweep.

![cs-ogamba.jpg](https://i.ibb.co/FLhqqGYM/c3bea4326b36.webp)

### A Divided Payroll in Junior Secondary

The January 2027 rollout effectively establishes a dual-tier workforce across public junior secondary classrooms. While 20,000 instructors will enter formal civil service terms with standard benefits and retirement protections, the remaining 24,000 will continue operating under contract terms that have drawn sustained labor unrest and litigation.

Ogamba acknowledged that bringing the deferred group onto permanent payrolls depends on future bargaining with fiscal authorities rather than an existing statutory schedule.

“We will continue to engage the National Treasury and other stakeholders with a view to securing additional resources to facilitate the gradual absorption of the remaining 24,000 intern teachers into permanent and pensionable service,” Ogamba stated.

The revised timeline also adjusts expectations previously set by Treasury Cabinet Secretary John Mbadi, who informed Parliament earlier this year that the 24,000 interns would transition to permanent terms from July 2027. Ogamba’s Senate remarks clarify that any such extension beyond the initial 20,000 remains unfinanced and will hinge entirely on the availability of supplementary allocations.

### Legal Pressures and Ongoing Classroom Gaps

![mbadi-ogamba-treasury.jpg](https://i.ibb.co/39HQMcp4/28522f09f5c6.webp)

The staged onboarding unfolds against a backdrop of unresolved legal friction. In April, the courts issued orders directing intern teachers to remain at their duty stations pending the full hearing and determination of a petition contesting the constitutionality of the internship setup.

Ogamba stressed that the state is maintaining its end of that judicial balance by continuing stipend disbursements while relying on the cadre to keep public schools running.

“The Ministry of Education and the Teachers Service Commission will continue to comply with court directives while pursuing long-term measures to address teacher staffing requirements in Junior Secondary Schools,” he said.

Even as the state balances the absorption of serving personnel against scarce revenue, broader staffing pressures remain severe. The national administration has signaled ambitious expansion targets to bridge acute classroom deficits, with the presidency outlining parallel plans to recruit an additional 20,000 new teachers before the end of 2026.

Whether the exchequer can support both an influx of new hires and the Ksh4.9 billion operational shift for the first 20,000 interns will become clear when the TSC and Treasury present the finalized supplementary spending framework to lawmakers.
