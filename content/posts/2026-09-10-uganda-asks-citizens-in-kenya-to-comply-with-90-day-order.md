Uganda’s First Deputy Prime Minister Rebecca Kadaga has told thousands of her compatriots working across Kenya to visit their High Commission in Nairobi and regularise their immigration papers, responding directly to State House’s three-month ultimatum targeting undocumented foreign nationals and informal traders.

**What we know**
* State House Spokesperson Hussein Mohamed issued a 90-day compliance window for all foreigners living and running businesses in Kenya without valid permits.
* Rebecca Kadaga directed Ugandans to formalise trade, secure county business licences, and open bank accounts instead of hawking without paperwork.
* The Ugandan High Commission on Riverside Drive in Nairobi will assist citizens seeking legal status before the November deadline.
* Thousands of regional traders in Nairobi, Mombasa, and Eldoret handle small-scale merchandise ranging from roasted groundnuts to second-hand garments.

Kadaga dispelled rumors that Nairobi was preparing mass deportations, clarifying that the order requires foreigners to bring their enterprises into formal tax and licensing brackets. She pointed out that while East African Community protocols guarantee free movement of labour, national sovereignty still dictates local rules. We are not yet a federation, she reminded traders, urging them to ditch undocumented hawking for formal enterprises.

Across commercial hubs like Eastleigh, Gikomba, and Kawangware, the directive puts non-compliant foreign operators on alert. Hussein noted that the multi-agency exercise will enforce work permits, registration, and municipal single business permits. Those city licences cost anywhere from KSh 5,000 to over KSh 50,000 annually, depending on the scale and location of the enterprise.

In settlements across Kasarani and along Jogoo Road, the announcement has rattled small vendors. Hundreds of Ugandan nationals make a living selling produce brought through the Busia and Malaba border posts. Most operate outside formal banking, relying entirely on mobile money wallets and supplier trust networks that leave zero records with immigration officers at Nyayo House.

Current Kenyan immigration rules mandate work permits for specific commercial cadres, but the bureaucratic hurdles along Kenyatta Avenue remain high for anyone making KSh 1,500 daily profit. While the regional integration treaty envisions hassle-free commerce, local enforcement agencies frequently treat undocumented East African traders as illegal immigrants subject to court fines or administrative pushbacks at border outposts.

### The Nairobi read

Watch the footbridge at Ngara or the matatu stage at Roysambu around dusk. The vendor serving hot spiced tea from an aluminium flask is often from Tororo or Mbale. The trader sorting groundnuts near the Westlands roundabout rarely carries an alien card in his pocket. Telling a roadside hawker to produce a tax clearance pin, an immigration file from Nyayo House, and a City Hall single business permit within ninety days is polite language for sweeping the pavement clean.

City Hall inspectors and local police gain fresh leverage to run daily crackdowns, squeezing informal fees out of regional workers who cannot afford statutory legal fees. Kenya loves to market itself as the commercial capital of the region, yet its street economy runs on cheap, energetic East African labour. If the administration turns this registration order into a hard dragnet, it will not deport thousands back to the border. It will simply hike the bribes along River Road while the price of breakfast goes up.
