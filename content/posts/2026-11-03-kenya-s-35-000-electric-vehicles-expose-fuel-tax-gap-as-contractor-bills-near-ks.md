The rapid expansion of Kenya’s electric vehicle fleet is forcing a structural rewrite of how the country pays for highway repairs, as declining fuel consumption begins to drain the primary cash engine used to maintain roads and manage nearly Ksh100 billion in unpaid contractor debts.

In a statement issued on Tuesday, November 3, the State Department for Roads confirmed it is actively exploring an alternative financing mechanism outside the traditional Road Maintenance Levy Fund (RMLF).

The ministry acknowledged that as more drivers convert to electric mobility, petrol and diesel consumption is dropping, systematically reducing the revenues collected per litre at fuel pumps across the country.

"The State Department for Roads is exploring alternative financing mechanisms to mitigate the potential decline in Road Maintenance Levy Fund revenues arising from the growing adoption of electric vehicles, which reduces fuel consumption," the statement noted.

### Debt Pressures and Infrastructure Spending

![An image of vehicles moving swiftly along Waiyaki Way in normal traffic on November 2, 2026](https://i.ibb.co/CKdc82D5/f7f05ae4fba5.webp)

The timing of the policy shift intersects with significant financial friction within the transport sector. On October 30, Roads and Transport Cabinet Secretary Davis Chirchir revealed that gross pending bills owed to road contractors stood at Ksh98.17 billion as of June 2026.

To keep major trunk routes operational and prevent stalled capital works, the ministry has disbursed Ksh208.6 billion through a specialized securitisation framework. These funds have been channeled toward clearing accumulated debt to contractors while keeping capital-intensive corridors moving, including the widening of the James Gichuru–Rironi Road and structural upgrades along the Garissa–Isiolo transport corridor.

However, while securitisation addresses legacy developer debt, routine road upkeep—filling potholes, resurfacing asphalt, and maintaining drainage—depends entirely on the fuel levy collected from motorists at every fill-up.

### The EV Fleet Paradox

Kenya’s road maintenance funding model operates on a direct consumption principle: the more petrol and diesel purchased at service stations, the more cash flows into national highway repair accounts. 

![CS Chirchir](https://i.ibb.co/DZNpyDs/4413e474ed2e.webp)

Ministry figures show that Kenya now has over 35,000 electric vehicles in active operation, with registration numbers expected to accelerate. Every zero-emission car, bus, or two-wheeler that bypasses the fuel pump reduces the pool of funds available to repair the very roads those vehicles travel on.

With fuel tax collections trending downward relative to overall road usage, transport planners argue that relying exclusively on a pump-based levy is no longer sustainable.

### Searching for a New Funding Model

The ministry has emphasized the urgency of establishing predictable, diversified road-sector revenue channels that do not rely strictly on fossil fuel sales.

While the government has not yet detailed the precise architecture of the proposed levy—such as whether it will take the form of an annual EV road-use fee, weight-based registration tariffs, or distance charges—the decision signals an inevitable transition toward direct user fees for zero-emission transit.

The next step for the State Department for Roads involves finalizing the policy framework for public and parliamentary review, balancing the national goal of decarbonizing transport against the immediate necessity of keeping Kenya's road networks maintained.
