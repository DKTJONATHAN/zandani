A compounding penalty burden has pushed county pension liabilities past Ksh118 billion, revealing a structural trap where punitive statutory interest now outstrips the actual unremitted retirement money by more than five to one.

Testimony presented to the Senate Labour Committee on Friday showed that while county administrations owe workers Ksh17.7 billion in principal pension contributions, accumulated default penalties have climbed to Ksh99.1 billion. Alongside a Ksh2 billion actuarial deficit, the resulting Ksh118.8 billion liability illustrates how administrative delays and diverted payroll deductions have multiplied into an intractable fiscal liability for devolved units.

The figures, disclosed by Retirement Benefits Authority (RBA) Chief Executive Officer Charles Machira, landed alongside separate findings from Controller of Budget Margaret Nyakang’o detailing broader cash flow distress across the 47 counties. Nyakang'o reported that total county arrears—spanning unpaid worker salaries and statutory third-party deductions—stood at Ksh100.24 billion as of June 30, 2026.

### The Limits of Automation

![Auditor General Margaret Nyakang'o](https://i.ibb.co/ZR4HpNY1/ee6c24852048.webp)

The disclosures underscore the limitations of recent public finance management interventions. On July 1, 2026, the state rolled out an automated bridge connecting the Integrated Financial Management Information System (IFMIS) directly to the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK). The mechanism processes payroll deductions simultaneously with salary requisitions, routing pension and statutory remittances at the point of disbursement to prevent county executives from diverting the funds into operational expenses.

Yet while the digital integration seals future leaks, Nyakang’o told lawmakers that the platform offers no mechanism to liquidate legacy defaults. 

"The new system does not clear existing debts," Nyakang’o said, emphasizing that the burden of clearing the Ksh100.24 billion backlog remains entirely on individual county balances.

For thousands of county civil servants approaching retirement, that historical overhang poses an immediate threat to post-service security. When counties withhold deductions on paper but fail to transfer cash to pension schemes, administrators cannot credit individual worker accounts or disburse full statutory benefits upon retirement.

### Budgetary Logjams and Requisitions

The Controller of Budget traced the origin of the salary crisis to procedural lapses within county treasuries rather than mere exchequer shortfalls. County spending plans frequently arrive late or fail to meet legal approval thresholds, freezing expenditure requisitions inside IFMIS.

![County Government Workers Union of Kenya](https://i.ibb.co/Hp1rZZYs/f5f709587f82.webp)

According to Nyakang’o, county budgets regularly omit mandatory repayment plans for pending bills. Without an approved schedule demonstrating how historical obligations will be cleared alongside recurrent operations, the Controller of Budget cannot legally sign off on expenditure approvals, leaving staff salaries caught in administrative deadlocks.

Inadequate budget allocation for existing wage liabilities has compounded the delays, allowing statutory fines to compound month after month under RBA regulations.

### Senate Demands Repayment Formulas

Confronted with the escalating penalty balance, senators rejected broad promises of reform, demanding that each devolved government formulate a county-specific debt resolution framework.

The committee directed that top leadership from the National Treasury, the Council of Governors, and regulatory authorities convene at an upcoming round-table to settle the fate of the Ksh99.1 billion in accrued fines—a sum county revenue allocations cannot realistically absorb alongside basic service delivery.
