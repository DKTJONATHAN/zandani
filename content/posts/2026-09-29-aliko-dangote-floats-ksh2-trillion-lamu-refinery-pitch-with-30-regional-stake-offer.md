Nigerian billionaire Aliko Dangote is dangling a KSh2 trillion mega-refinery blueprint before East African capitals, laying out a multi-country ownership proposal while putting heavy host-nation conditions on Kenya.

The proposed coastal installation in Lamu County would see the Dangote Group open up about 30 percent of the project's equity to regional governments, creating an investment consortium across East Africa while securing industrial dominance along the Indian Ocean corridor.

Financial details disclosed by FCPA Hesbon Omollo indicate the refinery is projected to cost roughly KSh2 trillion or slightly more, positioning it among the single largest capital undertakings mooted for the Kenyan coast. Under the draft ownership structure, Kenya has been earmarked for a 10 percent equity slice, though formal terms have yet to be locked down by state negotiators.

“Dangote has offered to give about 30 percent of the stake to the countries within the East African region, Kenya taking up 10 percent was a proposal, not yet agreed upon,” Omollo said.

### The Host Country Demands

Beyond the headline valuation and regional share allocation, the industrial tycoon’s plan places substantial logistical and infrastructural obligations directly onto Nairobi.

To make the KSh2 trillion facility viable, Kenya would be required to allocate extensive tracts of land and build supporting infrastructure around the designated site. Crucially, the project requires direct, unfettered operational access to the multibillion-shilling Lamu Port to handle raw material discharge and product shipping.

“The project has a commitment that the host country as a government must be able to give in terms of the land and all the things, infrastructure around that place, access to the Lamu Port and all the rest around that space,” Omollo stated.

The strategic reliance on Lamu Port mirrors the maritime logistics supporting Dangote’s flagship operations on the West African coast. The deep-water berths at Lamu would serve as the primary entry point for supertankers bringing in heavy crude from global markets and ferrying processed fuels across regional distribution chains.

### The Lekki Blueprint and Import Realities

Omollo drew a direct parallel to the operational model at Dangote’s massive Lekki refinery complex in Lagos, Nigeria, cautioning that a Lamu facility would operate overwhelmingly on foreign crude rather than local extraction.

Despite Nigeria being a major oil-producing nation, the Lekki mega-refinery relies heavily on international supply lines to maintain continuous processing capacity.

“Even in Lekki, Nigeria only gives about 17 percent of crude oil to that project, the rest is imported, about 80-something percent,” Omollo noted.

That dynamic means any planned Lamu facility would not hinge on domestic Kenyan oil production, but would instead function as an international processing and refining hub reliant on long-term maritime import arrangements.

### Where the Negotiations Stand

While the pitch presents an opportunity for regional integration in fuel processing and energy distribution, the proposal remains entirely at the discussion stage.

No binding agreements have been signed regarding Kenya's proposed 10 percent equity take-up, the remaining 20 percent regional allocation, or the specific land concessions demanded by the conglomerate. State authorities and regional partners are left to weigh the KSh2 trillion pitch against the extensive sovereign commitments required to clear the path for the industrialist's latest African venture.
