When commercial photographer Mwangi Kirubi, known professionally as Mwarv, cleared security at State House Nairobi in May 2017, the assignment carried an immovable deadline. Campaign manifestos and nationwide billboards for the Jubilee Party’s re-election bid were idling at commercial print houses, waiting on a single deliverable: the official joint and individual portraits of President Uhuru Kenyatta and Deputy President William Ruto.

Yet inside the presidential seat, the technical reality diverged sharply from the prestige of the brief. Called in as an eleventh-hour substitute after the originally booked photographer became unavailable, Kirubi arrived on the grounds only to realize the studio setup demanded lighting apparatus and specialized equipment he did not possess.

Facing the prospect of sub-par delivery on an assignment of national political weight, Kirubi resisted the impulse to bluff his way through. Instead, he placed an immediate distress call to Allan Gichigi, a fellow Nairobi visual craftsman whose technical discipline he respected, bringing him in as co-lead.

### Clearing the Security Gate

![Photographer Allan Gichigi shows pictures to former President Uhuru Kenyatta and then-Deputy President William Ruto while directing a campaign portrait session, May 4, 2017.](https://i.ibb.co/dsDyGpp3/4a86ae15990a.webp)

The logistical friction started almost immediately. A congested presidential calendar wiped out their initial afternoon slot, pushing the entire production back by 48 hours. When Kirubi, Gichigi, and an assistant returned at 6:30 a.m. on May 4, 2017, they entered the sterile constraints of State House security, surrendering their personal mobile and electronic devices before rigging their gear in silence.

Time was running short. Official duties continued to delay the principals, but the print production timeline permitted no further postponements. 

When the leaders finally appeared, Ruto arrived first, stepping onto the mark as the initial test subject. Gichigi took direct charge of framing and lighting balance, managing bounce boards and softbox reflections to establish the baseline before Kenyatta joined the floor.

### Breaking the Protocol Chill

The immediate hazard inside the room was not lighting—it was posture. Kenya's formal presidential protocol exerts a natural chill on studio environments, threatening to yield rigid, synthetic expressions ill-suited for an electoral campaign anchored to the populist slogan *“Tuko Mbele Pamoja”* (We Are Together).

To puncture the institutional stiffness, Gichigi deployed a tactic that Kirubi later integrated into his own commercial repertoire: ambient sound.

![Photographer Mwangi Kirubi (Mwarv), former President Uhuru Kenyatta and then-Deputy President William Ruto pose during a campaign portrait session at State House, May 4, 2017.](https://i.ibb.co/HfHsFj8r/e24d4972d41e.webp)

With clearance from handlers, Gichigi piped classic Congolese rhumba and the unmistakable vocal arrangements of Miriam Makeba across the room. The auditory shift loosened the dynamic. As the rhythms filled the space, the guarded posture softened into natural rapport, enabling the crew to capture the candid smiles and shared laughter that ultimately defined Jubilee's 2017 visual identity across billboards, television graphics, and party literature.

### Technical Humility and the Visual Record

For Kirubi, the session became a masterclass in professional ego management. Stepping back to invite a peer who held the right gear and directorial instinct preserved the commission and protected his output from technical failure.

That collaborative ethic anchored Kirubi’s subsequent career evolution, culminating on the global stage when he claimed Gold in the Motor Sports category at the 2026 World Sports Photography Awards.

The resulting May 4 frames went on to anchor one of Kenya's most turbulent electoral chapters—anchoring the annulled August 8 poll, the tense interlude through the Supreme Court judgment, and the October 26 repeat election. Nearly a decade later, the portraits survive not just as artifacts of an era when the Kenyatta-Ruto partnership presented an unbroken front, but as a textbook case of how technical humility and quick improvisation resolved a high-stakes campaign squeeze.
