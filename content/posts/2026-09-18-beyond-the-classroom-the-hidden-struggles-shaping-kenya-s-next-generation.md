The Kenyan education sector is currently navigating a turbulent period, with stakeholders raising alarms over systemic challenges that threaten to derail the academic journey for millions of learners. From the corridors of high schools to the lecture halls of universities, the narrative is one of persistent friction between policy implementation and the practical realities on the ground.

Principals across the country are expressing deep-seated anxiety regarding a potential repeat of the textbook shortages that caused significant disruption at the start of the senior school term in January. The logistical strain is evident, with some institutions, such as Kagumo High, reporting an influx of as many as 6,668 excess textbooks, highlighting a glaring imbalance in resource distribution that continues to plague the sector.

The tension is not limited to resource allocation; it has spilled over into the labor market and administrative offices. Teachers have taken to the streets in a dramatic push for improved working conditions, citing staffing gaps that have persisted for years. Meanwhile, ECDE programme officers are reporting that they are being left behind, missing out on essential salary reviews, promotions, and pension benefits.

Higher education is facing its own set of hurdles. The Kenya Universities and Colleges Central Admissions Service (KUCCPS) has adopted a firm stance, withholding student lists until institutions clear their outstanding debts. This standoff, coupled with the fact that four in 10 university graduates now take more than a year to secure employment, paints a sobering picture for the country’s youth, who represent a vast pool of talent struggling to find a foothold in the economy.

The administrative machinery is also under scrutiny. A high-stakes legal battle is unfolding as a police boss seeks High Court intervention regarding the United States International University (USIU), with a judge certifying the matter as urgent. Simultaneously, the High Court has found that the initial recruitment process for certain education roles provided applicants with insufficient time to apply, further complicating the hiring landscape.

Technological and systemic failures are adding another layer of difficulty. The Kenya Education Management Information System (KEMIS) is currently facing failures that are exposing wider gaps in learner data, funding, and long-term education planning. These technical hurdles are compounded by questions surrounding stalled projects at two technical colleges in Kisii, leaving students and trainees in a state of uncertainty.

As the Ministry of Education launches probes into various incidents—including school fires that have left survivors bearing the weight of trauma—the sector remains under immense pressure. With union leaders pointing to conflicting views between the President and the Cabinet Secretary regarding education financing, the path forward remains fraught with industrial threats and administrative gridlock.

Ultimately, the performance of Kenyan learners, who have recently scored below global benchmarks in tested areas, serves as a wake-up call. As the country steps up dual training to make TVET graduates more job-ready, the focus is shifting toward the broader implications of literacy, which extend far beyond the attainment of certificates and the pressure of examinations.
