A proposal to criminalise foreign currency hoarding with up to 10-year prison sentences has run into immediate pushback from parliamentary finance overseers, setting up a debate over whether Kenya should roll back decades of free-market foreign exchange policy to curb election-year speculation.

Sponsoring MP Paul Abuor (Rongai) defended the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) Amendment Bill, 2026 before the National Assembly’s Finance Committee during a pre-publication scrutiny session on Thursday, October 1. Abuor argued that unregulated accumulation of US dollars and other hard currencies by private entities ahead of general elections creates artificial scarcity, driving up exchange costs across the economy.

![A photo depicting a person holding Kenya Shilling notes and US Dollar notes](https://i.ibb.co/h1LYmhbV/e71701b1054f.webp)

Under the proposed legal changes, individuals or corporate bodies found guilty of holding foreign exchange for speculative ends would face a fine of up to Ksh1 million, a prison term of up to 10 years, or both.

However, committee members expressed strong skepticism over reintroducing state-mandated restrictions on foreign currency holdings—a regime Kenya systematically dismantled in the 1990s to let supply and demand dictate currency valuations.

Kitui Rural MP David Mboni challenged the sponsor during the session, demanding to know the economic justification for reviving mechanisms that Parliament previously repealed to maintain an open investment environment. Mboni questioned the sudden motivation to enact controls once again when market forces had been deliberately elevated to regulate forex access.

![The National Assembly, during the National Land Commission (Amendment) Bill sitting in 2023](https://i.ibb.co/TMX6vZmp/f86b9205fcff.webp)

In response, Abuor insisted that while citizens and businesses retain the right to hold foreign currency, statutory guardrails are required to stop capital from being withheld from circulation during high-risk political windows. He cited recurring dollar squeezes during election cycles, where heightened demand and speculative holding strain national reserves and inflate import costs.

The debate touches on a delicate balance for the Central Bank of Kenya and fiscal authorities. Re-imposing penal sanctions on foreign currency management could alter how commercial banks, exporters, and private investors handle hard currency reserves, particularly if market participants perceive a return to rigid capital controls.

The Finance Committee will complete its pre-publication assessment before determining whether the bill proceeds to the floor of the House for its formal first reading.
