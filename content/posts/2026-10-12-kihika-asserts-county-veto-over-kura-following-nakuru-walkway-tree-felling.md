Governor Susan Kihika has declared an immediate freeze on unapproved tree felling by the Kenya Urban Roads Authority (KURA), asserting a county-level veto intended to safeguard Nakuru’s remaining urban canopy and insulate residents from unchecked infrastructure clearances.

The directive, issued on Sunday, October 11, establishes that national road contractors may no longer clear roadside greenery without explicit permits from the Nakuru County Government. The intervention followed sharp public backlash after mature Jacaranda trees were cut down along major city arteries, including Oginga Odinga Road, to pave the way for paved pedestrian footpaths and non-motorised transit corridors.

"It’s KURA doing those roads and not Nakuru County Government," Kihika said in an executive statement clarifying project responsibility. "They initially didn’t seek the approvals as required before felling the trees, but once we stepped in, we stopped it, and now it can only happen with the county’s approval."

The governor noted that while pedestrian upgrades remain part of broader urban enhancement plans, future clearances will face strict county vetting and will only be sanctioned where road widening cannot proceed without removing specific trees.

### Devolved Pushback Against National Agency Works

The dispute exposes the sharp friction between national infrastructure execution and devolved environmental oversight. KURA has been rolling out city enhancement initiatives across Nakuru to modernise pedestrian mobility through paved walkways and drainage lines. However, the decision to chop down established shade trees drew swift public indignation.

Civic frustration mounted over the weekend as residents questioned why urban walkability initiatives were executed at the expense of urban forestry. Some critics had initially directed their grievances at City Hall, questioning the administrative oversight and technical competence of the county's physical planning teams for allowing mature avenues to be stripped bare.

By publicly calling out KURA's failure to seek statutory local approvals, Kihika sought to decouple her administration from the contractor's operations while reasserting the county's authority over local public spaces.

### The Cost of Pedestrian Modernisation

Urban tree canopy management has become a volatile flashpoint across Kenyan cities, where infrastructure expansions frequently run counter to urban conservation policies. Pedestrians along Nakuru’s central corridors now face exposed footpaths during peak afternoon heat, turning what was designed as a civic upgrade into a source of public discontent.

The situation in Nakuru mirrors parallel environmental disputes across the country, where state agencies have advanced dual-carriageway and walkway projects without reconciling engineering designs with mature tree preservation.

KURA has yet to issue a formal counter-statement regarding Nakuru's directive. Under the terms set by Kihika, the agency must now submit revised engineering alignments to county authorities to justify any further tree removal before road machinery can resume work along affected city corridors.
