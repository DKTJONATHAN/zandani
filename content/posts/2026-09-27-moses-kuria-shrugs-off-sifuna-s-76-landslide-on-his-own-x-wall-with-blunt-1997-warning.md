Moses Kuria set out to test the political waters on his own timeline, only for his followers to hand him a lopsided blowout that completely upended the script.

When the former Cabinet Secretary launched an informal snapshot survey on his official X account on Friday, September 25, 2026, asking who would take the 2027 presidential contest, he drew more than 687,000 views and 37,353 votes. By the time the digital ballot closed, Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna had walked away with a crushing 76.3 percent of the tally right under the former minister’s nose.

President William Ruto trailed far behind in second place with 14.3 percent, while former Interior Cabinet Secretary Fred Matiang’i managed 4.8 percent. Wiper Democratic Movement leader Kalonzo Musyoka brought up the rear with just 4.6 percent.

Yet rather than treat the lopsided verdict as a sign of a surging anti-incumbent wave, Kuria responded in classic provocateur fashion. Instead of entertaining the idea of an opposition juggernaut coalescing behind Sifuna, Kuria pulled out Kenya’s electoral history playbook to declare the entire exercise meaningless in practice.

### The 'Hii Imeenda' 1997 Playbook

Writing off the prospect of any cohesive coalition forming against the incumbent administration, Kuria warned his audience that internet enthusiasm would crash against the rocks of political self-interest.

“Take this to the bank,” Kuria posted alongside the final survey tally. “The 2027 Presidential race will be exactly like the 1997 election. There will be nothing like opposition unity. The following will all be on the ballot. With 318 days left, nothing will change. Hii imeenda.”

Kuria’s historical reference pointed directly back to the December 1997 General Election, when late President Daniel arap Moi secured another five-year term by taking advantage of a thoroughly fractured opposition field. During that contest, heavyweights Mwai Kibaki, Raila Odinga, and Michael Wamalwa Kijana split regional voting blocs, allowing Moi to retain State House despite commanding only a minority share of the aggregate national vote.

By Kuria’s calculation, the very figures listed on his poll—Sifuna, Matiang’i, and Kalonzo—will end up competing against each other on the ballot rather than agreeing on a unified challenger to take on Ruto.

### Numbers on the Wall

While Kuria’s survey carries no scientific weight and reflects the specific demographics of Kenya’s hyper-engaged digital public, the sheer margins delivered a sharp snapshot of current online sentiment.

Sifuna’s dominant 76.3 percent capture demonstrated how effectively the ODM secretary-general has absorbed anti-establishment energy on social platforms, consolidating online enthusiasm that traditionally spread across established regional chiefs. 

Conversely, the low single-digit showings for both Matiang’i and Kalonzo highlighted the growing difficulty traditional political figures face when trying to generate organic traction among young, digitally native voters.

For Kuria, the online provocation fits squarely into the vocal commentator role he has cultivated since exiting government. After serving as Cabinet Secretary for Investments, Trade and Industry, and later steering the Public Service docket in President Ruto’s Cabinet, the former Gatundu South lawmaker has returned to his familiar habit of floating uncomfortable political theories directly into the public square.

Whether his 1997 prediction holds true or falls apart as parties finalize their pre-election pacts, Kuria made sure of one thing: even when his own poll handed the opposition an overwhelming digital trophy, he still walked away with the loudest talking point.
