Commercial currency desks attribute the Kenyan shilling’s short-term resilience to sustained foreign exchange inflows from non-governmental and charitable organizations, which are currently offering relief to general merchandise traders even as mounting petroleum import bills threaten to strain local dollar liquidity.

While commercial banks held shilling quotations steady between Ksh129.75 and Ksh129.95 per US dollar on Thursday—matching levels recorded the previous week—dealers report that the equilibrium faces an immediate test as bulk buyers, led by oil marketing companies, accelerate hard currency purchases.

The emerging demand follows substantial increases in the landed costs of imported refined petroleum, which are settled exclusively in US dollars before being factored into local consumer pricing structures.

### Petroleum Import Costs Drive Hard-Currency Demand

![A photo collage of Central Bank of Kenya governor Kamau Thugge and shilling notes and coins](https://i.ibb.co/7d8ppYRV/bf5d7f7bf03c.webp)

The Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA) confirmed that the import bills for liquid fuels expanded substantially between August and September, driving the pump price adjustments that took effect on October 15.

Regulatory filings show the average landed cost of imported super petrol rose 11.81 per cent, moving from USD874.26 (Ksh113,409) per cubic metre in August to USD977.55 (Ksh126,808) in September. Diesel shipments climbed 11.32 per cent from USD957.05 (Ksh124,149) to USD1,065.38 (Ksh138,201) per cubic metre, while kerosene jumped 9.41 per cent from USD1,003.87 (Ksh130,222) to USD1,098.31 (Ksh142,473) over the same period.

Because oil procurement requires marketers to convert large pools of local currency into dollars at prevailing commercial rates, these landed price surges automatically elevate the volume of shillings required to clear international orders.

Under EPRA’s latest monthly pricing cycle running through November 14, pump prices in Nairobi rose by Ksh12.93 per litre for super petrol, Ksh9.90 for diesel, and Ksh28.49 for kerosene.

“The trade of petroleum products in the international markets is denominated in United States Dollars (USD), and an exchange rate is applied to convert the USD to KShs during the computation of local pump prices,” the regulator noted in its pricing review.

### Central Bank Evaluates the Inflation Buffer

Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) Governor Kamau Thugge indicated that while escalated energy outlays present upward pressure on consumer prices, concurrent agricultural shifts may absorb part of the macroeconomic impact.

“Even if there is an increase in the fuel prices, the food prices have actually been trending downward, so one will still need to wait and see what happens to the food prices, and whether the decline in food prices offsets whatever increase there might be on fuel inflation,” Thugge told Reuters.

The monetary regulator’s calculation relies on falling staple food prices continuing to counterbalance energy inflation. However, should commercial dollar demand from energy firms exceed the inflows generated by charitable agencies and exporters, the resulting depreciation would raise landed costs across a wider basket of imported goods.

Beyond petroleum, enterprise sectors dependent on foreign manufacturing inputs—such as industrial raw materials, capital machinery, and electronic components—face increased procurement costs if the exchange rate slips past the Ksh130 threshold. Commercial margins and retail pricing across those sectors will largely depend on whether distributors choose to absorb the conversion differentials or pass the adjustments along to consumers entering the final quarter of the year.
