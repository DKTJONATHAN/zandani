Pastor Victor Kanyari has traced his most damaging online blunders back to the intoxicating lure of TikTok's digital gifting economy, admitting that chasing late-night cash warped his judgment and cemented a reputation he is now struggling to shake.

Speaking during an interview on Radio Maisha, the preacher pulled back the curtain on the period when he crowned himself the platform’s self-styled "president," revealing that the pressure to retain audience numbers and collect monetary gifts regularly overrode his discretion.

For months, Kanyari was a permanent fixture on midnight livestreams, drawing thousands of viewers with erratic commentary, unfiltered jokes, and volatile banter. While the sessions attracted heavy public mockery, they also yielded lucrative returns through platform tokens sent by late-night audiences.

"Kuna wakati nilikuwa the president of TikTok. Iyo wakati nilikuwa naongea vitu, ndo maana nilitoka TikTok," Kanyari conceded during the broadcast. "Nilikuwa na pesa sana nikiwa uko. Nilikuwa naingia saa sita usiku."

The financial windfall, however, came tethered to an insatiable algorithmic demand for spectacle. Kanyari acknowledged that staying commercially relevant on screen meant constantly pushing boundaries, a spiral that eventually led to remarks he now looks back on with remorse.

"Nilikuwa naingia saa sita usiku lakini sasa niko na views kama elfu kumi, lakini at the same time nimechoma kuchoma," he admitted, using Sheng shorthand to describe how thoroughly he burnt through his public goodwill.

### Chasing Algorithms at Midnight

Kanyari’s retrospective offers a rare, unvarnished glimpse into the transactional mechanics of Nairobi's livestream creator circuit. In that digital ecosystem, sensationalism is directly pegged to immediate cash transfers, and creators frequently escalate their behavior to sustain the flow of virtual roses, coins, and badges from anonymous supporters.

Yet the preacher voiced frustration over how social media audiences consume content, questioning why the antics that earned him money remain permanently etched in public memory while his attempts at philanthropy go unnoticed.

"Mimi nashangaa, I don't know hata saa hizi nitatrend tu mimi. Sijuagi ni nini nimesema na mbona mambo yangu hayatrend kama mambo yangu funny," he said, arguing that the public actively rewards his scandals while ignoring ordinary or positive moments.

That dynamic has left him in a self-inflicted bind: the very theatrics that filled his pockets during his midnight broadcasts now define his broader identity.

### Real-World Fallout at Home

The timing of Kanyari's remorse is not incidental. His social media conduct is facing sharp renewed scrutiny as he navigates delicate family developments involving the children he had with his late ex-wife, gospel musician Betty Bayo.

The ongoing custody discussions have become a subject of intense public discussion after the children openly signaled reluctance to move in with him. The standoff thrust his lifestyle, parenting capability, and online antics straight back into mainstream scrutiny, stripping away whatever comedic shield his livestreams once provided.

For Kanyari, the contrast between virtual virality and domestic reality has rarely been sharper. The late-night broadcasts earned him quick revenue and fleeting influence, but the digital footprint left behind has hardened into an intractable public perception.

While his current livestreams draw a fraction of the viewership he once commanded, Kanyari appears to be reckoning with the structural trap of internet fame: the money spent on late-night screens vanishes, but the controversy that generated it remains on the record.
