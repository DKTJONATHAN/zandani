When online critics descended on gospel music duo Wapendwa Muziki over their integration of artificial intelligence tools in studio work, comedian YY did not just defend the musicians—he turned the spotlight directly onto the audience calling them out.

In a video shared on his Instagram account on November 5, 2026, YY argued that the backlash directed at the gospel duo exposes widespread double standards, with many of the loudest commentators routinely leaning on generative AI tools like ChatGPT to navigate their own daily routines.

Addressing the ongoing debate over technology in music, YY questioned why artists are being singled out for adopting digital tools to streamline creative work when ordinary users routinely delegate basic tasks to conversational software.

“Lakini Wakenya mnajifanyanga sana. AI! Double standards. Double standards. Sasa inaonekana mnashambulia Wapendwa Muziki because they use AI kurahisisha kazi,” he said.

### Everyday Reliance on Digital Prompts

YY detailed how deeply generative tools have quieted into private Kenyan communications, contrasting creative production with everyday typing habits. He pointed out that many who criticize musicians for technological shortcuts rely on software to draft routine correspondence, commercial documents, and personal messages.

“Na wewe uko na ChatGPT. Mnajibu nazo mpaka message za wasichana. Hata email hamuwezi andika. Hata email tu ukiambiwa, ‘Ni aje, tutumie invoice’, unarudi kwa ChatGPT,” he noted.

The comedian brought humor to the critique by singling out romantic interactions, arguing that young men frequently rely on AI prompts to manage routine social exchanges.

“Good morning, tu hamuwezi kujibu. Good morning, kabla hujauliza ChatGPT, ‘How do I sound romantic? Please make me sound romantic and not desperate at the same time,’” he joked.

### Gatekeeping Studio Evolution

The public exchange highlights an ongoing conversation across Kenya’s creative scene regarding the boundaries of artificial intelligence in production. While skeptics argue that relying on algorithmic software risks diluting traditional musicianship, proponents argue that digital tools serve as efficient pre-production assets that save studio time and financial resources.

By highlighting the contrast between widespread individual reliance on language models and the gatekeeping of artist workflows, YY's commentary underscores an evolving digital landscape where AI tools are shifting from novel studio experiments into everyday infrastructure.
