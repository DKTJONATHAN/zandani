The engineering blueprint that brought West Africa’s largest oil processing facility to life is officially anchoring Kenya’s multibillion-shilling coastal energy bet.

Engineers India Limited (EIL), a state-owned enterprise operating under India's Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas, has secured a consultancy and management contract valued at more than Ksh58 billion to steer the development of Dangote Group's planned greenfield project in Lamu.

![Dangote Oil Refinery in Nigeria, Africa's largest, with an insert of Aliko Dangote.](https://i.ibb.co/bMhqNKPG/d92e154b2e85.webp)

Under the terms of the agreement, the firm will act as the principal Project Management Consultant alongside its Engineering, Procurement and Construction Management (EPCM) mandate. Its technical team will directly oversee project planning, site engineering, contractor coordination, and strict real-time monitoring of construction costs, timelines, and safety standards.

Crucially, the Ksh58 billion outlay is strictly ring-fenced for the specialized engineering and administrative consultancy. It sits apart from the overarching capital expenditure required to deliver the proposed 700,000-barrel-per-day refinery and petrochemical complex, a mega-development previously valued at upwards of $17 billion—surpassing Ksh2 trillion.

The appointment relies heavily on an established cross-continental working relationship. EIL previously steered the delivery of Dangote’s 650,000-barrel-per-day refinery and petrochemical complex at Lekki, which has since achieved commercial commissioning, alongside separate mandates to expand that Nigerian plant to 1.4 million barrels per day and construct a massive four-train fertiliser facility.

![Africa's richest man, Aliko Dangote, at his Nigerian Oil Refinery on June 21, 2025.](https://i.ibb.co/9HBq48H6/2525ce6184fe.webp)

Highlighting the continuity in a stock exchange filing, the Nigerian conglomerate noted that it has once again partnered with the Indian state firm based on its proven project execution track record.

For Kenya, the involvement of EIL introduces battle-tested institutional capacity to a project that aims to anchor East Africa's long-term fuel security. Once operational, the Lamu plant is designed to temper the region’s historical reliance on imported refined petroleum products while carving out export channels to global markets.

Yet the industrial undertaking moves forward alongside long-standing logistical hurdles. The proposed coastal processing node must eventually mesh with complex domestic infrastructure plans required to evacuate and transport crude oil reserves from the inland fields of Turkana down to the Indian Ocean port city—unresolved logistical bottlenecks that will ultimately test whether the refinery's massive processing capacity can be fed by domestic oil fields.
