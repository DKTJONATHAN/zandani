Nairobi Governor Johnson Sakaja’s executive order requiring written permits for the removal of mature trees faced immediate frontline defiance on Sunday, October 11, after road crews levelled decades-old street canopies along State House Road to benefit future multi-lane vehicular traffic at the direct expense of local pedestrian access.

The operation, executed with power saws and machetes, left the corridor strewn with tangled boughs and heavy trunks, severing vehicular passage and forcing families on foot to scramble over debris across the transit link.

While preliminary project briefs suggest the clearance aims to accommodate a planned dualling project, the physical devastation directly tests whether City Hall’s penal threats against state infrastructure contractors carry operational weight.

### The Civic Pushback

![Screengrabs of trees fallen along Statehouse Road on Sunday, October 11, as pedestrians struggle to pass through the tree debris.](https://i.ibb.co/Xrm8tR7c/954e7e146af3.webp)

The weekend clearing revived acute opposition from neighbourhood stakeholders and legal advocates who dispute the engineering justification for widening the quiet transit artery.

“Lower State House Road has the least traffic,” stated city advocate and Westlands parliamentary aspirant Nelson Havi, who challenged the strategic premise of the clearance. “What justification is there in felling 50-year-old ornamental trees to dual the road?”

Organised civic resistance against the dualling project dates back to June, when the Kilimani Community Foundation staged demonstrations against state plans to widen State House Road. The community association warned that expanding the carriageway would induce higher volumes of commuter congestion into residential feeder routes rather than solving mobility bottlenecks.

Environmental defenders highlighted the compounding risk of stripping Nairobi’s established tree canopies during worsening climatic volatility, arguing that roadside shade and urban microclimates are being dismantled from the city centre outwards without credible ecological offsets.

Counter-arguments raised in support of the clearance pointed to structural risks associated with aging, brittle trees ahead of heavy rains, citing past vehicle strikes caused by rotting timber.

### City Hall’s Standing Directives

![Screengrabs of trees fallen along Statehouse Road on Sunday, October 11, with a highlight of a man using a machete to slice through the tree stamps and branches.](https://i.ibb.co/Zz0DmVZz/227b515c6479.webp)

The Sunday logging directly collides with formal policy pronouncements issued by Governor Sakaja, who earlier placed state agencies—including the Kenya Urban Roads Authority, the Kenya National Highways Authority, and Kenya Power—on notice regarding arbitrary tree removal.

Under City Hall’s current environmental directives:

* All tree felling within the county requires explicit written authorization from the county Director of Environment.
* Unauthorized fellers face criminal arrest and financial sanctions.
* Approved removals must be met with mandatory multi-seedling replacements, an administrative requirement Sakaja himself conceded cannot easily substitute for half-century-old canopies.

Whether the teams clearing State House Road held statutory clearance from the county Directorate of Environment remains unverified by county authorities.

Neither the Ministry of Roads nor municipal transport planners have issued a public gazette notice detailing the contractor’s identity or the formal environmental impact assessment authorising the canopy removal on the State House corridor.
