Kenya’s Ambassador to Burundi, Henry Wambuma, issued a statement on Monday, September 7, 2026, in Bujumbura, assuring Kenyan expats of their safety. The assurance follows fears of retaliation after President William Ruto ordered undocumented foreign petty traders to shut down their Kenyan businesses by yesterday's deadline.

* President William Ruto on September 2 ordered all unlicensed foreign retail traders and street vendors in Kenya to close their shops by September 7.
* Ambassador Henry Wambuma released a statement in Bujumbura on September 7 urging Kenyans to obey all local laws to avoid reprisal.
* Trade Cabinet Secretary Lee Kinyanjui issued a clarifying brief on September 6, claiming the president’s directive was tied to the pending Local Content Bill 2025.
* The directive directly impacts Burundians operating small-scale kiosks, coffee stalls, and mitumba businesses across Nairobi estates.

The diplomatic scramble began after President Ruto's September 2 decree. He stated that hawking, running kiosks, and operating small retail shops are rights strictly reserved for Kenyan citizens. Foreigners operating without valid permits had until yesterday, Monday, to close down their operations.

This hardline stance triggered immediate panic in Bujumbura, where a large community of Kenyan professionals and traders live. Fearing tit-for-tat actions from the Burundian government, Kenyans sought answers from the embassy. Ambassador Wambuma spent Monday trying to cool tempers, saying the bilateral ties between the two East African Community partners remain strong.

Wambuma argued that the President's remarks were taken out of context. According to the envoy, Ruto was referencing the Local Content Bill 2025, a legislative draft currently in Parliament meant to restructure foreign investment rules. He urged Kenyans in Burundi to ensure their papers are pristine to avoid administrative friction.

Meanwhile, on the streets of Nairobi, the reality is tense. Many Burundians sell coffee and peanuts along Waiyaki Way or hawk mitumba near the Kasarani bypass. They now face the threat of arrests and deportations. Trade Cabinet Secretary Lee Kinyanjui stepped in on September 6, attempting to soften the executive order by clarifying that the state wants to organize, not expel, legitimate small-scale operators.

### Why it matters

If you are stuck in traffic near the Muthaiga drift on Thika Road, you have probably bought groundnuts from a Burundian hawker. This trade is petty cash to bureaucrats, but it is survival for hundreds of families. President Ruto’s sudden directive looks like easy populist politics. It targets vulnerable traders to please a restless local base complaining about the high cost of living.

But economic nationalism is a double-edged sword. Kenya likes to position itself as the dominant economic powerhouse of the East African Community. We cannot expect our neighbours to welcome Kenyan supermarket chains, banks, and logistics firms in Bujumbura while we chase their peanut vendors off the Westlands Exit. When Nairobi sneezes, the region catches a cold, but our own citizens abroad are the ones who will suffer the chills of retaliation. If we shut the door on small-scale regional traders, we lose the moral authority to demand open borders for our own corporate giants.
