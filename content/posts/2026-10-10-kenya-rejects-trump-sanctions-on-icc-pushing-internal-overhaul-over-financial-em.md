In responding to Washington's aggressive economic offensive against the International Criminal Court, Nairobi has staked out a delicate middle ground: validating complaints that the tribunal operates selectively, while flatly refusing to endorse unilateral penalties designed to cripple it.

Foreign Affairs Principal Secretary Korir Sing’Oei made Kenya’s official position clear on Saturday, October 10, hours after the Trump administration barred financial transactions with the Hague-based court. Rather than endorsing the financial freeze, Sing’Oei argued that external coercion bypasses the statutory mechanisms established to hold the tribunal accountable.

“The ICC deserves serious criticism for failing to live up to the mandate and expectations for which it was established and for being selective in its focus,” Sing’Oei said. “What it needs is urgent reform. Not sanctions. Kenya will continue to work with State Parties to reform the court.”

### The Mechanics of the US Freeze

![Foreign Affairs Principal Secretary Korir Sing’Oei speaking at the Women in Diplomacy Forum in June, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/kgPHmGBk/be938f80895d.webp)

The penalties announced on Friday, October 9, by U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio represent an unprecedented attempt to isolate the international judicial body from the global banking network. The measures block the court from conducting standard financial transactions and accessing commercial banking services necessary to maintain field offices and payroll operations.

Washington justified the executive action as a protective measure to shield its citizens and overseas operations from international prosecution, explicitly citing ongoing inquiries into alleged conduct in Afghanistan and warrant applications touching senior Israeli officials, including Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.

“This action should encourage our allies within the court to work for solutions to end this threat to US sovereignty forever,” Rubio said when detailing the decree.

However, the executive order incorporates an initial six-month grace period for select provisions. That buffer creates a tight diplomatic window for member states to negotiate terms before the court's core operational mechanisms face severe disruptions.

![The International Criminal Court offices in The Hague, Netherlands.](https://i.ibb.co/1f2TqJdx/049c22639179.webp)

### Nairobi’s Diplomatic Balancing Act

Sing’Oei’s intervention highlights Kenya's complex historical entanglement with The Hague. Having long voiced sharp criticism over the court’s focus on African states, Nairobi is sympathetic to arguments that the prosecutor’s docket reflects institutional bias. Yet Kenya is deliberately stopping short of backing a total financial siege that would destabilise multilateral treaties.

By framing the solution around the Assembly of States Parties rather than executive fiat from Washington, Kenya aligns its immediate diplomatic stance with traditional Western signatories—including the United Kingdom, Canada, France, Germany, Japan, Denmark, Italy, and the Netherlands—who have publicly urged the United States to retract the measures.

United Nations Secretary-General António Guterres underscored that wider multilateral unease, warning that targeting the court's financial solvency strikes a severe blow against the global framework for criminal accountability.

With the six-month grace period now running, Nairobi’s focus shifts to whether it can rally fellow member states behind a credible internal reform agenda before the full weight of the U.S. financial prohibition takes effect.
