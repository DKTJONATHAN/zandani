The Teachers Service Commission's bid to police teachers' private online commentary has hit a firm judicial wall, after the High Court ruled that administrative regulators cannot invent broad, subjective speech offences without specifying the exact conduct that warrants punishment.

In a Thursday, October 29 ruling declining to lift conservatory orders, the court determined that the commission's August 2026 circular left educators vulnerable to arbitrary discipline. By issuing blanket prohibitions against intimidation, cyberbullying, and undefined "offensive content," the commission had expanded its disciplinary discretion without providing teachers with clear legal boundaries on what constitutes an infraction.

The ruling leaves the contested circular frozen, effectively halting the commission's disciplinary machinery against online speech while the substantive constitutional petition proceeds to a full hearing.

### The Article 24 Threshold

At the center of the court's rejection was the commission’s failure to satisfy constitutional benchmarks governing the limitation of basic rights. Under Article 24 of the Constitution, any restriction on fundamental freedoms—including the right to free expression—must be provided for by law, pursue a clear objective, and remain strictly reasonable and justifiable in an open and democratic society.

The court observed that the August circular inverted this requirement. Instead of establishing narrow, measurable violations, the commission introduced sweeping terms that failed to notify public-school tutors of where their rights ended and where professional misconduct began.

"What is clear from the circular is that the same does not sufficiently define or particularise the conduct which may expose teachers to disciplinary action," the judge noted, cautioning that an administrative agency cannot rely on ambiguous phrasing to claim sweeping disciplinary oversight over its workforce.

Critically, the court pointed out that the commission already commands statutory mechanisms to penalize ethical and professional violations. Existing labour statutes and the established Code of Conduct and Ethics for Teachers provide procedural pathways to address professional misconduct without resorting to vague, catch-all policy edicts on personal social media use.

### Uncontested Participation Deficit

Beyond the constitutional vagueness of the circular, the commission’s defence fractured on procedural grounds. The petition had directly challenged the unilateral rollout of the directive, accusing the Upper Hill-based commission of bypassing mandatory public participation before publishing terms that affect hundreds of thousands of public servants.

![Acting Teachers Service Commission CEO Evaleen Mitei speaking at a past function.](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_style/public/images/media/tsc-ceo-mitei.jpg.webp?itok=yGy1lGNr)

During proceedings, the commission did not dispute its failure to consult teachers, their unions, or secondary education stakeholders before enforcing the rules. The court flagged this omission as a serious procedural defect under national values of governance, which mandate transparent stakeholder consultation whenever an organ of state formulates rules that infringe upon individual rights.

While the commission argued it had acted within its constitutional mandate to manage the teaching service and uphold professional discipline, the court ruled that statutory authority does not grant an employer immunity from constitutional procedure. Restoring the circular’s enforcement before the primary case is decided would render the educators' constitutional petition moot before their grievances are examined.

Rather than relying on administrative edicts, the court advised the commission to formulate precise, legally sound guidelines if it seeks to regulate digital conduct in line with modern workplace challenges.

### What Lies Ahead

With the interim stay extended, the commission remains barred from instituting disciplinary files, issuing show-cause letters, or summoning tutors over their online posts based on the August circular.

The standoff now moves toward the primary constitutional hearing, where the commission must defend both the procedural legality of its policymaking process and prove that its proposed speech curbs can survive the rigorous tests set out under Kenya's Bill of Rights.
