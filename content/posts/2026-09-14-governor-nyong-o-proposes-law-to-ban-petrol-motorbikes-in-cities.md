Kisumu Governor Anyang’ Nyong’o has announced plans to push for a national legal framework that will systematically phase out petrol-powered motorcycles in urban areas, a policy shift that stands to heavily benefit green energy investors and domestic electric vehicle assemblers.

### What we know
- Nyong’o is leveraging presidential backing in Kisumu to force a national conversation on transitioning the informal transport sector toward sustainable energy solutions.
- Environmental advocates and electric mobility startups gain immense regulatory momentum, positioning clean-tech infrastructure as the definitive future of urban Kenyan public transport.
- Millions of riders face severe capital hurdles unless government-backed financing models materialize to subsidize the costly shift from combustion engines to batteries.
- Urban planners and public health officials secure a powerful policy precedent aimed at drastically cutting down municipal noise levels and hazardous carbon emissions.

According to the county boss, who spoke during a high-profile meeting attended by President William Ruto on Sunday, September 13, the continued reliance on fossil-fuel two-wheelers poses an escalating public health and environmental crisis in rapidly growing cities. Nyong’o formally appealed to Parliament to draft and enact legislation restricting urban transport licenses strictly to electric alternatives, while simultaneously urging the state to introduce cushioning incentives for riders.

"I would plead that we pass a law allowing only electric motorbikes to operate. Because these petrol motorbikes, unless there are proper batteries, there is a lot of pollution in our cities, which is further a health crisis," Nyong'o stated, emphasizing the urgent need for targeted financial buffers for the operators.

The proposal arrives against the backdrop of an expanding e-mobility ecosystem in Kenya, where rising fuel costs have already pushed over 33,000 registered electric motorcycles onto local roads by early 2026, according to National Transport and Safety Authority data. While the vast informal *boda boda* sector continues to sustain hundreds of thousands of livelihoods through daily passenger transport and deliveries, analysts note that the proposed transition risks facing major logistical bottlenecks given the current scarcity of widespread charging and battery-swapping infrastructure across secondary cities.
