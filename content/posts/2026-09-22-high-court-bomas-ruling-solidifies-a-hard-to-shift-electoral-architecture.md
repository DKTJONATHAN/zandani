The High Court on Tuesday delivered a verdict that effectively freezes Kenya’s electoral structure in place, explicitly upholding the Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission's (IEBC) right to maintain the Bomas of Kenya as the definitive national tallying centre. 

By dismissing the petition filed by Busia Senator Okiya Omtatah, the bench has clarified that the 'national verification' stage is not merely a ceremonial finality, but a constitutional requirement that stands above constituency-level declarations. The court ruled that when physical documentation from polling stations conflicts with electronic transmissions, the paper trail at the primary level must prevail—but crucially, it is the IEBC’s central hub that holds the final authority to determine the winner.

This decision serves as a significant regulatory blow to a growing movement of legal and civil society activists, including former Chief Justice David Maraga, who have been lobbying to dismantle the central tallying apparatus entirely. The reformist camp has long argued that presidential results should be finalized and declared exclusively at the constituency level to eliminate the potential for human tampering or 'middle-man' interference at a central hub.

Senator Omtatah’s argument centered on the fear that the Bomas facility acts as an 'unlawful second layer' of verification—a bottleneck that introduces unnecessary administrative stages between the casting of votes and the announcement of the victor. He had sought a court order to force the IEBC chairperson to act merely as a conduit for results transmitted directly from the 290 constituencies, stripped of the power to perform a secondary, central tally.

However, the court’s judgment on September 22, 2026, framed centralized verification as a vital administrative safeguard. According to the bench, the process is essential for ensuring arithmetic accuracy before the formal declaration of a president-elect. The judiciary essentially ruled that the risk of centralized collation is outweighed by the need for a national-level audit of the constituency forms before the final outcome is cemented.

This ruling creates a clear divide in the future of Kenyan electoral reform. While activists under the People’s Coalition on Electoral Reforms continue to push for independent, publicly accessible constituency servers to ensure real-time transparency, the judiciary has signaled that such a move would require a fundamental constitutional shift rather than mere administrative adjustment.

For now, the Bomas of Kenya remains the final legal stop for the presidential vote. The ruling leaves reformists with a difficult path forward, as any move to make the constituency the final point of declaration would now require a formal change to the constitutional framework governing the IEBC, rather than a successful litigation effort in the High Court.
