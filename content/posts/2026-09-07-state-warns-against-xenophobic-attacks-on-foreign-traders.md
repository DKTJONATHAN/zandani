Government Spokesperson Charles Owino ordered security agencies on Monday morning, September 07, 2026, at 10:00 AM EAT in Nairobi, to arrest individuals harassing foreign informal traders. The directive followed violent incidents across Nairobi County after a state ban on foreign hawkers took effect today.

**What we know**
* Government Spokesperson Charles Owino directed police at 10:00 AM EAT to prosecute anyone extorting or assaulting foreign nationals.
* President William Ruto's informal trade directive under the Local Content Bill 2025 took effect on Monday, September 7, barring foreign hawkers.
* Burundian and Congolese traders queued across Nairobi at bus terminals and cyber cafes in areas like Muthurwa and Kangemi to verify documents.
* Foreign Affairs Principal Secretary Korir Sing'Oei pledged round-the-clock police patrols in Muthurwa, Kangemi, and Bahati after the Burundian government protested the mistreatment of its citizens.

Owino told journalists that security teams must shield all foreign nationals, whether fully documented or actively processing permits, from arbitrary arrest and vigilantism. He emphasized that criminal gangs and rogue local enforcement officers using the retail trade ban to rob traders will face prosecution. "Any element seeking to exploit recent directives to fuel xenophobic sentiments or mistreat our neighbours will face the full force of the law," Owino said.

The government's pushback follows chaotic scenes along country bus stations in downtown Nairobi, where hundreds of East African traders scrambled for transport home or rushed cyber cafes to file temporary residency passes. The crackdown stems from state regulations reserving micro-retail, open-air markets, and roadside hawking exclusively for Kenyan citizens. In response, authorities in Bujumbura lodged formal concerns after several Burundian fruit and second-hand clothes sellers reported beatings and stock confiscation.

Foreign Affairs Principal Secretary Korir Sing'Oei spent the morning meeting affected traders and embassy officials in Nairobi. Sing'Oei assured them of state protection and admitted that many regional traders had operated without proper documentation due to bureaucratic delays rather than criminal intent. He confirmed that specialized police units have deployed to estates with high populations of regional migrants, including Bahati and Kangemi, to deter eviction mobs.

### What it costs you

Watch the guy selling phone chargers at the Westlands roundabout or the woman pushing pineapples down Kangemi. When state enforcement acts as a blunt instrument, it does not create local wealth overnight; it creates an extortion racket. Local youth groups and rogue county askaris smell blood and move in to grab stock, while genuine cross-border supply chains freeze.

If you sit in traffic on Waiyaki Way, you pay for this breakdown directly. The cheap greens from across the border disappear, pushing up the evening price of a basic meal in estates like Uthiru and Kawangware. Landlords in Bahati lose rent when tenants pack into night buses headed for the border. Nairobi built its regional dominance on being open for business across East Africa. When city enforcement resembles a shakedown, Kenyan traders in Goma and Bujumbura become instant targets for reciprocal hostility. Trade protectionism looks clean on executive paper, but on the street, it arrives with a club and an empty stall.
