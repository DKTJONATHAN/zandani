International pressure on Kenya to loosen city building restrictions is running headlong into Nairobi’s acute infrastructure deficits and an unresolved legal challenge at the highest court in the land.

In its *Sub-Saharan Africa’s Urban Awakening Report*, published on October 29, 2026, the World Bank explicitly called on regional governments to scrap rigid caps on building heights and floor-area ratios. The lender argued that restrictive municipal codes force capital cities like Nairobi to sprawl outward, driving up transport costs and pushing workers farther from commercial centers.

Yet the prescription for vertical density arrives while Nairobi is struggling to manage the high-density construction already under way.

### The Multilateral Argument for Vertical Growth

The World Bank’s central thesis targets the administrative bottlenecks preventing private developers from building upward in designated urban corridors. By relaxing height limits and floor-area ratios—the mathematical formula determining total usable floor space relative to plot size—the lender contends cities can unlock affordable housing inventory close to employment hubs.

![Several apartment blocks located in Kilimani Nairobi](https://i.ibb.co/VW8D3TRc/22bdb9b6bed7.webp)

"Relaxing excessively stringent building height limits and floor area ratios that constrain vertical development of residential buildings by private developers, especially in locations targeted for integrated area-based urban development, can help increase a city’s overall supply of housing," the report noted.

The multilateral lender stopped short of proposing the removal of safety regulations, advocating instead for targeted deregulation in high-potential urban zones to curb spatial fragmentation.

### Local Limits and Existing Frameworks

Under Kenya’s Physical and Land Use Planning (Building) Regulations of 2021, private developers must adhere strictly to approved local zoning frameworks before breaking ground. In the capital, those parameters are outlined in Nairobi’s 2026 Development Control Policy.

That municipal policy established a ceiling of 75 floors for commercial construction within the Central Business District and Upper Hill. In suburban residential hubs and transit corridors—including Ngara, Eastleigh, Pangani, Milimani, Valley Road, and estates along Thika Road—the policy capped maximum heights at 20 floors.

![nairobi cbd](https://i.ibb.co/GQW4VzkT/ce16766f877b.webp)

While the World Bank views these caps as economic drags, Nairobi residents and neighborhood associations view them as vital, if imperfect, buffers against urban collapse.

### Infrastructure Strain and Legal Gridlock

Rapid vertical infill across residential neighborhoods like Kilimani, Kileleshwa, and Lavington has routinely outpaced municipal service delivery. Residents point to failing sewer networks, frequent water supply shortages, stormwater drainage backups during heavy rains, and severe traffic bottlenecks along narrow feeder roads built for single-family residences.

These systemic operational failures escalated into legal battles. Aggrieved residents filed suit challenging the adoption and enforcement of the 2026 Development Control Policy, arguing that county authorities approved high-density development without expanding basic utility infrastructure or conducting adequate public participation.

The litigation has moved through the judicial pipeline to the Supreme Court of Kenya, which has yet to issue a definitive ruling on the policy's legal standing and statutory enforcement.

Until the apex court resolves the constitutional status of Nairobi's zoning rules, any attempt to implement the World Bank’s recommendations will remain trapped between municipal ambition and judicial restraint.
