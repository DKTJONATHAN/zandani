Foreign visitors entering mainland Tanzania are now required to hold mandatory travel insurance, a policy shift that formalizes the country’s approach to inbound risk management. Under the newly gazetted Insurance (Inbound Travel Insurance) Regulations, 2026, travelers arriving by air, sea, or land must secure coverage that includes emergency medical treatment, evacuation, and luggage protection.

The policy, priced at Ksh5,700 ($44), offers a 92-day validity period and allows for multiple entries. Unlike the broader, more complex framework recently attempted in Kenya, Tanzania’s model centralizes the provision of this cover. The National Insurance Corporation (NIC) is positioned as the primary provider, either acting independently or through partnerships with other registered insurers. Travelers arriving without pre-purchased insurance face the risk of being denied entry at the border.

Crucially, the regulation carves out exemptions for citizens of the East African Community (EAC) and the Southern African Development Community (SADC), effectively insulating regional travelers from the new costs. This creates a distinct regulatory divide within the bloc, as Tanzania integrates this requirement into its mainland tourism infrastructure, mirroring a system already established in Zanzibar since late 2024.

The timing of the rollout highlights the diverging paths taken by regional neighbors. In July 2026, Kenya attempted to launch a comprehensive inbound health insurance scheme with specific benefit limits-including $20,000 for medical expenses and $25,000 for emergency transport. However, that initiative was halted in August by the High Court following legal challenges that questioned the implementation of the mandate.

By leveraging the state-backed NIC to manage the scheme, Tanzanian authorities are attempting to bypass the market-led friction that stalled Kenya’s policy. While Kenya’s framework remains trapped in litigation, Tanzania’s policy is already operational, signaling a shift toward state-controlled insurance mandates for non-regional visitors.

Whether this centralized model will face similar pushback from international travel operators or legal challenges remains to be seen. For now, the policy stands as a fixed entry requirement for any non-EAC or non-SADC visitor arriving in the country.
