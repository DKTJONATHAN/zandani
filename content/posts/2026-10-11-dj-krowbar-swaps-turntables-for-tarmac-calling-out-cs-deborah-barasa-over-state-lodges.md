Karumba Ngatia, widely celebrated across Kenya’s gospel music circuit as DJ Krowbar, traded his headphones and mixing console for a pair of walking shoes and a protest placard on the streets of Nairobi.

The veteran disc jockey stepped out from behind the decks to join frontline environmental defenders in a high-visibility demonstration through Nairobi’s Central Business District. Marching side-by-side with community forest protectors and Greenpeace Africa, Krowbar took his platform directly to the door of Environment, Climate Change, and Forestry Cabinet Secretary Deborah Barasa, calling her out over what he termed deep administrative silence as public water towers are whittled down for luxury projects.

For an entertainer known mostly for high-energy gospel sets and broadcast ministry, Krowbar’s appearance on the CBD tarmac marked an unmistakable pivot toward direct civic accountability.

### The Ministerial Callout

Standing with demonstrators outside the ministry headquarters after marching from Uhuru Park, Krowbar did not mince words about the state’s current development footprint across protected zones. He zeroed in on the ongoing encroachment of public reserves to create state lodges, airstrips, and exclusive recreation facilities.

“The CS Deborah Barasa has been extremely silent for the many forests being put down by the State for the sake of opening State Lodges, Golf Courses, and Airstrips,” Krowbar stated as the crowd rallied around the petition drop-off. “[We are here] to submit over 60,000 signatures petitioning for ‘HANDS OFF OUR FORESTS!’ FORESTS = WATER = LIFE.”

His intervention elevated a tense environmental campaign into mainstream public focus, giving celebrity weight to the growing outrage over state-sanctioned infrastructure drives carving out sensitive natural reserves.

### From Uhuru Park to the Ministry Steps

The Tuesday procession began at Uhuru Park, cutting through core arteries of the capital’s central district and momentarily snarling city traffic as civil society groups, community activists, and urban supporters pressed toward the Environment Ministry. 

At the heart of the march was a hefty legal and public statement: a petition bearing the names of more than 60,000 citizens demanding an immediate freeze on all ongoing development projects within protected forest reserves across the country. 

Greenpeace Africa, which coordinated the mobilization alongside local grassroots defenders, framed the protest around two prominent ecological battlegrounds: Upper Imenti in Meru and Nairobi’s own Ngong Road Forest. Both ecosystems have faced sustained pressure from commercial and state projects, drawing alarm from conservationists who warn that Nairobi and surrounding regions are trading vital water catchment basins for private leisure and infrastructure.

“How many water towers must be flattened for a golf course before the government acts?” Greenpeace Africa questioned in an official address during the rally. “Over 60,000 citizens refuse to watch sacred ecosystems in Upper Imenti and Ngong Road get carved up behind closed doors. No more quiet approvals. No more fake consultations. We demand permanent legal protection for Kenya’s forests, not luxury real estate!”

### The Demands on Barasa’s Desk

The demonstration leaves Cabinet Secretary Deborah Barasa facing an organized pushback backed by both grassroots legal activists and prominent cultural voices. The petition formally lodged at her office insists on clear, uncompromised statutory protections, calling for an immediate moratorium on any further carving up of state forests for luxury real estate and government facilities.

Beyond stopping bulldozers currently on site, the activists and Krowbar are demanding full institutional transparency, insisting that any future land-use determinations regarding public forests must undergo mandatory, open public participation rather than quiet administrative approvals.

With 60,000 verified signatures officially handed over and public figures refusing to look away, the pressure shifts squarely to Barasa’s ministry to provide a public accounting of its conservation policy.
