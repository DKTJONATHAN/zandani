Former President Uhuru Kenyatta’s office announced on Tuesday that he has accepted a High Court order requiring retired heads of state to surrender political party leadership within six months of leaving office, a move that hands control of the fractured Jubilee Party to rival internal factions and shields his statutory pension benefits from further legal challenge.

### What we know

* Uhuru Kenyatta’s concession formally ends his active command over Jubilee, handing internal challengers their clearest opening yet to claim the former ruling party.
* Adhering to the six-month statutory exit protects the fourth president’s lucrative pension package from impending legal petitions questioning his partisan engagements.
* The bench’s statutory enforcement draws a hard line between retired statecraft and partisan brawls, establishing a strict precedent for future departing commanders-in-chief.

In a formal statement issued by his office, Kenyatta framed the judgment not as an eviction from political relevance, but as compliance with established constitutional guardrails.

"The Office of the former President has taken note of the High Court's ruling upholding Section 6 of the Presidential Retirement Benefits Act, which guides the transition of retired presidents from formal political party leadership," the statement read.

"H.E. Uhuru Kenyatta has received this ruling in a spirit of profound respect for the judiciary. He notes that the court's ruling is not a restriction on his political life; it is a reaffirmation of our democratic maturity. The 4th President of the Republic of Kenya acknowledges that leadership is not the chair one occupies; it is the trust one cultivates," the office added.

The decision stems from a judicial interpretation of Section 6 of the Presidential Retirement Benefits Act, which mandates that a retired president must not hold office in any political party beyond six months after stepping down. Since vacating State House in 2022, Kenyatta has remained at the center of fierce ownership wrangles within Jubilee, countering sustained attempts by rebel lawmakers to seize the party’s National Executive Committee and realign the outfit with the ruling coalition.

Legal analysts note that Kenyatta’s concession primarily benefits the splinter factions seeking to formally purge his allies from the party register, while relieving the former head of state from endless litigation that threatened his public standing. By deferring to the bench rather than pursuing protracted appeals, Kenyatta detaches his broader political influence from administrative party machinery, leaving local rivals to wrestle over the remains of a severely depleted political apparatus.
