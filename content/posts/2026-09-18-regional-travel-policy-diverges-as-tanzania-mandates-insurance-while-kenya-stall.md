The landscape for foreign visitors moving across East African borders has become increasingly fragmented. While Tanzania has officially activated its mandatory inbound travel insurance for mainland arrivals, the policy’s implementation stands in stark contrast to the legal paralysis currently gripping a similar framework in Kenya.

Under the Insurance (Inbound Travel Insurance) Regulations, 2026, foreign visitors entering mainland Tanzania-whether by air, land, or sea-are now required to secure coverage priced at Ksh5,700 ($44). The policy, valid for 92 days and covering emergencies ranging from medical evacuation to lost luggage, is managed by the National Insurance Corporation (NIC) and its partners. For travelers, the operational reality is binary: those without the cover risk being denied entry at the border.

This move effectively bridges the gap between Tanzania’s mainland and Zanzibar, where a mandatory insurance scheme has been in place since October 2024. However, the regional consistency that such policies might suggest is undermined by the situation just across the border.

In Kenya, a comparable mandatory health insurance framework for non-citizens, which set specific, high-value benefit limits, was halted in its tracks last August. Justice Francis Rayola Olel of the High Court issued interim orders suspending the scheme following a petition, leaving the policy in a state of suspended animation. While Tanzania’s government notice has successfully moved from regulation to enforcement, Kenya’s initiative remains entangled in the judicial process.

For the East African Community (EAC) and Southern African Development Community (SADC), the impact of these new requirements is mitigated by broad exemptions. Citizens of these member states remain exempt from the insurance mandate, preserving a degree of regional mobility.

However, for international travelers, the divergence creates a complex administrative environment. In Tanzania, the policy is designed to be accessible at the point of entry, yet it requires a proactive shift in travel planning for those arriving without prior arrangements.

As Tanzania formalizes its insurance infrastructure, the silence from the Kenyan courts leaves a significant policy gap. The question remains whether the regional bloc will move toward a unified standard for inbound medical coverage or if visitors will continue to face a patchwork of requirements that vary significantly from one jurisdiction to the next.
