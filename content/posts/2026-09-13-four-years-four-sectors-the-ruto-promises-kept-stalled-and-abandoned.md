Exactly four years after President William Ruto took the oath of office at Kasarani on September 13, 2022, his administration's expansive policy blueprint finds itself suspended between tangible sectoral gains and an expanding register of postponed deadlines.

### What we know

* State House delivered massive payroll additions for primary and secondary classrooms, but the elusive pledge for universally free higher education has drifted into 2027.
* Flagship transport corridors like the Mau Summit highway face stretching timelines, turning early infrastructural promises into stubborn tests of administrative endurance.
* A promised September ground-breaking for the Nairobi-Thika expressway remains conspicuously unannounced, testing executive credibility on rapid infrastructure rollouts.
* With the administration crossing the four-year mark, unresolved commitments are steadily mutating from fast-track executive orders into protracted legislative negotiations.

The ledger of the Kenya Kwanza administration at the four-year milestone reveals a presidency that has moved aggressively on specific administrative targets while recalibrating some of its most consequential economic and social promises. The foundational pledges-slashing the cost of living, expanding formal employment, and overhauling national healthcare-remain under intense scrutiny as delivery dates begin to brush against the next electoral cycle.

Education offers perhaps the clearest demonstration of this split reality. Since taking over the instruments of power, President Ruto has overseen the recruitment of over 56,000 teachers, easing chronic staffing strains in primary and junior secondary institutions as former interns transitioned to permanent and pensionable terms. The government paired that drive with an expanded Ksh2 billion budget for teacher promotions, unlocking long-stalled career progressions for thousands of educators across the counties.

Yet the larger systemic promise-placing 35,000 new teachers on public payroll annually until deficits vanish-remains constrained by fiscal reality. More glaring is the trajectory of tertiary funding. The explicit campaign vow to guarantee 100 percent state financing for university, middle-level college, and Kenya Medical Training College (KMTC) students was set aside in practice, replaced by a contested funding model. State officials now point to January 2027 as the intended starting date for free university education, contingent on sweeping legal and structural adjustments that remain unpassed.

On infrastructure, the administration’s delivery mechanism shows visible friction between initial ambition and execution. Works on the 175-kilometre Rironi-Mau Summit highway are underway, with the first phase targeted for conclusion on October 30, 2026. However, the completion date for the entire corridor has quietly migrated to June 2027, well beyond the August 2026 deadline the Head of State laid out as recently as late 2025. With current progress hovering around 20 percent, the project underscores how complex public-private concession negotiations continue to dictate pace.

The broader presidential ambition to double Kenya’s paved road network within seven years similarly faces fiscal drag. In February 2026, President Ruto pledged directly to residents that he would return by September to launch construction of a 60-kilometre expressway connecting Thika to Nairobi, emulating the Jomo Kenyatta International Airport-Westlands viaduct. As mid-September arrives, transport authorities have yet to issue tenders or formal notices confirming the commencement of works.

What emerges after four full years is an executive that has mastered incremental operational fixes, particularly within established bureaucracies, but one that increasingly relies on pushing transformational targets deeper into the 2027 political calendar.
