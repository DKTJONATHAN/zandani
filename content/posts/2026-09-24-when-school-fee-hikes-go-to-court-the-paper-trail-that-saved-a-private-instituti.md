Private school autonomy to adjust tuition fees depends less on institutional prerogative and more on an unyielding paper trail of compliance, a recent High Court ruling shows.

When two parents filed a petition challenging a 6% fee increment for the 2026/2027 academic year, they did not just question the added financial weight. They tested the mechanics of school governance, arguing that the adjustment bypassed meaningful consultation through a legally recognized Parents Association under the Basic Education Act.

![An empty classroom](https://i.ibb.co/cMnP8PD/5c1902e682ce.webp)

Rather than issuing a blanket endorsement of unilateral fee setting, the court's decision hinged on the physical evidence presented by the institution. The school successfully demonstrated that the increase had been tabled and approved during an Annual General Meeting held in May, with learners and parents participating, and that formal notice of the revised charges had been dispatched roughly three months before implementation.

Faced with documentation showing adherence to procedural steps, the judge dismissed the petition, ruling that the institution retained the legal authority to revise its charges to offset rising operating costs—spanning staff remuneration, technology, maintenance, and learning resources—so long as proper channels were respected.

![Secondary School students sitting for their Kenya Certificate of Secondary Education (KCSE) examinations.](https://i.ibb.co/JWMfqJYn/43ecc01b32ba.webp)

The ruling underscores the shifting battleground between families grappling with a high cost of living and institutions seeking financial cushion. Similar friction has surfaced in recent months through challenges at other institutions including Parklands Baptist School and Alliance Girls’ High School, reflecting heightened parental scrutiny over institutional governance.

As schools look to balance their ledger books against escalating operational demands, the judiciary's position signals that the legality of any fee adjustment will be judged strictly on the verifiable minutes of AGMs and the timeliness of parent communications rather than contractual claims alone.
