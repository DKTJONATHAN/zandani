The chasm between regulatory performance floors and what Kenya Power actually delivers to consumers widened sharply over the 2025/26 financial year, culminating in a May collapse that exposed deep structural vulnerabilities in the grid.

According to the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority’s (EPRA) latest reliability audit, May 2026 stood out as the worst single month for power stability in the financial year. Customers endured an average of 4.98 interruptions during the month, while the System Average Interruption Duration Index (SAIDI) surged to 27.33 hours—meaning the cumulative blackout time exceeded a full 24-hour cycle on average.

![An engineer carries out maintenance on power lines.](https://i.ibb.co/HpFXWXLN/2da6667b9bb0.webp)

Individual outages were similarly punishing. The Customer Average Interruption Duration Index (CAIDI) for May hit 5.49 hours per incident, translating to more than five hours of darkness or dead machinery every time the supply failed.

These monthly spikes formed part of a deteriorating annual trend. Across the 12-month cycle, consumers averaged 13.16 hours of interruptions, a steep climb from the 9.42 hours recorded during the previous financial year. EPRA’s enforcement data shows these figures tower above the regulator's annual SAIDI target of 1.5 hours. Frequency targets fared no better: customers experienced an average of 3.91 interruptions monthly against a benchmark of 1.10.

The persistent failure to meet these performance thresholds carries heavy economic friction. For households, recurring blackouts disrupt remote work, online learning, water pumping, and cold storage. Small-scale enterprises—from salons and roadside workshops to neighborhood eateries—absorb direct revenue losses when machinery and point-of-sale systems grind to a halt. While commercial entities running backup generators maintain partial operations, doing so piles on unbudgeted fuel and maintenance overheads.

![Kenya Power token and an electricity meter](https://i.ibb.co/TqBZBY1M/24a46a5b4e94.webp)

Long-term tracking underscores that the utility's reliability deficit is compounding rather than correcting. EPRA records show the annual CAIDI worsened to 3.23 hours per interruption in 2025/26, up from 2.57 hours the prior year, marking consecutive annual breaches of the regulator's targets.

With annual metrics drifting further from statutory benchmarks and monthly failures intensifying, the figures lay bare an accountability gap between regulatory expectations and utility execution.
