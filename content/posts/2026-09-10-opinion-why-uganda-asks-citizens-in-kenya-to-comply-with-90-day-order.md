State House is asking the impossible of the street vendor, and the result will not be more tax revenue, but more hungry families on the streets of Nairobi.

Hussein Mohamed, the spokesperson at the House on the Hill, has issued a 90-day ultimatum for all foreign nationals to formalise their stay and their businesses. Uganda’s First Deputy Prime Minister Rebecca Kadaga is now playing the good soldier. She is telling her people to head to the High Commission on Riverside Drive to get their papers in order. She says it is about sovereignty and the rule of law. But look at the ground. Look at the reality of how trade actually happens in this city.

Walk past the bus stop at Roysambu as the sun sets. Look at the men and women selling roasted groundnuts near the Westlands roundabout. These traders are the backbone of the micro-economy. They do not trade in ledgers or corporate tax returns. They trade in small margins and mobile money transfers. To expect someone who makes 1,500 shillings a day to navigate the bureaucracy at Nyayo House is a cruel joke.

The government wants these people to obtain work permits, register for tax pins, and pay for municipal business licences that can hit 50,000 shillings a year. Where is that money meant to come from? A hawker selling second-hand garments in Gikomba or produce in Kawangware is barely scraping by. They are not tax evaders in the sense of a multinational corporation hiding profits in offshore accounts. They are survivors.

The East African Community talks a big game about free movement of labour and goods. We sign treaties in boardrooms and toast to regional integration. Yet, when the rubber meets the road, we treat our neighbours as a security threat rather than a commercial partner. When a Ugandan trader moves goods through Malaba, they face enough hurdles to break a man’s spirit. Now, they are being told that living and working here requires a level of administrative compliance that many Kenyans themselves struggle to maintain.

Who benefits from this crackdown? Certainly not the consumer. If the tea seller at the Ngara footbridge is chased away, the morning commute becomes just a little bit harder and a little bit more expensive. If the produce vendors in Kasarani close shop because they cannot afford a City Hall permit, prices for tomatoes and onions will climb, and someone living in a bedsitter on Jogoo Road will pay the price at the counter.

Kadaga says this is not about deportation. She says it is about moving into the formal bracket. That sounds noble in a press statement. It is dangerous in practice. Once the government sets this machine in motion, the enforcement agencies will not stop to check if a trader is trying their best. They will start with the easiest targets. They will demand bribes. They will seize stocks. They will push small-scale entrepreneurs back across the border or into the shadows where they will be even more vulnerable to exploitation.

Our immigration system at Nyayo House is not built for the small trader. It is a labyrinth designed for the elite or the well-funded corporate expatriate. To ask a person selling groundnuts to produce a valid work permit is to ask them to pack their bags. If the state wants to formalise the economy, they should start by simplifying the process. Lower the fees for the smallest traders. Create a fast-track, low-cost registration for EAC citizens that does not require a legal degree to understand.

Instead, we see a heavy-handed approach that prioritises control over productivity. We are creating a hostile environment that contradicts the very spirit of the regional bloc we claim to lead. Every shilling spent on lawyers and travel to Riverside Drive is a shilling taken away from a household. Every hour spent waiting in line at an immigration office is an hour lost to productive work.

This 90-day deadline is a countdown to disruption. By November, if the government pushes this as hard as the rhetoric suggests, we will see empty stalls and quieter streets. We will see the same local officials who ignored these traders for years suddenly appearing with badges and books of fines.

We should be encouraging the movement of people and goods across our borders. We should be making it easier for a trader from Mbale to sell their wares in Nairobi. That is how you build a market. That is how you grow a region. Crushing the informal sector to satisfy a demand for paperwork is not governance. It is just another way to make life harder for the people who keep this city running.

State House needs to rethink the implementation. If the goal is tax, make it easy to pay. If the goal is order, stop treating the informal trader as a criminal. If we keep tightening the screws, the only thing we will achieve is a smaller, poorer, and more expensive Nairobi. That is a tax we cannot afford to pay.
