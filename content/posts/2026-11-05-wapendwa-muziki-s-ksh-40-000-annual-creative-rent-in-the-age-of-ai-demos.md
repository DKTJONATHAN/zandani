For many independent artists, the path from a half-formed melody to a polished studio master is littered with expensive hourly rates and communication hurdles with producers. For Wapendwa Muziki, that path now begins with an annual subscription cost of KSh 40,000.

During a recent appearance on Obinna Show Live, Mesh Mpendwa pulled back the curtain on the duo’s creative engine, revealing that their reliance on the AI-powered platform Suno is a deliberate, paid-for business decision. While the music industry continues to debate the ethics of artificial intelligence in songwriting, the duo views the software not as a creative replacement, but as a sophisticated, high-speed drafting tool.

For Mesh, the KSh 40,000 yearly outlay is essentially an operational cost—a 'creative rent' that buys him the ability to record rough ideas on the go and hear them manifested as full-bodied demos almost immediately. The software, which synthesizes vocals, instrumentation, and arrangements from text prompts and melody hums, has transformed how they approach the initial stages of a song.

In the traditional studio environment, conveying a specific arrangement vision to a producer can be an exercise in trial and error, consuming significant time and resources. By utilizing Suno AI, the duo creates a concrete blueprint of their intended sound before stepping into a professional recording space. This shift suggests that for the couple, the most valuable commodity in their production process is not just studio time, but the time saved by having an algorithmic reference point to guide their collaborators.

This workflow pivot highlights a widening gap in the local music scene between creators who rely solely on conventional studio time and those integrating digital infrastructure into their business models. The revelation has surfaced a broader, often uncomfortable question for the industry: when an artist pays for an AI subscription to handle the heavy lifting of composition, where is the line between an efficient workflow and the dilution of the human touch?

Mesh’s demonstration during the live interview—where he recorded a sample and watched the platform render it into a musical arrangement—was a practical display of this transition. It moves the conversation beyond the abstract fear of AI replacing artists and into the tangible reality of how creators are choosing to spend their budgets to expedite their output.

As the duo continues to lean on this technology for their demos, the focus remains on whether audiences will distinguish between the algorithmic backbone of these tracks and the final, finalized versions. For now, Wapendwa Muziki is betting that the speed and clarity provided by their paid subscriptions are worth the investment.
