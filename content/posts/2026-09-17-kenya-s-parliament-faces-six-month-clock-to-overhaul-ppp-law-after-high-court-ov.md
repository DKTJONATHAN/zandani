A six-month legislative clock is now ticking for Kenyan lawmakers to establish strict parliamentary oversight mechanisms for state-backed private deals, following a decisive High Court ruling that struck down key provisions of the Public-Private Partnerships (PPP) Act.

The court determined that Sections 59, 60, and 72 of the PPP framework violate the constitution by allowing major infrastructure arrangements to bypass legislative review even when they expose taxpayers to long-term financial liabilities. However, to prevent immediate disruption to economic development, the bench suspended its declaration of unconstitutionality for six months, placing the responsibility directly on Parliament to amend the legislation.

The judicial constitutional check establishes a clear financial trigger for legislative intervention. Under the ruling, parliamentary approval becomes mandatory whenever a PPP arrangement requires the state to issue financial guarantees, deploy public funds, absorb private investor debts, borrow funds, or enter into contingent liability commitments.

The legal dispute originated from challenges against privately initiated proposals involving Jomo Kenyatta International Airport (JKIA) and the Kenya Electricity Transmission Company (KETRACO). Government defense teams argued that the eventual cancellation of those specific proposals rendered the petition academic. The court firmly rejected that defense, maintaining that the overarching statutory rules governing public exposure to private-sector debt raised unresolved constitutional questions that required clear judicial determination.

While the six-month window prevents an immediate freeze on ongoing developments, the judgment places a strict compliance deadline on the state's broader infrastructure pipeline. Major capital initiatives currently utilizing the PPP framework-including the ongoing expansion of the Rironi-Mau Summit highway, the planned Mau Summit-Malaba transit corridor, military residential housing programs, the smart driving license project, the Londiani Dam, and the Galana Kulalu Food Security Project-now operate under a temporary legal baseline while Parliament drafts statutory remedies.

The judges clarified that the ruling does not impose a blanket requirement for legislative approval on every minor joint project. Instead, the constitutional boundary applies strictly to deals that impose financial obligations on the public purse.

Over the next 180 days, lawmakers must construct a revised statutory framework that balances the state’s drive for private sector capital with constitutional mandates for fiscal transparency and parliamentary authority.
