Apostle James Maina Ng’ang’a took to his pulpit at Neno Evangelism Centre with a very specific grievance about the judicial fate of Paul Mackenzie: a 200-year prison sentence, in his view, gave the Shakahola cult leader an easy way out.

Addressing his congregation during a live broadcast in Nairobi, the 73-year-old televangelist dismissed the historic judgment handed down by High Court Judge Diana Kavedza-Mochache on Friday, November 6, 2026. Rather than celebrating the multi-century sentence, Ng’ang’a argued that the court had pulled its punches after Mackenzie’s conviction on 191 counts of murder involving 190 children and one adult.

“I heard that Mackenzie was sentenced to 200 years, haha,” Ng’ang’a told his congregants, punctuating his critique with his characteristic dry chuckle. “The judge should have considered the death penalty and she could have been done with that.”

### Calling Out the Pulpit Con

For Ng’ang’a, who has spent decades courting controversy for his unpredictable theatrics and aggressive pastoral tongue-lashings, Mackenzie’s operation crossed from eccentric ministry into calculated fraud. His sermon quickly pivoted to the theological absurdity of the Good News International Church doctrine, zeroing in on the physical double standard Mackenzie maintained while directing followers to starve themselves in the Kilifi County scrubland.

“Someone who tells people to fast but does not fast himself… Isn’t that using the name of Jesus?” Ng’ang’a asked the congregation. “Did Jesus say people should fast and die to go to heaven?”

The critique targeted the core contradiction exposed during the Shakahola exhumations, where state forensic teams recovered over 400 bodies from shallow graves while Mackenzie himself remained adequately nourished throughout his custody.

### Drawing the Prison Line

Beyond the theological rebuke, Ng’ang’a used the sermon to settle an uncomfortable public narrative. Faced with frequent public discourse that groups eccentric Nairobi preachers under the same broad umbrella of rogue religious outfits, the Neno Evangelism founder leaned into his own gritty pre-ordination biography to draw a sharp dividing line.

Ng’ang’a reminded the church of his extensive history within Kenya’s penal system, having served consecutive sentences across various correctional facilities between 1972 and 1992 prior to establishing his ministry. Joking about his intimate familiarity with prison muster rolls, porridge rations, and cellblock discipline, he framed Mackenzie’s impending incarceration not as an ideological martyrdom, but as a grueling reality the cult leader is ill-prepared to handle.

By framing his own criminal past as a finished, survived chapter and contrasting it with Mackenzie’s catastrophic body count, Ng’ang’a sought to permanently separate his flamboyant ministry brand from the lethal horrors uncovered in Coastal Kenya.

### High-Security Reality

Mackenzie’s 200-year term—structured as 100 years for murder by starvation and another 100 years for direct homicide—remains one of the most severe custodial sentences handed down by a Kenyan court. While his defense advocates have indicated they intend to lodge an appeal against Judge Kavedza-Mochache’s ruling, the state has already moved to ensure he receives no platform behind bars.

Interior Cabinet Secretary Kipchumba Murkomen confirmed that the correctional services department will keep Mackenzie under strict, isolated maximum-security custody, specifically designed to curtail in-prison radicalization and contact with general-population inmates.

Even with two centuries of prison time already on his sheet, Mackenzie's legal jeopardy is far from exhausted. The cult leader still faces an array of unresolved terrorism charges and ongoing prosecutorial files tied to hundreds of additional fatalities linked to his movement.
