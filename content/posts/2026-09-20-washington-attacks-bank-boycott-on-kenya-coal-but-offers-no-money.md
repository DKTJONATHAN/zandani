Washington has mounted a sharp diplomatic offensive against global development banks over their refusal to fund coal power in Kenya, but the critique arrives with plenty of ideological friction and zero financial backing.

Speaking at a Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) forum on U.S.-Africa energy cooperation on Friday, September 18, U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright took direct aim at the institutional lending boycott that permanently stalled coal generation along Kenya's coastline.

“They’ve been trying to build a coal-fired power plant in Mombasa, in Kenya for years. And no one, no international development bank, no commercial bank, no government would fund that,” Wright told the Washington audience, characterising the financing freeze as an arbitrary blockade on African self-determination.

“That’s nuts. And it’s wrong.”

![A photo of containers at the Port of Lamu](https://i.ibb.co/q3yvvyxg/2f92939ab083.webp)

Yet beneath the combative rhetoric signalling the Trump administration's pro-fossil posture, Wright offered no chequebook. The Energy Secretary stopped short of committing any United States government agency or export-credit institution to underwrite the stalled coastal coal concept. He named no alternative Western financier willing to absorb the high commercial risk, nor did he outline a structural lending mechanism to revive the project.

Instead, the remarks functioned as a broadside against multilateral governance rules that prevent African nations from tapping sovereign debt for carbon-heavy generation. Wright argued that African governments should possess the sovereign latitude to exploit whichever resource fits their baseline industrial needs—spanning coal, natural gas, oil processing, small modular nuclear reactors, geothermal steam, and utility solar.

While pointing to Kenya’s geothermal exploitation as an example of domestic energy capability, Wright's defence of a coastal coal grid arrives long after Kenya’s planning machinery quietly moved on.

The long-delayed plan for a coastal coal installation faced years of relentless public litigation, stringent local opposition, and the very capital flight Wright lamented. With commercial and development lenders closing their balance sheets to new coal assets globally, Kenya’s coastal energy strategy has pivoted towards liquefied natural gas (LNG) and industrial captive generation.

![U.S. President Donald Trump addressing the press during the signing of an Executive Order creating an anti-fraud task force to be led by Vice President JD Vance, March 16, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/wZ4YNh3B/95a9531ed8a5.webp)

That transition is currently centred in Lamu, where state officials are in discussions with Nigerian industrial conglomerate Dangote Industries over an integrated coastal power infrastructure. The negotiations seek to expand the capacity of a proposed power plant tied to Aliko Dangote's planned Lamu crude oil refinery to 1,000 megawatts.

Under that blueprint, the Lamu plant would not rely on imported coal, but on regional natural gas. Planners are evaluating supply arrangements with Tanzania, weighing a cross-border pipeline against marine LNG tankers discharging directly at the Port of Lamu's deep-water berths.

The resulting generation would meet the heavy operational load of the refinery while feeding surplus baseload into the national transmission network—anchoring an industrial corridor without relying on coal finance that global markets have abandoned.

Whether that LNG corridor comes to fruition remains tied to complex negotiations over off-take tariffs, bilateral pipeline treaties, and private capital structuring. But while Washington uses Kenya's stalled coal history to wage an ideological battle against multilateral development banks, Nairobi's planners are looking elsewhere to secure actual gigawatts.
