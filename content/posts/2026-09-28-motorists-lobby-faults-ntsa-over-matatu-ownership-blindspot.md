The Motorists Association of Kenya has confronted the National Transport and Safety Authority over what it terms a structural failure in road transport oversight, questioning how the state regulator continues to license, inspect, and penalise public service vehicles while claiming ignorance of who owns and finances the fleet.

In a statement issued on Monday, September 28, the lobby challenged the authority's public defence that the matatu industry remains ungovernable because investor identities and financial flows are concealed from the government.

The motorists' association argued that this stated information gap directly conflicts with NTSA's daily enforcement record, where the agency routinely identifies specific drivers, saccos, and vehicle owners whenever it chooses to enforce suspensions.

### The Opacity Defence

The dispute revisits disclosures made in June 2026 by NTSA Director General Nashon Kondiwa, who stated during a televised interview that the regulator could not fully enforce standards because the industry's ultimate beneficiaries and capital sources remain untraceable.

![NTSA mounts a multiagency roadblock along a major highway in Kenya](https://i.ibb.co/1tLFV3Y6/634e89e4da9f.webp)

"There is no industry where the regulator does not know the investors in the sector," Kondiwa said during the broadcast. "In the matatu industry, I don't know. That is the first problem. I don't know who the investors are or where the money comes from. I don't know whose interests they are running."

MAK rejected that assessment on Monday, questioning the core premise of NTSA's regulatory mandate if the agency cannot connect licensed vehicles to their underlying proprietors.

"If the regulator admits it does not know who owns the vehicles it regulates, who controls them or who should be held accountable, then the obvious question is: what exactly has NTSA been regulating all these years?" the lobby posed.

### Inconsistent Enforcement and Roadside Safety

According to the motorists' lobby, the authority applies its administrative reach unevenly across the public transport network. The association noted that while NTSA demonstrates full visibility over vehicle ownership records during specific crackdowns, it cites systemic data gaps whenever persistent violators escape sanctions.

"When other operators repeatedly violate safety rules, we are told the problem is that the Authority lacks clear data on ownership and accountability," MAK stated.

The lobby linked this enforcement disparity to recurring road safety hazards, warning that dangerous driving habits and aggressive manoeuvres by rogue commuter operators continue to trigger fatal crashes without decisive regulatory intervention.

![Matatus at a terminal in Nairobi in August 18, 2024.](https://i.ibb.co/7JC256Yg/8e0f1562b74a.webp)

MAK maintained that the agency cannot use structural opacity to excuse operational shortfalls, pointing out that NTSA commands substantial exchequer allocations specifically meant to maintain an integrated, verifiable transport registry.

"NTSA must not be allowed to explain away its failure to regulate the industry with all the huge taxpayer funding at its disposal," the lobby added.

### Expansion of Regulatory Mandate

The clash over basic vehicle tracking comes as Parliament prepares to expand NTSA's oversight through the National Transport and Safety Authority (Amendment) Bill, 2026.

The proposed legislation amends the principal Act to establish a statutory framework for public service vehicle conformity assessors. Under the proposed provisions, NTSA will be empowered to register, license, and regulate third-party inspectors responsible for verifying that passenger vehicle body structures meet national technical and safety standards.

Whether the authority can enforce these expanded technical standards depends on whether it closes its foundational registry gaps—establishing verified beneficial ownership before certifying new inspection regimes across the commercial fleet.
