A legislative push to overhaul Kenya’s higher education financing is facing stiff resistance over built-in debt recovery mechanics that force graduates to begin servicing student loans within 12 months of finishing school, regardless of whether they have found a job.

Under the Tertiary Education, Placement and Funding Bill, 2026, the government seeks to replace existing funding bodies—including the Higher Education Loans Board (HELB), the Universities Fund, and the TVET Funding Board—with a centralized Tertiary Education Funding Authority. However, draft provisions setting a 4 per cent annual interest rate, a KES 1,000 ledger fee, a strict one-year post-graduation repayment trigger, and payroll deductions of up to 25 per cent have drawn sharp pushback during Parliamentary public participation hearings in Western Kenya.

During sessions conducted by the National Assembly Departmental Committee on Education at Kibabii University in Bungoma and Alupe University in Busia, academic administrators and youth leaders warned that the proposed framework treats graduation, rather than income generation, as the threshold for financial recovery.

### The Post-Graduation Countdown

![University students accessing HELB services.](https://i.ibb.co/hJzx1s1V/71993c542bb6.webp)

The central point of friction is the Bill’s mandate requiring beneficiaries to start repaying their study loans within one year after completing their university or college programs. In a job market where formal employment often takes years to secure, university officials argue that a fixed timeline places unreasonable legal and financial pressure on young jobseekers.

Joseph Ogendo, Deputy Vice Chancellor for Academics and Student Affairs at Kibabii University, urged lawmakers to link loan recovery directly to employment status rather than academic completion.

“Repayment should begin within one year of being employed, including where there is proof of gainful employment,” Ogendo told the committee, arguing that completing a degree does not guarantee immediate earnings.

Ogendo also challenged a provision granting the new funding authority statutory powers to attach up to 25 per cent of a loanee’s monthly earnings once employed, describing the quarter-salary ceiling as excessively punitive. He proposed capping maximum deductions at 10 per cent to prevent severe wage distress for early-career workers.

### Interest Rates and Low-Income Reliance

![File Photo of University Students At the Anniversary Towers in Nairobi](https://i.ibb.co/hvhdcdB/15532858b314.webp)

At Alupe University, youth representatives focused on the cumulative financial burden created by applying commercial-style charges to social support loans. The Bill retains a 4 per cent annual interest rate on outstanding balances alongside administrative fees.

Harriet Opoma, speaking on behalf of youth groups in Busia, argued that charging interest undermines the core purpose of public education funding, which primarily targets households unable to pay tuition, housing, and living expenses upfront.

“The current loan interest of 4 per cent per annum on the outstanding balance, in addition to a KES 1,000 ledger fee, is detrimental,” Opoma stated, making the case for fully interest-free study credit.

### What Comes Next

The Departmental Committee on Education is gathering feedback across counties as Parliament evaluates the six education reform Bills currently in the legislative pipeline. While the committee has acknowledged calls from stakeholders to scrap the 4 per cent interest rate and soften recovery terms, any modifications will depend on the final report presented to the floor of the National Assembly before the proposed Tertiary Education Funding Authority can be established.
