When commercial crude begins flowing from Turkana's South Lokichar fields, its initial output of 20,000 barrels per day will meet only a fraction of the industrial appetite being built on the coast.

Even when the Turkana development scales up to its targeted second-phase ceiling of 50,000 barrels per day, that volume will represent less than eight percent of the capacity envisioned for a planned mega-refinery in Lamu.

![A photo impression of an oil refinery.](https://i.ibb.co/Y4yhwSnK/b736db0a6cb0.webp)

The infrastructural ambition on the coast, spearheaded by industrialist Aliko Dangote with a design capacity of up to 700,000 barrels per day, places an extraordinary spotlight on just how much crude Kenya and the wider region can realistically pull from the earth. According to an updated assessment by the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA), Kenya’s petroleum potential rests across four major sedimentary basins spanning 485,000 square kilometres.

Geographically, the vast Lamu Basin dominates the inventory, accounting for 261,000 square kilometres and holding 29 of the country's 50 total petroleum exploration blocks. Yet, despite holding more than half of the nation's exploration acreage, the Lamu Basin remains largely unproven in terms of commercial extraction compared to the Tertiary Rift.

Covering roughly 100,000 square kilometres, the Tertiary Rift hosts the country's most advanced petroleum province. It is here in Turkana County that the South Lokichar sub-basin has moved past speculative exploration, backed by a Field Development Plan for Blocks T6 and T7—encompassing the Ngamia, Amosing, Twiga, and Ekales fields—which received parliamentary ratification in February 2026.

![dangote-ruto-epra.jpg](https://i.ibb.co/v4PS1DMQ/33a861f8612a.webp)

Nationwide, exploratory efforts have yielded 95 drilled wells across the four regions, which include the Anza Basin at 81,000 square kilometres and the Mandera Basin at 43,000 square kilometres. While the government prepares data packages for a fresh licensing round to attract investors to open blocks, EPRA has stopped short of issuing a combined production target for the country's 50 blocks, underlining that exploration activity primarily generates geological data rather than immediate commercial output.

For now, the country's petroleum trajectory relies entirely on the December 1, 2026, first-oil target set for South Lokichar. As policymakers ready data packages for untapped blocks, the underlying math remains definitive: bridging the chasm between local extraction and continental-scale refining will require far more than exploratory potential.
