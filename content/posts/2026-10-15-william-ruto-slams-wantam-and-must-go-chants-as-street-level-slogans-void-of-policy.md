President William Ruto has trained his sights on the cultural lexicon of his political challengers, singling out viral catchphrases like “Wantam” as empty rhetoric designed to inflame ethnic divisions rather than offer concrete economic alternatives.

Stepping into the escalating verbal sparring ahead of the 2027 General Election, Ruto rejected the rising street-level chants during a public address, arguing that opposition messaging has devolved into reactionary name-calling. According to the president, catchphrases such as “Wantam” and “must go” lay bare an underlying shortage of substantive development blueprints among those seeking to unseat him.

“Our opponents only operate on slogans like Wantam and must go, revealing their lack of ideas to transform our country,” Ruto told gatherings as political alignments tighten nationwide.

The presidential rebuke zeroed in on what he termed a deliberate balkanization strategy by his rivals. He asserted that political competitors were deliberately mapping out communities along regional and ethnic lines—explicitly citing fault lines drawn across the Rift Valley and the Coast—to manufacture political grievances.

“Those trying to compete with me are severely misguided, as they have unfortunately become merchants of anger, hate, division, and exclusion,” Ruto said, warning that regional categorization has no structural place in national leadership.

The critique highlights how campaign vocabulary is rapidly hardening into weaponized slang across public platforms as political camps lay early groundwork for the ballot box. While opposition networks lean into catchy, easily repeatable street chants to galvanize crowds, State House is pushing back by casting those very linguistic choices as proof of policy hollow-points.

With the 2027 polls still months away, the battle for linguistic ownership of the public square remains a central theater in Kenya's shifting political landscape.
