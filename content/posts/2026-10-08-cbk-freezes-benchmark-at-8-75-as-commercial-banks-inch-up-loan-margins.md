The Central Bank of Kenya has anchored its benchmark lending rate at 8.75 percent for eight consecutive months, but retail borrowing costs are refusing to stay entirely flat.

While the Monetary Policy Committee kept the Central Bank Rate unchanged on Wednesday, October 7, commercial lenders nudged their average lending rates up to 14.4 percent in September from 14.3 percent in August. The marginal uptick underscores the stubborn transmission gap between monetary policy decisions at the CBK headquarters on Haile Selassie Avenue and the interest charged across commercial bank counters.

Yet the slight upward drift in credit costs has not dampened demand for financing. Private sector credit expanded by 10.6 percent in September, building on a 10.3 percent gain in August. The performance marks a sharp reversal from January 2025, when private sector credit contracted by 2.9 percent amid broader economic retrenchment.

### Appetite for Credit Expands

The rebound in commercial lending suggests corporate and retail borrowers are absorbing prevailing borrowing costs rather than delaying investments. Supporting this credit turnaround is an improving loan book across the banking sector.

![Central Bank of Kenya Headquarters along Haile Selassie Avenue, Nairobi CBD](https://i.ibb.co/DPW8C3Jp/45cdc7bfe901.webp)

Gross non-performing loans dropped to 13.9 percent in September, down from 14.8 percent in June. The contraction in bad debt provides commercial lenders with greater room to extend new financing without immediately having to price in punitive default cushions.

At the same time, foreign exchange stability has anchored bank planning. The Kenyan Shilling has held steady at roughly Ksh129 against the United States dollar since April, removing the foreign currency volatility that had previously forced lenders to price higher risk premiums into local currency assets.

The country’s foreign currency reserves stood at USD 14.7 billion—approximately Ksh1.9 trillion—providing an import cover cushion against sudden balance-of-payments shocks.

### The Food and Fuel Divide

The decision to leave the benchmark unchanged follows split movements within the consumer price index. Overall inflation edged up to around 4 percent in September, driven by stubborn retail cost spikes for staple kitchen commodities including cooking oil, milk, and wheat products, which have pressured household budgets over the past two months.

![Central Bank of Kenya Governor Kamau Thugge appears before the National Assembly’s Committee on Delegated Legislation, July 30, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/rKxjw9pJ/3fcdde9c4ff4.webp)

However, non-core inflation—which captures volatile food produce and household energy expenses—retreated from 14.7 percent to 14 percent. Easing market prices for fresh vegetables and lower domestic electricity and transport tariffs helped contain overall pressure, bolstered by state fertiliser distribution programmes that cushioned agricultural yields.

The MPC noted that headline inflation remains well within its target ceiling of 6.8 percent, giving policymakers room to hold rates steady rather than tightening financial conditions.

### Growth Path and External Vulnerabilities

The central bank expects the economy to expand by 5 percent this year, led by service industries and industrial activity, before accelerating to 5.3 percent in 2027. 

That projection relies on continued credit availability to the private sector and predictable domestic input costs. However, the committee noted that unresolved geopolitical friction in the Middle East poses recurring threats to global supply chains and fuel imports, while domestic exposure to weather anomalies like El Niño rains remains an active risk to agricultural output.

With commercial lending yields sitting nearly six percentage points above the central bank’s benchmark, the next test for the banking sector will be whether credit growth can sustain double-digit momentum if external supply pressures spill into domestic consumer prices before the year closes.
