A persistent departmental boundary between line ministries has effectively ring-fenced Kenya’s Ksh300 billion higher education financing engine away from public health trainees, stranding thousands of qualified candidates who cannot afford to take up their places.

Appearing before the National Assembly's National Government Constituencies Development Fund (NG-CDF) Committee, Kenya Medical Training College (KMTC) Chief Executive Officer Dr Kelly Oluoch revealed that approximately 20,000 students selected for the institution through the Kenya Universities and Colleges Central Placement Service (KUCCPS) fail to report during every intake cycle. The cause, Oluoch told lawmakers, is a cash shortfall driven by their complete exclusion from Higher Education Loans Board (HELB) funding and state scholarships.

While public university and technical vocational students under the Ministry of Education draw from central loan pools and bursary models, KMTC operates strictly under the Ministry of Health. That organizational division leaves its 89,586 trainees without statutory access to state higher education financing, despite the college producing over 85 percent of Kenya’s clinical workforce.

### The Cost of Statutory Isolation

![KMTC CEO Dr Kelly Oluoch speaking to students of Mathari campus on Tuesday February 10, 2026](https://i.ibb.co/0pYNK4Db/48160aad7059.webp)

The funding mismatch creates an operational deadlock across KMTC’s 98 campuses. In the March 2026 intake alone, KUCCPS advertised 21,774 vacancies across 36 specialized medical programs, with similar volumes slated for the September cycle. Yet when economically disadvantaged students receive placement letters, the absence of public loan options forces them to abandon their slots.

Because these placements are formally assigned through the central placement framework, non-reporting candidates inadvertently lock up public training capacity. Qualified applicants who might have financed their own studies or secured constituency-level support are turned away because the slots remain legally assigned to candidates who cannot afford to enroll.

When committee members pressed the college management on why a public institution handling national health manpower was omitted from government subsidy frameworks, Oluoch pushed back against proposals to transfer KMTC to the Ministry of Education to solve the budget crisis.

He argued that the college’s core curriculum relies on direct integration with public hospitals, clinical attachments, and specialized health professional regulatory bodies—ecosystems managed exclusively under the Ministry of Health.

"KMTC should not have to leave the Ministry of Health to get funded," Oluoch told the committee, pointing out that other specialized public institutions, including Bukura Agricultural College, the Kenya Water Institute, and Utalii College, similarly operate under their respective sector ministries without forfeiting their students' identity as post-secondary trainees.

### Misaligned Signals and Disrupted Intakes

The funding confusion has also exposed coordination gaps between state agencies. In January, placement officials signaled that government funds had been earmarked to support students joining KMTC through KUCCPS, encouraging candidates to select medical courses on the portal. In practice, however, no institutional framework exists to channel central student loans into Ministry of Health institutions.

Addressing the disconnect, Kitutu Masaba MP Clive Gisairo urged KUCCPS to implement explicit warnings on its application portal to inform prospective applicants that KMTC courses operate on a self-sponsored basis and carry no state financial backing.

To resolve the systemic barrier, KMTC management proposed a structural realignment of how student aid is governed. Oluoch recommended that the planned Tertiary Education Funding Authority (TEFA) be placed directly under the National Treasury rather than the Ministry of Education.

Under that model, state student financing would attach to the trainee based on citizenship and financial need, rather than the parent ministry overseeing their training institution.
