Five months after State House directed transport regulators to anchor minimum trip pricing into law, administrative inaction has left ride-hailing drivers absorbing both record retail pump costs and double-digit app commissions, triggering an ultimatum for a mandatory Ksh350 base fare.

The Organisation of Online Drivers (OOD) declared on Friday that its members will halt vehicle operations unless ride-hailing platforms immediately apply a baseline rate of Ksh350 for trips covering up to three kilometres. The demand follows a steep revision in petroleum retail caps announced mid-week by the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA), which pushed Nairobi pump prices past the Ksh226 mark.

Under the union’s proposed structure, the Ksh350 price floor is designed to apply to the lowest engine displacement tier on digital platforms—specifically compact vehicles with engine capacities of 650cc.

“The shortest trip shall be not less than Ksh350 for the smallest category, that is 650cc, and for a distance not exceeding 3 kilometres,” said OOD chairperson Justin Nyaga, outlining the terms required to avert a coordinated service withdrawal.

### The Commission Squeeze

![Fueling at a petrol station in Kenya.](https://i.ibb.co/Q2BpnXR/36f4cd94bb22.webp)

The dispute centers on an unresolved regulatory vacuum. In May, President William Ruto instructed the Ministry of Transport and the National Transport and Safety Authority (NTSA) to fast-track binding minimum fare guidelines for digital hailing applications. The executive directive was meant to settle recurring pricing disputes between drivers, fleet owners, and foreign-owned technology platforms.

Yet five months later, no binding statutory floor has been gazetted. That delay leaves app algorithms entirely in control of trip pricing, even as drivers foot the entire burden of escalated operating inputs.

Frida Gatwiri, an active digital taxi operator, noted that unchecked commission cuts combined with sudden fuel increases routinely wipe out daily operating margins before drivers can cover vehicle maintenance or loan obligations.

“You find a driver has been on the road the whole day, but by evening, because of commissions and high prices, they are left with nothing in their pockets,” Gatwiri said. “Companies are refusing to follow the President’s orders, and we are wondering what authority they have in this country. Are they above the law?”

### Double-Digit Pump Revisions

The immediate trigger for the labor dispute is EPRA's October 14 pricing schedule, which stripped away previous pump cushions. The regulator raised super petrol by Ksh12.93 per litre, diesel by Ksh9.90, and kerosene by Ksh28.49, drawing sharp rebuke from commercial motorists for executing the adjustments without stakeholder consultations.

![Vehicles belonging to taxi operators who met Deputy President William Ruto at his Karen residence on October 2, 2020.](https://i.ibb.co/NnVYVh7K/6670dd4f6416.webp)

In Nairobi, the changes raised super petrol to Ksh226.96 per litre, diesel to Ksh227.76, and kerosene to Ksh219.87. Because urban app taxis run primarily on super petrol, the Ksh12.93 single-cycle surge instantly escalated daily vehicle running expenses by hundreds of shillings per shift.

Union representatives argued that passing the pricing shock directly onto drivers while platform percentages remain fixed between 15 and 25 per cent renders short-haul trips economically unviable.

### Cascading Commuter Pressures

The ride-hailing standoff is part of a broader pricing revolt taking shape across Kenya’s commercial transit sector.

The Matatu Owners Association has formally advised its members to introduce an across-the-board 10 per cent fare increase on passenger service vehicles to offset diesel costs. Concurrently, the Motorists Association of Kenya (MAK) issued a notice of planned street demonstrations, demanding an immediate independent audit of EPRA’s price-setting formulas and a forensic review of the state's Government-to-Government (G-to-G) petroleum import framework.

While matatus can adjust street fares unilaterally, ride-hailing drivers remain tied to the dynamic algorithms of platform operators. With the Ministry of Transport and NTSA yet to convert the May executive directive into enforceable regulations, the push for a Ksh350 floor leaves drivers with only two immediate options: absorb mounting daily operating losses, or park their cars.
