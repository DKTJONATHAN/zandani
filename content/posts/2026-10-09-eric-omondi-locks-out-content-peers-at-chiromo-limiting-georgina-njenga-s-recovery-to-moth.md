Eric Omondi has drawn an uncompromising perimeter around Georgina Njenga’s hospital room, shutting out entertainment industry peers and digital associates who have been pressing for access to the creator’s bedside in Lavington.

Following complaints and public inquiries from third parties questioning why they were turned away from Chiromo Hospital Group, Omondi moved to shut down the social chatter, establishing that the facility’s secure ward is strictly off-limits to everyone except her mother and young daughter.

“Yes… Only the Mother and the Daughter can see her for now. Let’s give her Space and Time to recover,” Omondi clarified in an update directed at those scrutinizing the facility's stringent gatekeeping.

The hard line on visitation comes after a frantic search that began in late September, when Njenga shared distressing messages across her Instagram Stories that openly referenced her own death. The alarm escalated across Nairobi’s creator circles after circulating video clips captured the mother-of-one appearing disoriented and visibly troubled in public spaces.

For Omondi, who sounded a nationwide public alarm on October 1 to track her down, the memory of earlier entertainment tragedies dictated the urgency of the intervention. He explicitly tied her case to that of the late rapper Stoopid Boy, whose public decline played out on social feeds without an effective, decisive rescue.

“We watched Stoopid Boy deteriorate before our eyes. We will not lose another person,” Omondi stated during the height of the search, reiterating that Njenga’s primary responsibility was staying alive for the young daughter who depends on her.

That rescue culminated on October 2, when Omondi, coordinating closely with Njenga’s family and immediate friends, tracked down the creator and oversaw her admission into Chiromo Hospital Group’s Lavington branch for specialized inpatient mental health care. The facility subsequently intervened on a humanitarian basis, waiving her treatment costs to eliminate financial friction for the family while she undergoes stabilization.

Njenga is now enrolled in a structured 90-day clinical recovery program. The three-month regimen is designed to strip away the relentless scrutiny of the digital creator economy, substituting constant online performance with structured psychiatric care, therapeutic counseling, and sustained rest.

While early progress markers documented by Omondi show Njenga’s spirits steadily improving, both the comedian and her immediate family view digital peer visits as a direct threat to that fragile stabilization. The initial stages of clinical inpatient therapy are physically and emotionally grueling, requiring an insulated environment where external social dynamics cannot intrude.

By narrowing her contact list down to two individuals—her mother and her child—the care team is betting on fundamental kinship over industry camaraderie. As outside commentary persists regarding her treatment conditions, the directive from inside the Lavington facility remains unchanged: the door stays locked to the public, leaving Njenga to complete her 90 days in deliberate, uninterrupted quiet.
