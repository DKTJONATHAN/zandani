Oga Obinna has drawn a hard boundary between his personal identity as an entertainment host and the professional standards of journalism, pushing back against veteran investigative reporter John Allan Namu’s warnings over undisclosed political cash in Kenya’s creator economy.

Refusing to be judged by newsroom ethics, the digital presenter declared that hosting interviews on camera does not make a content creator a journalist. Instead, Obinna positioned himself strictly as a comedy talent and media entrepreneur running a production company—one that places qualified professional journalists on its payroll to manage actual reporting tasks while he focuses on entertainment, music, and scripted performance.

“I’m not a journalist. I do have my company. It’s a media company that focuses on just productions,” Obinna stated, insisting his digital assets operate under a commercial entertainment mandate rather than a public interest watchdog role.

The pushback follows concerns raised by Namu, who publicly questioned the independence of podcasters and online hosts accepting money from political figures. Namu warned that blurring the lines between digital production and political patronage compromises creator credibility, leaving audiences unable to distinguish genuine commentary from paid promotion and creating a perception that digital platforms are available to the highest bidder.

Defending his revenue strategy, Obinna argued that mainstream journalism requires rigorous research, investigative verification, fact-checking, and public accountability—burdens he believes should not be placed on entertainment-first channels built to motivate and amuse viewers. By separating his role as the face of the brand from the trained newsroom staff he employs behind the scenes, Obinna maintains that comedy platforms should remain exempt from traditional editorial disclosures.

The debate highlights a growing friction point across Kenya's digital entertainment space. As high-reach podcasters command massive online audiences and lucrative political bookings, traditional journalists continue to call for clear financial disclosures. For Obinna, maintaining that separation is essential to protecting his platform's business model; for the industry, it leaves open the unresolved question of where digital showmanship ends and paid political influence begins.
