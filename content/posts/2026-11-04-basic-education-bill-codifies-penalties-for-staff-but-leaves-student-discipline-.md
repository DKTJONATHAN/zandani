Two decades after Kenya criminalized corporal punishment in learning institutions, the state’s latest legislative overhaul of the sector has exposed a persistent statutory void: clear, enforceable legal mechanisms for disciplining violent students remain unwritten in primary law.

While Clause 19 of the proposed Basic Education Bill, 2026 explicitly reinforces protections for learners—backed by existing statutory penalties of up to Ksh 100,000 in fines and six-month prison terms for offending educators—lawmakers contend the framework leaves school administrators legally exposed when managing severe student misconduct.

During a parliamentary stakeholder engagement on seven education reform bills on Tuesday, November 3, National Assembly Education Committee Chairperson Julius Melly warned that the draft legislation codifies student protections without establishing parallel statutory protocols for school heads.

### A One-Sided Legal Shield

![Tinderet Member of Parliament Julius Melly chairs the National Assembly Departmental Committee on Education during a session with the Teachers Service Commission, May 13, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/pjqSNTWq/8941b392accc.webp)

Under Clause 19, subsections 2 and 3, the proposed bill prohibits subjecting any learner to corporal punishment, torture, or cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment, whether physical or psychological. However, the clause contains no direct statutory tools empowering school heads to address violent offenses, ranging from physical assaults on teaching staff to incidents of arson targeting fellow learners.

Melly argued that this imbalance has created operational paralysis in secondary institutions, leaving teachers vulnerable to physical confrontation without established legal remedies.

"Schools are now ungovernable. Teachers are very unsafe since students can wake up and manhandle a teacher, and they cannot do anything since the law protects the student," Melly told Ministry officials, adding that school administrators require explicit operational guidelines to maintain order.

The concern was echoed by Council of Legal Education Chairperson Collins Odote, who told lawmakers that existing disciplinary mechanisms have broken down in practice, making structural clarity an urgent legal necessity.

![Secondary School students sitting for their Kenya Certificate of Secondary Education (KCSE) examinations.](https://i.ibb.co/4wPj4vZ6/cfd0ee0531a1.webp)

### Shift to Executive Regulation

Responding to the committee, Education Principal Secretary John Ololtuaa acknowledged the gap, stating that the Ministry plans to rely on executive regulations issued by the Education Cabinet Secretary to define how schools will handle disciplinary cases.

This approach shifts the responsibility of defining discipline from primary legislation to subordinate ministerial rules. While the Basic Education Bill, 2026 sets strict legal boundaries prohibiting physical and psychological abuse, the specific tools available to teachers—such as formal suspension protocols, referral pathways, or behavioral intervention frameworks—will depend entirely on rules drafted by the executive after the law passes.

The debate highlights a long-standing governance challenge since the formal ban on physical chastisement in 2001. While physical punishment carries clear criminal liabilities for staff under Kenyan law, alternative administrative frameworks have remained largely discretionary across public schools.

As the National Assembly Education Committee scrutinizes the reform package, lawmakers are insisting that the Ministry of Education draft and present the proposed disciplinary regulations before the Basic Education Bill moves to its next stage on the parliamentary floor.
