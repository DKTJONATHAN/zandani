Eko Dydda has turned the mirror straight back onto his critics, arguing that Kenyans who hide behind bold internet pseudonyms have little moral authority to police the ambitious name he chose for his newborn child.

The gospel artist sparked widespread commentary across digital channels after revealing that his daughter, born on November 1, 2026, had been named 5 Star Billions Increase Dydda. When the public balked at the title, Dydda took to his Instagram page on November 6 to dismantle the criticism by examining how Kenyans handle identity in public spaces.

"All Kenyans online have changed their names. Nobody uses their ID names on social media because they hate them," Dydda stated, highlighting the irony of anonymous users scrutinizing legal birth choices.

### Ambition on Paper, Caution in Parenting

![Eko Dydda// Instagram](https://i.ibb.co/7JsxyHVq/8799f56eb0e4.webp)

For Dydda, the pushback against non-traditional naming reflects an underlying anxiety about personal potential. He contended that society has grown comfortable bestowing bold, expansive labels onto corporate entities while deliberately limiting the linguistic horizons of human beings.

"What I came to realise about the names is people are more afraid of calling their kids great names in fear of their failure, so they settle for normal names," he wrote. "They can call their businesses great names but not their children."

The musician insisted that spoken names operate as declarations of intent rather than mere administrative markers. In his view, defaulting to conventional choices stems directly from a defensive posture against possible future shortcomings, a compromise he refuses to adopt in his household.

### A New Dynamic at Home

![Bahati’s KSh1 Million Wedding Turns Out to Be a Publicity Stunt](https://i.ibb.co/ZRLHVY9S/27a8d2407ea4.webp)

The arrival of 5 Star Billions Increase marks a distinct personal chapter for the recording artist, who admitted that navigating fatherhood with a daughter requires an entirely different emotional vocabulary.

Alongside the public statement, Dydda revealed that he had already composed and produced a dedicated musical record for her, treating the release as an initial step toward mastering the new domestic role.

"I had to make a song for my little girl. I know I will learn how to be a father to a baby girl through her," he said.

While public debate continues over where parental expressive freedom ends and civil naming norms begin, Dydda remains firm on the registry entry, maintaining that spoken names carry intrinsic authority that should never be tailored to satisfy online commentary.
