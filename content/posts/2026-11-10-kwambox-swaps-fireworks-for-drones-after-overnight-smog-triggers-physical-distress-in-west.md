Sheila Kwamboka was not long into her arrival in Westlands when the physical toll of overnight celebrations hit her. Following four to five consecutive hours of pyrotechnic activity across Nairobi’s commercial heartlands over the Diwali weekend, the radio presenter encountered a thick atmospheric haze that immediately triggered chest heaviness and persistent sneezing.

The immediate respiratory reaction prompted Kwambox to issue a direct public health appeal regarding the capital’s deteriorating air quality, arguing that heavy urban pyrotechnics are compounding pre-existing environmental pressures in densely built neighbourhoods.

Rather than dismissing the incident as a brief seasonal nuisance, Kwambox framed the heavy smog in Westlands and Parklands as an urgent urban habitability issue. She pointed out that suspended particulate matter from prolonged fireworks does not dissipate quickly in areas where active real estate construction projects and sparse tree cover already strain atmospheric clarity. Across Westlands, pedestrians were seen turning to protective face masks to navigate the lingering dust and airborne residues.

![Sheila Kwambox // Instagram](https://i.ibb.co/PGyNrwZb/f1bd529f6e76.webp)

"Last night was Diwali celebrations, and fireworks were going on for about 3 to 4 hours straight," Kwambox stated in a video addressing city residents. "There is a thickness in the air. The air is so dirty."

Anticipating potential pushback around cultural sensitivity, the media personality established clear boundaries regarding her commentary. Kwambox emphasized that her intervention was strictly anchored in environmental safety and public health standards rather than festive traditions, pointing out that urban spaces require practical management to protect residents' baseline health.

"This is not attacking anybody's religion," she clarified. "I think there's better ways we can do this. I'm not trying to mess anybody's business. I'm just trying to stay alive, and I think everybody in Nairobi deserves fresh air."

The incident marks a continuation of Kwambox's ongoing public campaign regarding urban air safety. Earlier in the year, during a nationwide radio tour, she raised concerns over unrestricted pyrotechnic use in residential and mixed-use commercial districts, calling for tighter spatial governance over heavy smoke sources.

Her latest intervention highlights the broader environmental stakes for a city experiencing rapid commercial growth. Standard environmental health metrics place a healthy baseline Air Quality Index (AQI) around the 20 mark. In contrast, megacities experiencing extreme seasonal smog, such as New Delhi, regularly record baseline AQI levels between 300 and 400—with intense Diwali pyrotechnics pushing peak spikes to 3,000.

While Nairobi has not approached those extreme metrics, current municipal policy requires commercial pyrotechnic organizers to secure county permits before detonating fireworks in residential or commercial precincts. Nairobi County enforcement officers are periodically tasked with monitoring noise levels and environmental compliance during high-density celebrations.

To bridge public celebration with environmental safety, Kwambox proposed a practical technological shift: transitioning from heavy chemical pyrotechnics to modern synchronized drone light displays. The alternative, she argued, would preserve visual spectacle without injecting heavy airborne particulate matter into high-density commercial corridors.

Kwambox called on urban planners, local administration, and city residents to open a wider dialogue on tracking and managing Nairobi’s changing air composition. "We need to have a conversation about the changing air quality in Nairobi," she urged.
