Pastor Victor Kanyari found himself wrestling with the unglamorous side of philanthropy when a routine check on his Kasarani community fleet revealed a drying fuel tank and a yellow jerrican sitting beside his charity bus.

The confrontation, captured on video as workers and onlookers gathered around the vehicle's open fuel port, laid bare the day-to-day friction of managing community transport initiatives. Kanyari made no secret of his frustration, questioning the arithmetic of an employee earning a monthly salary of KSh 30,000 risking their livelihood over fuel meant to keep community outreach rolling.

“Mshahara ya 30,000 ni njaa gani unaskia? Si ako kazi? Zingine ni tamaa,” Kanyari remarked during the roadside showdown, pointing directly to workplace greed as the culprit behind the unusually fast depletion of fuel reserves.

The vehicle sits at the center of an outreach programme launched in July 2026, when the preacher rolled out a fleet under his foundation designed to offer free transport for Kasarani residents facing emergencies like illnesses and funerals, alongside community activities such as transporting local football teams. Under the initiative's stated rules, beneficiaries are not required to cover transport or fuel costs.

However, the mechanics of keeping such a free-service vehicle operational proved vulnerable. Kanyari noted that his suspicions were first aroused when newly refilled tanks emptied at an abnormal rate, prompting him to track down the alleged siphoning operation. While he accused his driver of directing the fuel to outside buyers, he adopted a softer stance toward those who allegedly purchased it, suggesting they may have been unaware of the fuel's origin.

As the confrontation highlighted the strain placed on community transport projects by internal misconduct, the incident leaves open questions regarding formal administrative action against the accused staff members and the ongoing security of the Kasarani foundation's remaining fleet.
