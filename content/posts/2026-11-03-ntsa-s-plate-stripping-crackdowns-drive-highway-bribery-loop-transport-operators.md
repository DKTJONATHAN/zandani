The National Transport and Safety Authority's practice of stripping registration plates from public service and commercial vehicles during highway crackdowns is creating a friction point that favors roadside bribery over long-term road safety, commercial transport leaders have warned.

While multi-agency teams escalate day and night operations along major transit corridors, transport operators argue that targeting physical vehicles rather than holding reckless drivers individually accountable distorts compliance. The enforcement gap comes into sharp focus as state agencies attempt to curb rising road casualties ahead of the December holiday travel peak.

### The Mechanics of Vehicle Confiscation

Speaking on Tuesday, November 3, Mass Mobility Operators Association leader Wilfred Bosire challenged NTSA’s operational reliance on plate removal for unroadworthy or non-compliant vehicles. He pointed out that officers routinely detach registration plates even from newly acquired vehicles with no visible mechanical faults, shifting the punitive burden onto vehicle owners while leaving driver behavior unaddressed.

"I wonder why we can pluck a number plate from a vehicle that is brand new and maybe has no issue, rather than getting the black number plate off," Bosire stated, questioning why enforcement design prioritizes physical asset disruption over direct, structured driver discipline.

![NTSA officials removing number plates from a vehicle during an enforcement exercise in April 2024.](https://i.ibb.co/tpJsvBhJ/797c5ba7276e.webp)

Under current highway operations, removing a vehicle's front or rear license plate effectively grounds the asset, preventing further operation until cleared by NTSA inspectors. Transport operators argue this method creates immediate financial leverage for enforcing officers without resolving the underlying driving infractions.

### High Fine Economics and Highway Bargaining

Beyond physical plate confiscation, operators point to the direct relationship between steep statutory fines and systemic bribery on Kenyan highways. When statutory penalties exceed an operator's immediate cash flow or daily earnings, roadside negotiation becomes the default resolution.

"If the fines are exorbitant, what happens? We bargain by giving out bribes," Bosire explained. "If you compromise a person by giving a bribe, then you get familiarity, knowing each other. So then we get compromises in the implementation."

This cycle of informal settlement undermines the deterrence value of high fines. Instead of enforcing compliance, high penalty thresholds create recurring transaction points between drivers and enforcement personnel, allowing habitual offenders to return to the roads after paying informal clearance fees.

### Holiday Travel Risks and Driver Disconnect

![Mass Mobility Operators Association boss Wilfred Bosire during a panel discussion on KBC on Tuesday, November 3, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/hJ1xjbZf/6d2835ee17c1.webp)

The enforcement mismatch is particularly critical as public transport shifts toward high-volume December travel. Upcountry travel surges annually during the festive period, pulling temporary drivers and hired fleet vehicles onto long-distance routes.

Bosire noted that festive travel risks are compounded when temporary drivers operate rented or short-lease vehicles they do not own. Without individual driver tracking systems or personal accountability metrics, driver recklessness escalates on major highways precisely when passenger volume reaches its annual peak.

### Multi-Agency Night Checks Escalate

NTSA's current operations are being conducted under the *Msimu Bila Ajali Usalama Barabarani* campaign, a joint initiative combining safety officers and police units for high-intensity highway checks. Enforcement priorities include targeting drunk drivers, motorists with expired driving licenses, vehicles missing valid insurance, and operators running without current inspection certificates.

Safety authorities have specifically expanded night-time checkpoints along major transit highways, identifying evening and late-night hours as peak windows for fatal crashes.

As the multi-agency campaign continues through the end-of-year travel season, the transport sector is pressing NTSA and the Ministry of Roads to shift enforcement frameworks away from vehicle-based confiscations toward digital driver tracking, uniform penalty systems, and anti-extortion measures at police checkpoints.
