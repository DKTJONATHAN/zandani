Jaymoh Decin is drawing a sharp line against what he describes as half a year of silent sabotage aimed squarely at his commercial lifeline.

The comedian, known across social media feeds for his breakout satirical character Choke Jingli, went public on Thursday, October 8, 2026, revealing that an unidentified individual has systematically targeted his TikTok catalogue with guideline violation reports on nearly every post for six consecutive months.

Rather than treating the flags as routine online friction, Jaymoh framed the persistent crusade as an existential push against his professional footing in Nairobi.

“Someone has just maintained reporting my content on TikTok in every post for the last six months… Anataka turudi ushago. Shindwe! Shetani amlambe! Shindwe pepo mbaya! Mungu anakupenda lakini,” Jaymoh stated in an Instagram address.

### The Fragile Architecture of Online Revenue

While Jaymoh stopped short of naming his suspected antagonist or detailing whether TikTok’s moderation algorithms have issued strikes, suppressed his reach, or removed individual clips, the outburst lays bare the precarious reality facing Kenyan digital entertainers. 

TikTok relies on automated safety triggers alongside user reporting to enforce community guidelines. For independent creators who depend on sustained algorithmic visibility to secure brand partnerships and market ticketed events, an orchestrated reporting campaign can easily throttle engagement before human moderators ever review the flag.

For Jaymoh, the timing of the harassment hits at the most critical turning point of his creative career. 

Just four weeks earlier, on September 12, 2026, he achieved a milestone transition that few purely digital acts successfully pull off: staging his debut live standalone comedy production, *Sudhe Experience*, inside the Kenyatta International Convention Centre (KICC).

### From Mobile Screens to KICC

Taking over the KICC stage marked an expensive, high-stakes wager for a performer who spent years recording skits on limited gear. Jaymoh assembled a large cast, coordinated live choreography, and carried the financial risk of filling seats relying purely on the digital goodwill accumulated across his social channels.

Reflecting on the September showcase immediately after the curtain fell, Jaymoh openly acknowledged the anxiety that went into financing and directing the production, expressing deep gratitude to the ticket buyers who turned out and hinting at a second edition of *Sudhe Experience*.

That leap from phone screens to physical auditoriums followed a decade-long grind in Nairobi’s entertainment trenches. Jaymoh built his digital following through relatable observational comedy, anchored by the eccentricities of Choke Jingli and high-profile impressions.

In March 2026, his exact mimicry of comedian Eric Omondi went viral, drawing wide commentary across creator circles for capturing Omondi’s distinct vocal cadence, nervous energy, and physical mannerisms with clinical accuracy.

### Drawing the Perimeter

Yet even as Jaymoh eyes larger physical stages, the baseline machinery of his business remains tethered to platform discoverability. An adversary targeting his primary digital megaphone strikes directly at the top of the funnel that converts passive video viewers into paying arena patrons.

By pushing the matter into the open, Jaymoh is betting on public exposure to blunt the anonymous campaign. He has not indicated whether he has escalated the targeted reporting directly to TikTok's regional support desks.

What remains clear is that after spending years climbing from shoe-string productions to headlining KICC, Jaymoh has no intention of letting anonymous platform flags undo his gains.
