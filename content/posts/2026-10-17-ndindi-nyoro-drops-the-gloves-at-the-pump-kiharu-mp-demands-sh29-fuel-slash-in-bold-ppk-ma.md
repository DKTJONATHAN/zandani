Kiharu lawmaker Ndindi Nyoro has stepped out under the People’s Party of Kenya (PPK) banner with an aggressive counter-offensive against the country's energy managers, demanding an immediate Sh29 per litre cut on retail fuel prices.

Taking aim at the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA) following its latest monthly pricing schedule, Nyoro framed the current pump escalation as a tone-deaf burden on households already navigating a punishing 6.8 per cent national inflation rate. Rather than sticking to broad political talking points, the Kiharu legislator arrived with a sharp mathematical blueprint intended to push petrol down to Sh197.96 and diesel to Sh198.77 per litre.

“It is insensitive for the government to have announced such hikes in fuel prices,” the PPK stated on Saturday, October 17, 2026. “The current trend is already unbearable.”

### The Sh29 Balance Sheet

At the center of Nyoro’s push is a specific three-pronged fiscal restructuring that intentionally avoids tinkering with Value Added Tax (VAT). The proposal seeks to shave off retail costs through existing administrative levers and state expenditure adjustments.

First, PPK wants an immediate Sh5.5 billion government fuel subsidy injected into the stabilization pool, a move calculated to drop both super petrol and diesel by Sh12 per litre at the pump.

Second, the party is going after the private margins pocketed along the supply corridor, demanding a mandatory Sh10 per litre trim on allowances granted to petroleum importers and distributors.

Finally, Nyoro’s outfit is calling for the wholesale elimination of the Sh7 per litre fuel levy enacted in 2024, arguing that consumers cannot continue carrying emergency levies during a prolonged cost-of-living crunch.

### Taking Aim at Importer Margins and Landed Costs

Beyond consumer relief, Nyoro’s statement zeroed in on what he termed unjustified windfalls within the oil logistics chain. PPK pointed to recent regulator adjustments that handed fuel importers an extra 12 per cent margin bump—amounting to Sh2.16 per litre—describing the increase as indefensible when household budgets are tightening.

The party also confronted EPRA’s pricing mechanics by laying out conflicting data on global procurement costs. While the energy regulator explained its upward review as a reaction to rising international refined petroleum costs and higher petrol landing expenses, PPK highlighted a noticeable anomaly on diesel cargo.

According to calculations released by the party, the landed cost of diesel dropped by 17 per cent, falling from Sh167 per litre in May down to Sh138.21 in the October cycle. Nyoro’s outfit argued that an imported commodity landing significantly cheaper at Mombasa should not translate into a higher pump price for transport operators and farmers.

Under EPRA’s contested October 15 to November 14 pricing cycle for Nairobi, super petrol climbed by Sh12.93 to trade at Sh226.96 per litre. Diesel jumped by Sh9.90 to reach Sh227.76, while kerosene absorbed the heaviest shock, surging by Sh28.49 to hit Sh219.87 per litre.

### Opening the Books on Refining and Import Deals

Nyoro’s public intervention reached past the immediate pump math, turning up institutional scrutiny on major state-backed energy agreements.

PPK demanded the termination of the ongoing Government-to-Government (G-to-G) fuel supply arrangement, calling for a return to open, competitive market bidding among international oil majors to unlock fair consumer prices.

At the same time, the party demanded full transparency around regional refining assets. Nyoro’s party insisted that the government publish the complete investment agreement and unveil the shareholding register for the Dangote East African Petroleum Refinery. The statement concluded with a caution directed at administrative officials regarding the handling of local crude reserves in Turkana, demanding complete protection of the asset.

With the October pump review locking in high transit and energy costs until mid-November, Nyoro’s public challenge moves the conversation from quiet economic frustration to an open, high-stakes confrontation over industry profits and regulatory oversight.
