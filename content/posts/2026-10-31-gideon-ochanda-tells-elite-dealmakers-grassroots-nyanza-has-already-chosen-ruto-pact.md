Former Bondo MP Gideon Ochanda Ogolla has stepped directly into the delicate debate over Nyanza’s political future, delivering a sharp reality check to politicians still locked in high-level alliance talks: the ground has already moved.

In a formal policy paper titled *Reintegrating Nyanza into National Leadership and Community Decisiveness*, the former lawmaker argued that everyday residents across the region have grown weary of protracted political negotiations and have independently resolved to partner with President William Ruto’s administration.

Ochanda’s intervention takes direct aim at the traditional playbook of regional dealmaking, where party principals spend months brokering pacts behind closed doors. While those national political heavyweights continue to navigate boardroom discussions, Ochanda contends that ordinary households on the ground have already made up their minds to prioritize practical engagement over ideological standoff.

### The Cost of Rigid Stances

At the center of Ochanda’s thesis is an unsparing autopsy of the region’s historical positioning. For an extended period, he observed, Nyanza endured systematic exclusion from key state decision-making apparatuses—a condition he attributed directly to rigid political stances that favored resistance over strategic access.

By taking absolutist positions during previous political cycles, Ochanda argued, the community effectively locked itself out of executive governance, leaving regional priorities vulnerable during national budget and development allocations.

According to the former legislator, the current shift is not an ideological surrender but a pragmatic course correction driven by voters who want tangible economic transformation. Communities across the counties, he said, are actively reordering their priorities around sustainable representation, infrastructure, and direct inclusion in public administration.

### Identity Without Isolation

Addressing long-standing fears that cooperating with the national government amounts to political co-optation, Ochanda drew a firm boundary between collaboration and identity.

He maintained that working closely with the Ruto administration does not demand that the Luo community sacrifice its collective dignity or abandon its distinct cultural identity. Instead, he framed state cooperation as an essential mechanism for self-preservation, arguing that true political maturity lies in pursuing partnerships that actively secure the region’s interests.

By engaging the state machinery directly, Ochanda noted, Nyanza is finally reclaiming the institutional influence it surrendered during years of political isolation. The relationship with the executive, in his assessment, serves as a direct bridge into the national policy room where critical resources and long-term economic strategies are determined.

### A Widening Disconnect

Ochanda’s remarks put an uncomfortable spotlight on the widening disconnect between top-tier political brokers and their traditional grassroots bases. As senior leaders deliberate on coalitions, conditions, and future electoral tickets, local voters are increasingly measuring political value by measurable development outcomes rather than symbolic defiance.

By declaring that the grassroots has already settled the question of government cooperation, Ochanda has effectively challenged the political establishment to catch up with the people they claim to lead. The unresolved question now rests on how the region’s established power brokers respond to a voter base that is increasingly choosing state partnership over perpetual opposition.
