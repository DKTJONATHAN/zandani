The public square is not a courtroom, nor is it a sanctuary for the airing of private grievances, yet we have watched over the past fortnight as the personal affairs of activist Morara Kebaso have spilled into the digital commons with corrosive effect. When a figure who positions himself as a standard-bearer for civic integrity chooses to conduct the dissolution of a family unit through performative social media sparring, the public is forced to reckon with a troubling question: does the mandate of leadership extend to one’s private conduct, or are we witnessing the erosion of the boundary between the advocate and the individual?

The evidence of this collapse is visible in the trail of accusations that have defined the month of September. What began as an announcement regarding a change in marital status-a private life choice protected by law and custom-rapidly devolved into a public theater of claims regarding domestic violence and child neglect. By choosing to counter these serious allegations in the same arena they were raised, Mr. Kebaso did not merely defend his name; he invited the public to participate in the dismantling of his own credibility. While his recent apology and call for restraint are belated steps toward decorum, the damage is already etched into the collective memory of a citizenry that was looking for a catalyst for change, not a domestic drama.

Who pays for this theater? The cost is not merely the dignity of those directly involved, but the currency of public trust. When an activist-someone who demands accountability from the state-cannot navigate his own household affairs without resorting to public vitriol, he diminishes the potency of his platform. The taxpayer, who is frequently asked to place their faith in rising political figures to lead the nation, is the ultimate victim of this distraction. We are left with a hollowed-out discourse where the substance of civic advocacy is eclipsed by the spectacle of personal scandal.

If leadership is to mean anything in this republic, it must be rooted in the consistency of character. We demand that those who seek the public eye understand that leadership is not a costume to be worn only when the cameras are rolling. Mr. Kebaso’s apology is a start, but it is insufficient. He owes the public a return to the work that earned him their attention in the first place-a return characterized by the very transparency and accountability he has preached to others. It is time to shutter the theater, resolve these matters in the quiet spaces where they belong, and prove that the commitment to public service is not merely a slogan, but a discipline practiced even when no one is watching.
