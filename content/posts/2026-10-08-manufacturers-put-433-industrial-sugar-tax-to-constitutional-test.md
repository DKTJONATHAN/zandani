A steep fiscal intervention aimed at shielding domestic millers has collided with constitutional scrutiny at the Milimani High Court, where industrial processors are contesting the legality of a more than fivefold tax hike on their primary manufacturing inputs.

The Kenya Association of Manufacturers (KAM) lodged a constitutional petition demanding the annulment of Section 36(a)(vi) of the Finance Act 2026. The disputed clause raised the excise duty on imported industrial sugar from Ksh7.50 to Ksh40 per kilogramme—an abrupt 433 percent surge that processors argue threatens factory floor operations and offends foundational public finance rules.

In its filing, the manufacturing lobby asked the court to declare the enactment null and void, asserting that Parliament pushed through the heavy levy without meaningful public engagement.

![The Kenya Association of Manufacturers building.](https://i.ibb.co/cXYhWd2K/a9f3d98fc566.webp)

### The Burden of Article 201

Beyond procedural lapses during the legislative cycle, the manufacturers' case rests on the constitutional architecture governing how the state extracts revenue. KAM contends the enactment contravened Articles 10(2)(a), 118(1)(b), and 201(a) of the Constitution, which dictate national values of governance, parliamentary openness, and the equitable sharing of tax obligations.

The lobby submitted that the Ksh40-per-kilo levy subjects a narrow industrial band to "unnecessary duplication and cumulative over-taxation." By levying punitive excise rates on an essential, lawful raw material that domestic sugar mills do not produce in sufficient refining grades, the petition contends the state imposed an arbitrary and disproportionate measure under the guise of tariff protection.

The government enacted the revised duty within the 2026 fiscal framework under a declared policy to stimulate domestic cane agriculture and support local milling operations. Yet industrial users maintain that blending commercial table sugar policies with raw manufacturing inputs penalizes processing facilities that have lawful recourse to imported industrial grades.

### Fast-Tracked Hearing

The economic friction generated by the tax increase drew swift attention on the bench. High Court Judge David Mburu certified the constitutional petition as urgent, declined to leave the matter in ordinary registry queues, and issued immediate directions to the state legal team.

Justice Mburu ordered the national government, the Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA), and other cited respondents to file their formal replies to the petition within seven days. 

The case returns before the High Court for mention on October 15, setting up a decisive procedural showdown over whether state revenue targets and protectionist trade tariffs can outrank the constitutional bounds of taxpayer consultation and equitable burden-sharing.
