Kenyans living abroad who bank on domestic rental properties to service mortgages or generate passive yields are facing an aggressive fiscal reality: a flat 30 percent gross tax deducted before maintenance, management fees, or property repairs are accounted for.

The requirement, established under the Finance Act 2026 and enforced retroactively from July 1, creates a stark fourfold disparity in the domestic housing market. While landlords residing in Kenya pay a simplified residential rental income tax of 7.5 percent on gross rent, offshore owners must surrender nearly a third of their total top-line earnings on identical housing assets.

On Friday, the Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA) moved to clarify the compliance machinery behind the framework, directly placing collection liability on the domestic intermediaries—primarily letting agents, estate managers, and family members—who oversee rental properties on behalf of relatives abroad.

### The Math of the Gross Deduction

Under the non-resident property framework, KRA disallows ordinary expense offsets that commercial landlords traditionally use to balance their tax burden. The 30 percent cut is calculated on top-line billings rather than net earnings.

![A collage of rental apartments in Nairobi](https://i.ibb.co/QFQdwzd1/f720b525cdfd.webp)

In practical numbers published by the taxman, a Kenyan based in Europe, North America, or the Gulf earning Ksh100,000 in monthly rent from a Nairobi apartment block will see Ksh30,000 sent directly to the tax authority. The landlord retains Ksh70,000 to cover any ongoing service charges, loan repayments, tenant arrears, or structural upkeep.

By contrast, a resident landlord collecting the exact same Ksh100,000 monthly rent hands over Ksh7,500 under the domestic 7.5 percent gross rate, leaving them with Ksh92,500 before expenses.

"Under the Finance Act 2026, there is now a simplified tax framework for non-residents earning rental income from Kenyan property," KRA stated in an advisory on Friday, directing offshore owners to audit their registration records and reconcile their portfolios.

### Relatives as Statutory Tax Collectors

The central compliance lever for Times Tower does not rest abroad; it rests within Kenya's borders. Recognizing the administrative challenge of pursuing individuals residing overseas, the tax agency has explicitly deputized domestic managers and kin into tax-withholding agents.

Under the guidelines, any local person collecting rent on behalf of a diaspora owner—whether a formal property management firm or a sibling handling tenancy agreements—is statutory responsible for withholding the 30 percent and remitting it to KRA.

![Kenya Revenue Authority building at Times Towers](https://i.ibb.co/v40pyxPt/81a11c1f9966.webp)

"Do you have someone managing the property for you, for example, a relative, agent, or property manager?" KRA noted. "They may now be required to withhold and remit tax on your behalf."

This shift transforms informal family arrangements into legal tax-agency obligations. Where a relative merely forwards net rent to an offshore bank account or mobile wallet without settling KRA's share, the domestic collector risks statutory non-compliance.

### Filing Deadlines and Late Penalties

The collection calendar operates on a strict monthly cycle. Remittances must hit KRA accounts by the 20th day of the month following the period the rent was earned, matching standard withholding tax timelines.

Missing that cut-off carries immediate statutory costs. Under existing tax procedures, late remittance triggers a flat 5 percent penalty on the principal unpaid balance. Unsettled dues then accrue compounding interest at 1 percent per month until the account is cleared.

With the framework already legally operative since July, the taxman is now actively demanding reconciliation for the third quarter of 2026. For diaspora investors who poured capital into Kenya's residential developments on the assumption of high single-digit net yields, the enforcement focus now shifts to whether local agents will automatically deduct the 30 percent at source or leave offshore landlords exposed to accumulating arrears.
