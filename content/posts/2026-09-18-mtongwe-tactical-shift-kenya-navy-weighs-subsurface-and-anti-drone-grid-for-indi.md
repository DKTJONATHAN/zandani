The Kenya Navy is recalibrating its territorial surveillance architecture, shifting its tactical focus beyond conventional surface patrols to counter emerging threats unfolding both in coastal airspace and beneath the waterline.

![Kenya Navy officers during a maritime security operation in the Western Indian Ocean.](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_inner/public/images/media/kenya%20navy%20officers.jpg.webp?itok=evq9FsVB)

During high-level military demonstrations conducted at the Mtongwe Naval Base in Mombasa, service commanders reviewed specialized reconnaissance hardware that would establish a multi-layered detection net across the country's Indian Ocean maritime domain.

The presentations, led by a delegation from Swedish defense manufacturer Saab, were hosted by Kenya Navy Commander Major General Paul Otieno, centering on systems engineered to address gaps that conventional coastal observation posts cannot track.

### Expanding Beyond Surface Patrols

Traditional naval defense along the East African seaboard has historically relied on surface vessels and visual watch posts to track commercial traffic, intercept smuggling conduits, and deter pirate skiffs. However, the proliferation of low-cost commercial drones and unmanned systems in regional maritime corridors has created vulnerabilities for fixed coastal installations and territorial sea lanes.

To counter aerial vectors, the Swedish defense firm pitched 3D radar installations paired with Counter-Unmanned Aircraft Systems (C-UAS). These platforms are designed to identify low-radar-cross-section targets, track autonomous aerial drones, and provide target data to neutralize inbound incursions before they breach sensitive shoreline boundaries or critical port facilities.

Complementing the airspace shield is long-range coastal radar hardware aimed at feeding real-time positional data into naval command centers, improving operational awareness across Kenya's Exclusive Economic Zone.

### Tracking Below the Surface

A critical segment of the Mtongwe assessment involved underwater reconnaissance, a sector that remains difficult to police with surface ships alone. Saab showcased sensor arrays alongside Unmanned Underwater Vehicles (UUVs) capable of autonomous submerged operation.

These subsurface units conduct underwater mapping, monitor seabed infrastructure, and detect illicit subsurface craft or submersibles that bypass surface radar nets. For Kenya, where underwater monitoring has historically faced technical and asset constraints, deploying unmanned submersible sensors would represent an operational first in domestic naval architecture.

According to the Kenya Defence Forces, recent structural changes in global maritime warfare require continuous capital spending in surveillance and detection hardware. The regional threat picture has shifted from basic armed sea robbery into more technical asymmetric harassment, requiring naval assets to anticipate risks on, above, and under the water.

While the engagement at Mtongwe marks an exploratory technical evaluation, the focus on integrated counter-drone and autonomous sub-surface systems reflects the military's broader push to modernize Kenya's blue-economy security perimeter amid evolving regional maritime pressures.
