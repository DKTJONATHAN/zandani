Interior Principal Secretary Dr. Raymond Omollo did not lean on standard administrative decrees to tackle the long-running political argument over who truly owns Nairobi. Instead, he pointed directly to the dinner tables and living rooms of ordinary city residents.

Speaking to delegates at the Nairobi County Women Coordinators’ Consultative Forum, Omollo used the reality of modern Kenyan family life—specifically inter-ethnic marriages and cross-cultural ties—to dismantle the idea that the country’s capital could ever belong to a single ethnic bloc.

His message was delivered with deliberate clarity to the gathered women leaders: Nairobi is not, and cannot become, the private preserve of any one community.

### The Reality of Modern Households

Addressing the forum, Omollo framed the capital as a shared national home whose identity is built on demographic mixing rather than regional entitlement. He cited the everyday bonds formed between Kenyans of diverse origins—specifically naming members of the Luo, Kikuyu, Kalenjin, Kisii, Luhya, and other communities—who live side by side, work together, and build blended families.

According to the PS, cross-cultural marriages provide tangible, living proof that ordinary Kenyans naturally look past tribal boundaries in their personal lives. When families unite across traditional cultural lines, Omollo observed, they establish organic bonds that render divisive political rhetoric hollow.

By spotlighting family structures, Omollo shifted the conversation on city identity from high-level state policy to relatable domestic realities. In his assessment, the social fabric created by these domestic unions already reflects the cohesion that institutional leaders often struggle to articulate.

### Charging Women as Community Anchors

The choice of audience was central to his delivery. Omollo pointed to the women attending the consultative forum as an authentic cross-section of Kenya’s collective diversity, noting that their joint participation proved how community organizers can mobilize without falling into ethnic favoritism or discrimination.

He challenged the coordinators to translate that diversity into everyday grassroots protection. Omollo urged them to step forward as active defenders of social inclusion, ensuring that every resident in their respective neighborhoods feels recognized, represented, and treated as an equal member of the city.

In Omollo’s view, local women coordinators occupy a strategic vantage point where national policy meets household reality. Because they interact directly with families, youth, and neighborhood welfare groups, he argued they bear a collective responsibility to reject insular attitudes that treat fellow citizens as outsiders based on their surnames or ancestral homes.

### Pushing Back on Enclave Politics

Nairobi’s political arena has frequently been subjected to periodic territorial claims, with rival formations occasionally attempting to frame the capital as an exclusive electoral or cultural stronghold. Omollo’s address stood as an explicit pushback against that posture.

He insisted that no Kenyan should ever experience exclusion or preferential disadvantage within the metropolis. A city that serves as the economic engine and administrative seat of the republic, he maintained, must reflect the aspirations and rights of the entire population.

Beyond calling for social tolerance, Omollo asked the coordinators to maintain steady cooperation with government officials in delivering public services. He stressed that administrative support and public welfare initiatives can only succeed when public servants and community liaisons work toward common metropolitan goals rather than competing ethnic interests.

Omollo closed his address by reminding the delegates that the true character of Nairobi lies in its openness. For a city defined by internal migration and blended households, the PS asserted, shared ownership is not merely an idealistic goal—it is the everyday reality that keeps the capital running.
