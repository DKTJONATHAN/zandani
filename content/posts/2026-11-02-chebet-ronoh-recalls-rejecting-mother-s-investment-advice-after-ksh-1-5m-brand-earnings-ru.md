Chebet Ronoh was barely 19 when a single month of digital brand deals delivered KSh 1.5 million into her bank account. Fresh off a string of viral humorous skits that transformed her into one of Kenya's early online celebrities, the sudden influx of commercial capital presented an immediate decision point: invest the windfall or succumb to the financial chaos of teenage stardom.

When her mother stepped in with advice on long-term wealth preservation, Ronoh dismissed the suggestion. She funnelled the earnings into social gatherings, heavy drinking, and seeking peer approval across Nairobi's nightlife circuit.

Speaking in an interview with BBC News Africa, the creator traced her early financial missteps back to a combination of sudden wealth, high-pressure brand contracts, and an unguided digital space that offered few structural precedents for young talent.

### The KSh 1.5 Million Month

Ronoh’s commercial peak as a teenager arrived rapidly, fueled by high engagement rates that drew corporate advertisers directly to her social channels.

“I made my first million as a teenager at 19 and I mismanaged it,” Ronoh said. “In one month, I think I was doing 4 to 5 brands and it reached like KSh 1.5 million.”

![Chebet Ronoh // Instagram](https://i.ibb.co/mVz5XdTy/b9abb9a474f5.webp)

The immediate reaction was excitement, shared first with her family before financial reality gave way to instant gratification.

“I remember telling my mom, ‘Oh my God, this is it, I am going to be a star,’ but my mom was trying to tell me we should invest,” she recalled. “I didn’t want to hear none of that advice. So, I remember a lot of my money went to alcohol.”

Without an established blueprint for managing multi-figure monthly incomes, Ronoh treated the financial influx as a means to purchase social standing among her peers.

“I was still a kid, I’d say I was a teenager desperate for validation,” she explained. “I didn’t even know who I was, I just loved that people liked me and I wanted to prove so much to my friends.”

### Pioneer Isolation in Kenya's Creator Economy

When Ronoh broke into public prominence, Kenya’s influencer industry was vastly different from today's structured ecosystem of talent management agencies and commercial brokers. Most recognized comedy figures operated within legacy television formats, leaving digital-native creators to negotiate, price, and manage their own brand accounts without industry mentors.

“I didn’t really have role models, at that time especially in Kenya, there weren’t really a lot of creators,” Ronoh noted. “They were a handful; they were having comedians from TV, and very few content creators.”

That lack of institutional guidance left her navigating complex commercial contracts while simultaneously handling personal adolescence, peer pressure, and substance exposure in the public eye.

“I was going through it as a teenager for the first time while also navigating friendship and alcohol and all these things. It was a disaster,” she stated.

### Public Image and Recovery

The financial missteps quickly extended beyond personal balance sheets into her public profile. Heavy drinking sessions consumed both her earnings and her professional availability, complicating her ability to sustain brand partnerships at a time when corporate reliance on digital creators was accelerating.

“As a teenager, I got introduced to alcohol and that really affected me, affected my opportunities, my selfhood as a person that in return was a big part of my public image,” Ronoh acknowledged. “I’d say it wasn’t really easy, but it was beautiful to experience.”

Her decision to publicly address the loss of her early earnings highlights the recurring pressure facing young digital stars across East Africa, where viral visibility often outpaces financial literacy.

Having since pivoted toward personal growth and sober lifestyle habits, Ronoh’s candid breakdown of her pioneer years serves as a stark reflection on the financial traps lurking behind early viral stardom.
