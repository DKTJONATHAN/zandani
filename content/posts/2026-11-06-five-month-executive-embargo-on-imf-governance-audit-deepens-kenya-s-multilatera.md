Five months after receiving the final version of a comprehensive governance and corruption diagnostic from the International Monetary Fund, the National Treasury has yet to table the report in Parliament or commit to a publication date, triggering a multi-front standoff over fiscal transparency.

The document, formally handed over in June 2026 after an initial review process that began with a December 2025 draft, was commissioned by the government in September 2024 following widespread youth-led public protests demanding institutional accountability and tighter controls on public spending.

Despite public assurances, the Executive’s withholding of the audit has coincided with a prolonged freeze in sovereign credit support. Kenya has been unable to secure a new funding programme with the Washington-based lender for several months, following the expiration of its previous $3.6 billion (Ksh467 billion) facility in April 2025. Unresolved governance concerns and anti-corruption delays remain central obstacles to renewing structural financial backing.

![A collage of Cabinet Secretary of National Treasury John Mbadi (left), and that of International Monetary Fund Banner.](https://i.ibb.co/FL11bhM3/58dd8e77814c.webp)

Pressure on the Treasury escalated significantly following the release of the World Bank’s latest *Kenya Economic Update*, which placed the country among the most corrupt lower-middle-income nations. The World Bank report estimated that corruption and institutional collusion drain 2 per cent of Kenya's Gross Domestic Product annually, driving up fiscal deficits and forcing the state into costlier market borrowing to plug budget holes.

On Friday, a coalition of civil society organizations led by Amnesty International Kenya and the Okoa Uchumi Campaign issued a joint statement demanding that the Executive immediately lift the embargo on the report, table it before the National Assembly, and release a binding implementation timeline.

The advocacy groups argued that because the diagnostic relied on public consultations, taxpayer resources, and state financing, the government lacks legal or moral grounds to maintain confidentiality.

![President William Ruto at Eldoret State Lodge, Uasin-Gishu County, October 16, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/mVwKCZb7/e6f9b0db93f5.webp)

"The Government must honour that demand," the joint coalition stated. "Release the report to citizens. Table it in Parliament and let Kenyans hold power to account."

The political battle over the report moved into the legislature on Wednesday, November 4, when Tetu Member of Parliament Geoffrey Wandeto filed a formal query targeting the Departmental Committee on Finance and National Planning. Wandeto demanded an official explanation from the Executive regarding the document's status, triggering a statutory requirement for the committee to respond within two weeks.

While Treasury Principal Secretary Chris Kiptoo acknowledged during the World Bank report launch that the government intends to make the IMF audit public, he declined to commit to a specific deadline or outline the publication workflow.

The technical assessment examines systemic governance breakdowns, public financial management loopholes, and institutional vulnerabilities across key state organs. With the National Assembly's two-week clock now ticking, lawmakers and civic groups are positioning the report's mandatory disclosure as a test of whether the administration will implement structural reforms required to restore both domestic trust and international credit access.
