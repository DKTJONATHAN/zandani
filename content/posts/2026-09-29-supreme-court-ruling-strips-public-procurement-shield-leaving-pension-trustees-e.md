By carving state-sponsored retirement schemes out of Kenya's public procurement laws, the Supreme Court has dismantled the rigid statutory framework that public pension trustees traditionally used to justify and protect their spending. 

This legal shift strips away the protective compliance checklist of the Public Procurement and Asset Disposal Act (PPADA), leaving trustees to navigate a regulatory vacuum. For the administrators of these multi-billion-shilling funds, the change means they can no longer hide behind state procurement bureaucracies; they now bear direct, personal fiduciary responsibility for ensuring that every contract signed is competitive, fair, and value-driven.

### The Constitutional Carve-Out

The sudden policy shift stems from a landmark Supreme Court decision delivered on May 15, 2026. The apex court declared Section 2(o) of the Public Procurement and Asset Disposal Act, 2015, unconstitutional and void to the extent that it governed the pension funds of public entities.

![A signpost of Retirement Benefits Authority (RBA).](https://i.ibb.co/tMcx66CL/672949c50af0.webp)

While the ruling was celebrated by some as a liberation from the slow, stifling processes of government procurement, it immediately invalidated the legal playbook used by public-sector pension schemes. Without PPADA coverage, there is no standardized, legally mandated process for how these schemes acquire goods, physical works, or professional services.

To prevent a governance free-fall, the Retirement Benefits Authority (RBA) has stepped in to draft a new "Retirement Benefits Procurement Guideline." The regulator has issued an urgent call to trustees, fund managers, sponsors, and the public to submit proposals and memoranda by Friday, October 9, 2026, at 5:00 PM to help shape these stopgap rules.

### A Twin Administrative Crunch

This procurement overhaul comes at a particularly stressful moment for pension administrators. Just a week before launching this public consultation, the RBA squeezed trustees on another critical compliance front.

On September 22, the authority issued a directive ordering all retirement schemes whose financial years ended on June 30 to submit their audited financial accounts by September 30. Under the law, schemes are granted a strict three-month window after the close of their financial year to file these audits. Schemes that miss this looming deadline face immediate statutory penalties.

Consequently, trustees are currently trapped between wrapping up their past financial year under close regulatory scrutiny and scrambling to submit proposals for how they will procure services in the future.

### The Weight of Fiduciary Duty

Until the RBA finalizes and publishes its new procurement guidelines, public pension funds are operating in an uncertain regulatory transition. 

In the absence of a rigid procurement act, the RBA has made it clear that trustees must rely heavily on their primary fiduciary duties. These duties are dictated by the Retirement Benefits Act, individual scheme trust deeds, and the RBA's Good Governance Practices Guidelines. 

While this gives schemes more operational flexibility to hire service providers and managers, it also removes the legal defense of "bureaucratic compliance." If a contract fails, or if fund assets are wasted on poor-value service providers, trustees can no longer argue that they simply followed the government's procurement steps. They must prove they actively protected the financial interests of their members through transparent, competitive, and accountable negotiations.

Once the October 9 deadline for public submissions passes, the RBA plans to compile the feedback to create an exposure draft. This draft will undergo further review and stakeholder validation before becoming the new operating standard for Kenya's public retirement sector.
