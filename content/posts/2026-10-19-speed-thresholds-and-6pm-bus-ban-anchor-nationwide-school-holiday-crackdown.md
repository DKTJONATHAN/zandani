A 20-kilometre-per-hour difference on a vehicle speedometer is the dividing line between life and death for Kenyan children heading home for the long school break.

According to casualty impact assessments compiled by the National Transport and Safety Authority (NTSA), a child struck by a vehicle moving at 30 kilometres per hour faces a 10 per cent risk of death. That figure surges past 80 per cent once vehicle velocity touches 50 kilometres per hour.

That statistical cliff is now anchoring an intensified nationwide road safety campaign launched on Monday, October 19, by the National Police Service (NPS) in coordination with NTSA. The joint operation deploys additional traffic personnel to major transit arteries and transport hubs as hundreds of thousands of learners exit boarding institutions and day schools across the country.

### The Operational Checklist

State regulators are focusing enforcement on the mechanical readiness of school fleets and long-distance passenger service vehicles before they leave bus parks.

![School buses awaiting inspection by NTSA officials](https://i.ibb.co/pjqNvVX5/79cc41cecff7.webp)

Under the joint directive, officers on highway patrol and inspection units have been instructed to conduct on-the-spot assessments of vehicle roadworthiness. Enforcement teams are checking brake efficiency, tyre tread depth, and the functional integrity of vehicle lighting systems—three mechanical failure points blamed for severe crashes during peak travel periods.

Inside vehicle cabins, officers are mandated to verify functional safety belts for every passenger seat, moving to arrest drivers carrying excess passengers.

Regulators have also placed transporters under a strict operating window through the standing *Pumzisha Mboka* initiative. The directive bars institutional school buses from operating on public roads between 6pm and 6am, forcing institutions to schedule departure times that allow buses to reach their destinations before dusk.

### Curbing Transit Recklessness

The coordinated rollout follows widespread alarm over footage circulating on digital platforms showing high-speed commuter matatus—popularly referred to as *nganya*—overtaking recklessly along urban routes while passengers appearing to be secondary school students hang from open doors and windows.

In response, the joint campaign has directed patrols to intercept and impound any passenger vehicle permitting passengers to protrude from windows or bodywork, with operators facing immediate suspension of road service licences.

![NTSA](https://i.ibb.co/C5tr3Qjp/4203e68d78fa.webp)

NTSA data confirms that pedestrians make up the largest overall share of traffic fatalities on Kenyan roads. During national school closures, that risk profile shifts heavily toward unaccompanied learners navigating highway crossings, crowded termini, and peri-urban transport corridors.

### Engineering Safe Corridors

Beyond roving highway checkpoints, transport planners are attempting to insulate school environments using structural engineering rather than relying solely on driver discretion.

Under its *Safe Roads, Bright Futures* programme, NTSA recently retrofitted transit approaches around Mwangaza Comprehensive School in Kayole, installing speed bumps, demarcated pedestrian walkways, and clear signage engineered to enforce a mandatory 30-kilometre-per-hour speed cap.

While fixed engineering solutions remain limited to pilot zones like Kayole, the immediate challenge for transport marshals over the coming days rests on highway compliance.

With commercial operators facing surging passenger volumes, the joint police-NTSA patrol network will test whether rigorous pre-trip mechanical checks and nighttime movement curbs can prevent the travel spike from transforming into a preventable casualty toll.
