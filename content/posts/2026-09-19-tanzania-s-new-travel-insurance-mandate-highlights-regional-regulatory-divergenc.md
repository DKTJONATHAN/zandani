While the East African Community (EAC) often speaks to the harmonization of trade and movement, the recent introduction of mandatory inbound travel insurance in Tanzania reveals a growing divergence in how regional neighbors manage the risks-and revenues-associated with international visitors.

Under the newly gazetted Insurance (Inbound Travel Insurance) Regulations, 2026, foreign nationals entering mainland Tanzania must now secure a policy priced at Ksh5,700 ($44). The mandate, which covers emergency medical care, evacuation, and luggage loss for up to 92 days, effectively formalizes a state-backed insurance net managed by the National Insurance Corporation (NIC) or its partners.

Crucially, the policy provides a clear regulatory shield for the state: visitors arriving without coverage face the risk of being denied entry, a firm enforcement mechanism that contrasts sharply with the experience of travelers at Jomo Kenyatta International Airport.

### The Legal Divide

Kenya’s attempt to introduce a similar inbound travel health insurance framework in July 2026 was met with immediate, high-stakes resistance. While Kenya’s proposed policy aimed for a more comprehensive benefit structure-including specific caps for mental health and medical repatriation-it was halted by the High Court in August. Justice Francis Rayola Olel’s interim order effectively suspended the program, leaving the government’s revenue stream and its mandate for foreign visitors in a state of indefinite legal limbo.

Tanzania appears to have bypassed this friction by leveraging existing precedents. The mainland mandate is a natural expansion of the insurance policy already in effect for Zanzibar, which has been operational since October 2024. By utilizing this existing architecture, Dar es Salaam has managed to implement a nationwide requirement without the immediate judicial pushback that stalled Nairobi’s initiative.

### Regional Exceptions

Unlike Kenya’s suspended policy, which faced scrutiny over its broad application, Tanzania’s regulations include explicit carve-outs. Citizens of EAC member states and the Southern African Development Community (SADC) are exempt from the requirement. This distinction underscores a deliberate effort to keep regional integration corridors open, even as the country tightens the requirements for global tourists.

For the international traveler, the policy offers a simplified, if mandatory, entry requirement. The 92-day validity period allows for multiple entries, providing a degree of flexibility for business travelers and tourists alike. However, for those staying beyond the three-month mark, the requirement resets, necessitating the purchase of a new policy.

As it stands, the implementation highlights a fragmented regional landscape. While both Kenya and Tanzania are moving toward a model of mandatory, state-linked insurance to offset the costs of emergency services for non-residents, the path to implementation remains uneven. Tanzania has successfully institutionalized its policy, while the Kenyan version remains a subject of ongoing litigation.
