Kenya's exchequer will surrender tens of billions of shillings in foregone petroleum revenues over the next half-year, after the National Assembly intervened to shield motorists from a statutory tax snapback that would have pushed pump prices into unprecedented territory.

Lawmakers on Wednesday passed the Tax Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, anchoring Value Added Tax on super petrol, diesel, and kerosene at 8 percent for an additional 90 days. The legislation, introduced by Majority Leader Kimani Ichung’wah, grants the Cabinet Secretary for the National Treasury discretion to stretch the concession by another three months if tensions in the Middle East fail to subside.

The legislative manoeuver prevents fuel VAT from automatically reverting to the standard 16 percent rate. However, the intervention comes with severe fiscal trade-offs for a government already operating under constrained revenue targets.

### The Ksh54 Billion Tax Gap

Parliament’s decision to lock the discounted tax rate in place follows a steep toll on state collections. During debate on the floor, Finance Committee Chairperson Kimani Kuria revealed that the National Treasury had already relinquished approximately Ksh54 billion in uncollected revenue across the preceding six months to keep the 8 percent petroleum rate alive.

By renewing the concession for at least three months—with an administrative pathway extending to six—the legislature has committed the Treasury to absorb comparable revenue haircuts through early 2027. Kuria argued that letting VAT double to 16 percent amid skyrocketing crude import costs would have triggered uncontrollable inflationary shocks across public transport, agricultural machinery, and industrial logistics.

Yet the tax sacrifice also functions as an emergency buffer for a regulatory mechanism that broke down in the latest monthly pricing cycle.

### Regulators Freeze the Safety Net

The rush to pass the Tax Laws (Amendment) Bill came hours after the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA) implemented one of its sharpest price revisions of the year, increasing super petrol by Ksh12.93 per litre, diesel by Ksh9.90, and kerosene by Ksh28.49.

Those hikes pushed Nairobi pump prices to Ksh226.96 per litre for super petrol, Ksh227.76 for diesel, and Ksh219.87 for kerosene. In Mombasa, petrol climbed to Ksh223.81, diesel to Ksh224.61, and kerosene to Ksh216.71.

The price increases were compounded because EPRA opted not to deploy the Petroleum Development Levy’s stabilization fund to absorb the landing cost shock. EPRA Director General Edward Mwirigi defended the withholding of stabilization funds, stating the authority chose to preserve cash reserves rather than deplete them, keeping money aside to cushion consumers in subsequent cycles if global markets deteriorate further.

That conservative regulatory posture shifted the immediate burden of relief entirely onto the legislature’s tax policies.

### Middle East Shockwaves Hit Import Costs

The twin pressures of regulatory austerity and fiscal intervention stem from escalating geopolitical disruption. EPRA Petroleum and Gas Director Edward Kinyua told journalists that the widening U.S.-Israel military confrontation involving Iran turned September into the most volatile crude procurement window in recent memory.

According to Kinyua, the landed cost of super petrol spiked to USD 1,300 per metric tonne, more than double the USD 600 baseline recorded before the conflict expanded. The resulting surge in import invoices overwhelmed regular pricing models, leaving the government with few alternatives outside tax suppression.

"We need to amend the VAT Act to extend the reduced rate for another 90 days, in the hope that the war in the Middle East will come to an end," Ichung’wah told the House when moving the bill on October 14, noting that the statutory extension could run for up to six months if required.

### The Relief Ceiling

While the passage of the Bill spares consumers an immediate eight percentage-point tax jump, Parliament’s statutory intervention does not insulate pump prices from further increases.

Under EPRA’s monthly calculation formulas, retail prices remain exposed to cargo insurance premiums, foreign exchange fluctuations against the US dollar, and shipping diversions linked to maritime conflict corridors. With the fuel stabilization fund deliberately sidelined by EPRA and the exchequer absorbing a Ksh54 billion tax deficit, the state's capacity to absorb another international supply shock will hinge on how long the Treasury can sustain its revenue surrender.
