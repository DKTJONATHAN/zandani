Former President Uhuru Kenyatta’s insistence that his former prime minister "Baba" actually won the August 2022 general election has done little to rewrite history, but it has successfully reopened a raw, bitter ledger among Azimio insiders who are still pointing fingers over why the campaign collapsed.

Speaking on the 2022 contest, Kenyatta asserted that his camp deployed every available resource and that he personally knows the opposition chief carried the vote. "Na kura akapata, na mimi mpaka wa leo najua ya kwamba yeye ndiye alishinda hiyo kura," he maintained, framing the outcome as an open secret among his inner circle.

That retroactive declaration collides head-on with the cold legal reality sealed on September 5, 2022, when Chief Justice Martha Koome led a unanimous seven-judge Supreme Court bench to uphold William Ruto’s victory. Yet by dragging the debate back into the open, Kenyatta has bypassed the legal consensus to land squarely in the middle of a festering internal blame game that has haunted the coalition ever since.

For years, key figures within the Azimio machinery have traded heavy accusations over structural breakdowns and the chaotic mismanagement of polling station agents. Former chief agent Saitabao Kanchory and Jubilee Party Secretary General Jeremiah Kioni have frequently pointed to logistical failures on the ground, while Embakasi East MP Babu Owino publicly questioned the depth of Kenyatta’s strategic commitment to Odinga’s ticket.

Those simmering tensions boiled over earlier this year when Suna East MP Junet Mohamed found himself defending accusations from Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna regarding unpaid ODM election agents. Mohamed fired back with a revealing structural defense: he claimed that campaign funds designated for those very agents were actually managed independently by figures close to Kenyatta out of a restricted office in Westlands, bypassing core party officials and candidate structures entirely.

By reviving the assertion that the election was won despite these fractures, Kenyatta’s remarks expose the lingering operational disconnect that plagued the coalition from within. Four years after the ballots were cast and counted, the debate within the former opposition is no longer just about what the Supreme Court decided, but who controlled the safehouses, managed the war chests, and left the foot soldiers stranded on polling day.
