When the Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission stepped into Kajiado County for its Mashinani community engagement, the caution against campaign handouts was backed by hard figures drawn directly from national integrity metrics.

EACC Chief Executive Officer Abdi Ahmed Mohamud pointedly tied the acceptance of financial inducements to the erosion of voter leverage, warning that taking money from aspirants strips citizens of the moral ground to demand accountability or service delivery later. But behind that political caution lies a stark financial reality captured in the Kenya National Gender and Corruption Survey.

According to the survey findings, the national average bribe amount surged by 38 percent, climbing from Ksh 4,878 in 2024 to Ksh 6,724 in 2025. The data maps a clear hierarchy of extortion and informal payoffs across public sector tiers, showing that citizens paid an average bribe of Ksh 13,038 to elected county representatives, Ksh 20,126 to civil servants, and Ksh 1,445 to legislators.

Addressing residents during the Wednesday forum, Mohamud stressed that the habit of trading votes for cash directly compromises development mandates. 

“We understand that next year we will be going to the elections, and all that is required of us is to choose leaders with integrity. That’s our responsibility; however, some of us do not take it seriously. That’s where we go wrong as citizens,” Mohamud stated, adding that voters must evaluate candidates on their managerial competence rather than short-term financial gifts.

EACC Chairperson Bishop David Oginde reinforced the stance, challenging public servants to view executive appointments as public trusts rather than personal enrichment vehicles, and characterizing individuals who loot public funds as direct adversaries of community welfare.

With the 2027 General Election cycle approaching, the commission's data-driven approach highlights the steep long-term cost of low-level transactional politics against the backdrop of rising institutional bribe thresholds.
