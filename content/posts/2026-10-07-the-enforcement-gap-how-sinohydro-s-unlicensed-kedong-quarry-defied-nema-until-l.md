The High Court’s suspension of quarrying activities by Sinohydro Corporation in Kedong on Wednesday exposes a critical disconnect in Kenya’s environmental governance: how a major multinational developer can operate a heavy-industrial site without statutory licenses, ignore direct regulatory shutdown orders, and only be reined in when affected citizens bypass state watchdogs entirely.

On Wednesday, October 7, the High Court in Naivasha issued conservatory orders halting all stone-crushing, blasting, and excavation by the Chinese state-owned firm. The judicial intervention came less than twenty-four hours after more than 300 residents, school parents, and local business owners took matters into their own hands, marching on the Moi South Lake Road facility to demand an immediate end to operations that had disrupted the area for two months.

But behind the community's outrage lies a stark admission of regulatory impotence from the National Environment Management Authority (NEMA). 

![Sinohydro engineers work on a construction project in Kenya.](https://i.ibb.co/gFrpZkkh/7fa693b27a25.webp)

Speaking directly to the demonstrators, NEMA Naivasha Environment Officer Jane Njihia confirmed that Sinohydro lacked the necessary approvals from both NEMA and the Nakuru County Government to run the Kedong quarry. More critically, Njihia revealed that the agency had already served the firm with a formal stop order, which the company allegedly chose to ignore, continuing to extract and crush stone for ballast and construction aggregate.

This flagrant defiance shifts the focus from a standard neighborhood environmental dispute to a deeper question of institutional teeth. Sinohydro Corporation has been a fixture of Kenya’s infrastructure landscape for nearly twenty years. Yet, in Kedong, the company operated outside the basic legal frameworks governing environmental safety, leaving local communities to absorb the physical consequences of unchecked industrial blasting.

According to residents, the site’s two-month operations blanketed nearby homes, agricultural properties, and a neighboring school in thick dust, while heavy blasting shook physical structures and threatened local livelihoods. 

![An image of a court gravel](https://i.ibb.co/LDjVPdcH/91af5349fd54.webp)

"The community was seeking justice over the alleged environmental damage," said David Chege, an area youth leader who mobilized residents to camp at the facility. Chege noted that the community was prepared to sustain weekly protests indefinitely to force compliance with local laws.

Rather than relying on state machinery that had already failed to enforce its own administrative directives, local hoteliers and residents chose a dual-track strategy: public demonstration coupled with direct litigation. They filed a private lawsuit at the Naivasha Law Courts to address the economic and environmental fallout of the unsanctioned quarry.

The High Court's conservatory order provides temporary relief to the Kedong community, but it leaves an unresolved precedent. With regulators like NEMA seemingly unable to enforce their own administrative stop orders against major international contractors, the burden of policing industrial compliance has effectively been shifted onto local communities, requiring them to fund private legal battles and coordinate public protests to secure basic environmental protections.

The next phase of the dispute will play out in the Naivasha Law Courts, where the civil suit filed by local business owners and residents will test whether Sinohydro will face financial liabilities for the unauthorized extraction of sand, aggregate, and ballast, and whether Nakuru County will pursue formal prosecution for unlicensed commercial operations.
