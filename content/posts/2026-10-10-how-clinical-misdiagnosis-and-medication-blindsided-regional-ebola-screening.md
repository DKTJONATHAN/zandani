East Africa’s layered biosecurity net failed to intercept a fatal Ebola case not because screening infrastructure was absent, but because routine clinical misdiagnosis and symptom-suppressing medication neutralized the tripwires long before the patient reached a border post.

Details disclosed by the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) show that the 40-year-old Kenyan businessman who died on October 5 had spent nearly a month seeking care across several medical facilities in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). At each station, clinicians treated his worsening illness as an ordinary bacterial condition, allowing him to embark on a multi-stage journey across DRC, Uganda, and Kenya while carrying the Bundibugyo strain of the virus.

According to Wessam Mankoula, head of emergency preparedness at Africa CDC, the diagnostic trail broke down at its inception in September.

"We have multiple diagnoses that vary from one station to another. At the beginning, he presented with fever and a rash," Mankoula told The Associated Press. "They suspected it was a staphylococcal infection, and he began getting treatment."

### The Masked Transit Trail

That initial diagnosis of a staph infection set off a compounding surveillance breakdown. While undergoing antibacterial and palliative therapies that failed to arrest the underlying viral infection, the patient moved across northern DRC from Buta to Kisangani by road. He then boarded an internal flight to Beni—an active Ebola hotspot—without raising an alert.

From Beni, the patient bypassed formal border closures designed to contain hemorrhagic fevers, slipping across the DRC frontier into Uganda by road. Once inside Uganda, he entered mainstream public transportation networks, boarding a bus to Kampala before proceeding to Entebbe.

On October 3, the businessman boarded a commercial Jambojet flight from Entebbe to Nairobi, touching down at Jomo Kenyatta International Airport (JKIA). Despite presenting with malaise, a sore throat, and skin lesions, he walked through airport screening checkpoints undetected.

Kenya’s Ministry of Health confirmed that medications the man took during his weeks of mismanaged care had temporarily subdued his fever spikes. The suppressed core temperature allowed him to clear JKIA’s automated thermal screening cameras, which are calibrated primarily to flag passengers with elevated body heat.

### Late Detection at Nairobi Hospital

![cs-duale.jpg](https://i.ibb.co/g52G410/72725ab822ea.webp)

After clearing the airport, the patient travelled by private car to Nairobi Hospital. It was only upon admission there that attending doctors observed advanced hemorrhagic indicators, including subcutaneous bleeding.

Laboratory tests confirmed the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola. The patient died two days later, on Monday, October 5.

The trajectory highlights a critical operational vulnerability for regional disease surveillance desks: thermal imaging at international entry ports remains dependent on visible physiological markers that standard over-the-counter and prescribed antipyretics can easily mask.

Health authorities in Nairobi have since mounted an aggressive contact-tracing operation. The Ministry of Health has identified 57 contacts connected to the case, comprising immediate family members, medical workers who attended to him in Nairobi, and the passengers and flight crew aboard the October 3 Jambojet flight from Entebbe. 

Public health officials note that identifying contacts represents the first phase of surveillance, with secondary protocols determining which individuals require monitored home isolation versus institutional quarantine over the virus's incubation period.
