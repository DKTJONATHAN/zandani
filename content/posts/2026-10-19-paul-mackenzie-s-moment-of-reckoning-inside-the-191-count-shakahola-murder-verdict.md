Paul Mackenzie steps into the dock at the Mombasa High Court on Monday, October 19, 2026, to receive the verdict that could define the rest of his natural life.

Flanked by 30 co-accused, the controversial founder of the Good News International Church sits before Lady Justice Diana Kavedza-Mochache to hear judgment on 191 counts of murder. The decision marks the formal culmination of an exhaustive marathon trial triggered by the April 2023 discovery of mass graves hidden across an 800-acre wilderness in Kilifi County.

Throughout the proceedings, the self-styled preacher has maintained an unyielding public posture of innocence. Mackenzie’s defense team spent months attempting to pick apart the state's voluminous dossier, asserting before the court that the prosecution built its case on circumstantial inferences, second-hand hearsay, and forensic evaluations that defense attorneys insisted failed to tie the accused directly to individual deaths.

Yet the evidentiary record placed before Justice Kavedza-Mochache tells a deeply detailed story of organized obedience. Over the course of the trial, state prosecutors called a total of 121 witnesses to the stand. That parade included surviving former adherents, grieving family members who watched relatives disappear into the Kilifi scrubland, medical specialists, and homicide detectives who dug through hundreds of shallow sites.

### The Compound Machinery Under Scrutiny

State counsel argued that the deaths inside Shakahola were neither random nor unmanaged. Instead, prosecutors presented testimony alleging Mackenzie instituted a phased, enforced starvation regime framed around an apocalyptic pledge to "meet Jesus" before the world ended.

According to the prosecution's trial submissions, the camp enforced a rigid demographic sequence of mortality: children were allegedly directed to fast first, followed by adolescents, then women, and finally adult men. To ensure adherence, the state argued that Mackenzie relied on an insular hierarchy of village elders, lookouts, grave diggers, and enforcers who kept watch over the secluded property and blocked followers from breaking the fast or escaping.

Autopsies entered into evidence added a chilling layer to the state's case. While severe malnutrition accounted for the majority of the fatalities, government pathologists documented that several victims died from trauma entirely unrelated to starvation, recording blunt force injuries to the head, neck compression, and asphyxiation.

### Families Gather in Mombasa

Outside the courtroom corridors, the human weight of the case remained visible early Monday. Relatives of victims, many of whom made the long bus journey from rural coastal villages into Mombasa town, gathered inside the gallery waiting for the bench to deliver its findings.

For these families, the Mombasa trial represents the sharpest criminal lens yet trained on the Kilifi tragedy, where more than 400 bodies were eventually exhumed. Many of those remains, recovered from the sunbaked thickets of Shakahola, continue to sit in coastal mortuaries awaiting conclusive identification.

The proceedings have also kept an uncomfortable spotlight on Mackenzie’s prolonged public profile prior to the disaster. Long before the forest raids of 2023, the preacher had been picked up by police in 2017 and 2019 over controversial teachings and child welfare concerns, only to secure release and relocate his core followers deeper into Kilifi's hinterland—a history that continues to drive intense national debate around intelligence lapses and the oversight of independent ministries.

### A Sprawling Legal Web

Whatever determination Lady Justice Kavedza-Mochache delivers in Mombasa, Mackenzie’s legal battles will not conclude with this single ruling.

The 191-count murder prosecution represents only one front in a vast, multi-jurisdictional web of state litigation against the preacher and his inner circle. Across different coastal court registries, Mackenzie continues to face separate ongoing criminal cases alleging terrorism, manslaughter, organized torture, and extreme cruelty toward children.

As the court session gets underway, the man who once commanded hundreds of devoted followers through fiery sermons and broadcast broadcasts sits quietly beneath the judge's dais, waiting to see whether his claims of total innocence can survive the state's 121-witness record.
