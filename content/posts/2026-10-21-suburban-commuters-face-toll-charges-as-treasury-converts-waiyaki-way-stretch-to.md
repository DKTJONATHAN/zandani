Daily commuters between Nairobi’s commercial core and Kiambu’s satellite residential nodes face a new transport overhead following the National Treasury’s decision to place the James Gichuru–Rironi highway under private toll management.

The plan, formally greenlit through a Treasury project note approved in September 2026, will convert the 25-kilometre brownfield section of the Northern Corridor into a tolled thoroughfare once outstanding construction works are finalised under a Public-Private Partnership (PPP).

For thousands of motorists and public service vehicle operators running daily routes through Kangemi, Uthiru, Kinoo, Gitaru, and Rironi, the move marks a fundamental shift: turning an essential suburban arterial into a pay-to-use facility to recoup long-term maintenance and civil works spending.

### Filling the Financing Deficit

Treasury detailed the transaction in its latest Quarterly PPP Status Update report, classifying the section as a brownfield venture requiring both civil completion and long-term asset management.

![Cars moving through a busy section of traffic on Waiyaki Way in Nairobi on November 19, 2024.](https://i.ibb.co/Z69SCBpM/1519a0f55add.webp)

"The road is a brownfield project involving the rehabilitation and completion of outstanding civil works, together with the introduction of tolling under a Management Contract," the Treasury noted in the report. "The project seeks to establish a sustainable framework for the operation, maintenance and long-term management of the corridor, which carries high volumes of commuter, passenger and freight traffic."

The pivot to user fees follows nearly a decade of funding disruptions on the Ksh20 billion corridor. Ground broke on the expansion in August 2017, but progress ground to repeated halts after the World Bank withdrew its financing support, forcing the government to scout for alternative funding mechanisms to complete the carriageways.

By packaging the unfinished highway as a private management contract, the state is offloading the capital requirements for final civil works onto a commercial concessionaire, which will recover its outlay directly from road users through toll gates.

### Arterial Pressure Along the Northern Corridor

The James Gichuru–Rironi stretch serves two distinct transit roles that complicate any move to levy user charges. 

![Undated image of the Westlands toll station of the Nairobi Expressway](https://i.ibb.co/6G57tkh/ee6768b0d92c.webp)

Locally, it serves as the primary commuter channel feeding residential traffic from Kikuyu and lower Kiambu into Westlands and the Nairobi central business district. Regionally, it operates as the initial upcountry neck of the Northern Corridor, carrying heavy commercial freight from the Port of Mombasa onward to Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, South Sudan, and the Democratic Republic of Congo.

Engineered specifications from the Kenya National Highways Authority (KeNHA) originally mapped out a six-lane configuration—featuring a three-lane dual carriageway alongside continuous service roads and redesigned non-motorised transit and drainage infrastructure. KeNHA's operational projection was designed to cut transit times along the 25-kilometre stretch from 60 minutes during peak congestion down to 30 minutes.

While the addition of capacity was intended to unlock bottlenecked freight heading toward the Nairobi–Nakuru highway junction at Rironi, tolling the main deck threatens to alter the economics of daily commuting for surrounding neighbourhoods.

### Next Steps in Procurement

With Treasury's formal clearance of the project note, the state is now preparing procurement stages to solicit bids from private consortiums capable of financing the remaining civil works and installing tolling infrastructure.

The procurement will determine the duration of the management concession, the exact toll structure, and whether local feeder traffic using parallel service lanes will face charge exemptions—a critical technical and political factor that will shape transit costs across Nairobi’s western fringe.
