Governor Johnson Sakaja has never been one to deliver boilerplate City Hall talking points when a candid observation about Nairobians will do the job. Stepping up to the podium during the Second National Development Implementation Committee meeting in the capital, the county boss made it categorically clear that his administration will not be entertaining prolonged displaced persons camps, regardless of how intense the anticipated El Niño deluge turns out to be.

His reasoning was characteristically frank: offer free meals and a dry roof in Nairobi, and nobody will ever pack their bags to leave.

“We do not want to create IDP camps,” Sakaja stated plainly, explaining why City Hall is pivoting strictly toward digital cash disbursements instead of open-ended physical care centers. “If you set up dependency on a single location, they will never leave if they know there’s food [and] shelter… In Nairobi, if you give people some resources, cash transfers, they will find their way.”

The remarks lay bare Sakaja’s transactional philosophy when dealing with urban crises. Rather than establishing sprawling tent settlements across the city, the county has partnered with the Kenya Red Cross and the national government to identify public schools and social halls to act purely as holding points. Under Sakaja’s blueprint, these facilities will serve as transit desks and registration clearinghouses to capture verified recipient details, issue emergency money, and disperse crowds before any permanent encampments take root.

That hard-nosed posture comes against a massive displacement forecast. County assessments presented by the governor have mapped 227 flood-prone hotspots across 17 subcounties, with worst-case municipal estimates warning that up to 100,000 residents could be forced out of their homes once the rain peaks.

For Sakaja, managing that human footprint also means managing City Hall’s balance sheet and clearing the bottlenecks that routinely bring Nairobi’s road networks to their knees. County teams have already cleared 26 out of 61 critical drainage points, hacking away at silted riverbeds, blocked culverts, and unauthorized structures that choke the capital’s natural waterways.

Yet the structural bill remains staggering. Fully sorting the ongoing drainage maintenance and river rehabilitation requires KSh 5.17 billion—a budget Nairobi County is attempting to shoulder alongside partner state agencies, including the Kenya Urban Roads Authority (KURA), the Kenya National Highways Authority (KeNHA), and the Red Cross.

Beyond the budget hurdles, the governor took direct aim at the pervasive city skepticism that regularly accompanies weather advisories. With many urbanites treating periodic rain alerts as administrative hyperbole, Sakaja urged the public to drop the cynicism and take the Kenya Meteorological Department’s scientific tracking seriously. Forecasters have assigned a 96 to 98 percent probability to the heavy downpours, projecting sustained wet conditions and temperature spikes that will drag into early 2027.

While Nairobi’s existing stormwater systems can absorb steady, dispersed rainfall, Sakaja noted that the true infrastructure test will come from concentrated flash storms delivering violent volumes in compressed windows. For the governor, the strategy is drawn: unclog what can be unblocked, map the 227 flashpoints, and wire the relief money directly to residents’ pockets before a single tent peg is driven into the mud.
