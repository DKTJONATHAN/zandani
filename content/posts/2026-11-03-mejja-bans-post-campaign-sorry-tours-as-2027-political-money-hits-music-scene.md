Mejja has drawn a strict line for Kenyan artists contemplating political paydays ahead of the 2027 General Election: if you take the money and burn your fan base, do not show up on radio stations later asking for forgiveness.

Speaking during an interview with radio presenter Kwambox on Tuesday, November 3, 2026, the Genge veteran addressed the intense commercial pressure facing musicians as political operatives begin courting creative talent for campaign mobilization. When Kwambox presented a scenario involving a hypothetical Ksh 2 million payout to record campaign material that directly contradicts an artist’s personal beliefs, Mejja dismissed the idea that cash should override core convictions.

“Huwezi kuwa anakuambia nikuambie uende against what you believe alafu ati akupatie 2 million,” Mejja said, insisting that personal values must hold higher weight than an attractive single-day cheque.

### The Cost of the Cheque

For Mejja, the decision to engage in political monetization carries a binary choice. An artist can either preserve their personal principles and walk away from the cash, or accept the payout alongside whatever long-term reputational fallout follows—without expecting the public to digest a subsequent redemption campaign.

“Unasema mimi niko na principles zangu, si ndio? Siezi chukua doh,” he stated during the broadcast. “Lakini nasema tu hivi, unaonanga wale hawachukua doh? Naweza sema, chukua pack ya, ikiaribu career yako you will be okay. Usije uimbe alafu tena nikuje nipige tena interviews zingine za kusema sorry.”

His warning targets a recurring pattern in the local entertainment landscape: musicians who willingly sign lucrative political production deals during high-stakes election cycles, only to launch multi-station apology media tours when corporate sponsors pull out and fan support evaporates post-election.

### 2027 Realignments and Creative Risks

The tension between immediate financial survival and sustained brand equity is escalating as political factions prepare for the 2027 polls. Historically, campaign tracks have served as high-paying windfalls for artists, offering brief periods of national broadcast rotation and substantial upfront cash. However, as youth demographics demand greater alignment between creator advocacy and public policy outcomes, political endorsements carry increasingly severe commercial consequences.

Mejja’s position emphasizes that once fan trust is traded for short-term political compensation, media appearances designed to explain away the choice rarely succeed in restoring standard career momentum.

Rather than dictating whether artists should take campaign funds, the Genge hitmaker framed the issue as one of strict self-ownership: take the Ksh 2 million if you must, but own the long-term career damage in silence when the campaign lights go down.
