According to the Central Bank of Kenya’s weekly statistical bulletin, the domestic currency held practically flat against the US dollar through the first week of November, masking a steep climb in the cost of settling transactions in British pounds and euros.

The regulator’s closing numbers for the week ending November 6 place the local unit at Ksh129.96 against the greenback—unchanged from the prior Friday. That rate keeps the shilling Ksh0.04 clear of the Ksh130 threshold, a psychological benchmark closely tracked by state economic managers and commercial desks.

Yet the stability registered against the dollar did not extend to European trading partners. The British pound advanced by Ksh2.14 over five trading sessions to touch Ksh175.18, up from Ksh173.04 on October 29. The euro recorded a parallel gain, rising Ksh1.49 over the same timeframe to close at Ksh146.27 compared to Ksh144.78 the previous week.

### Divergent Settlement Pressures

For Kenyan entities conducting business across European corridors, the price adjustments alter the transactional arithmetic. Local importers acquiring machinery, pharmaceuticals, and specialized services denominated in sterling or euros faced an instant markup in shilling outlays over the week. Conversely, agricultural exporters shipping cut flowers, tea, and fresh vegetables to British and European supermarkets stand to capture wider shilling receipts upon converting their export earnings, assuming contract prices held steady.

Commercial bank quotations monitored by Reuters mirrored the tight dollar band, trading between Ksh129.85 and Ksh130.05 on Friday, marginally wider than Thursday’s Ksh129.90 to Ksh130.00 spread. Interbank currency dealers indicated expectations of near-term stability for the dollar pairing, supported by balanced corporate demand.

### Central Bank Buffers and Rate Shocks

![A photo collage of Central Bank of Kenya governor Kamau Thugge and shilling notes and coins](https://i.ibb.co/YFFM2N63/de6eee2c7c8c.webp)

The pressure on the pound was amplified offshore. The Bank of England enacted a 0.25 percentage-point policy rate increase, raising its benchmark borrowing cost to 4 per cent. The monetary tightening reinforced sterling yields against peer currencies, driving the rapid repricing seen on Nairobi dealing desks.

In broader global markets, the US Dollar Index climbed 0.4 per cent to cross 102 points, buoyed by safe-haven flows after a technology-sector equity sell-off. Despite that international greenback strength, Kenya’s dollar parity held firm, underwritten in part by an institutional foreign exchange reserve cushion.

Official foreign exchange reserves held by the CBK stood at Ksh1.91 trillion—equivalent to USD 14.699 billion—as of November 5. The reserve position provides the central bank substantial latitude to smooth out transitory liquidity crunches in the interbank market.

Dealing desks are now watching international labor indicators, with forthcoming US non-farm payroll figures expected to determine whether the dollar's global rally accelerates or eases off the shilling's Ksh130 frontier into the next trading cycle.
