Independent contractors who attempt to bypass contractual invoicing terms to claim statutory employment perks in court face an immediate dead end, following a precedent-setting ruling by the Employment and Labour Relations Court.

In a judgment delivered on October 5, 2026, Justice Hellen Wasilwa threw out a Ksh5.31 million suit brought by a project management consultant against a local construction company, ruling that commercial contractors cannot retroactively recast themselves as salaried staff to claim workplace benefits after a business deal collapses.

The claimant had approached the labour court seeking Ksh1.98 million in unpaid fees spanning an 11-month period, alongside Ksh720,000 in housing allowances and termination compensation. He contended that the construction firm frustrated his operational duties, withheld his dues, and abruptly severed ties without statutory notice or procedural fairness.

![Court](https://i.ibb.co/39ghfy7S/3cbed6d79cf3.webp)

### The Ksh180,000 Invoice Clause

The construction company disputed the jurisdiction of the employment claim entirely, maintaining that the engagement was governed strictly by a commercial agreement rather than an employment relationship. The builder told the court that the consultant had simply halted professional services and could not claim statutory employee benefits without first proving an underlying master-servant dynamic.

Upon reviewing the signed agreement, Justice Wasilwa determined that the pact was structured from inception as a business-to-business deal rather than a contract of service. Under the agreed terms, the project manager was entitled to a professional fee of Ksh180,000 per month, payable only upon submitting itemized monthly invoices documenting work carried out during the preceding period.

The agreement was stipulated to remain active until completion of the construction project, subject to early termination clauses.

### Evidentiary Void at the Labour Bench

The claim faltered when the court scrutinized the claimant's administrative trail. Beyond the fact that the contract explicitly and repeatedly designated him as an independent consultant, the claimant failed to provide copies of the mandatory monthly service invoices required to trigger fee disbursements.

Justice Wasilwa ruled that an individual cannot rely on employment legislation to recover commercial debt without first surmounting the evidentiary burden of proving an employer-employee dynamic. By failing to submit the required billing paperwork while attempting to extract statutory employment heads such as housing allowances, the claimant extinguished his own cause of action.

The court observed that any grievance arising from the engagement represented an alleged breach of a commercial agreement rather than an unfair labour practice, leaving the contractor with a dismissed suit and bearing the cost of filing on the wrong legal footing.
