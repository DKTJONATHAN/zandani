The Kenya National Highways Authority (KeNHA) has linked the delay in delivering life-saving dual-carriageway upgrades across more than 2,500 kilometres of critical transit corridors to intense competition for exchequer funding, a stance that shifts immediate relief away from commercial hauliers, public service transport operators, and motorists navigating lethal highway blackspots.

Speaking on Monday, KeNHA Director General Luka Kimeli stated that capital expansion across high-risk networks remains the most decisive engineering measure to curb recurring fatal crashes. However, he acknowledged that the agency’s pipeline is heavily constrained by fiscal rivalry with other statutory sectors for Treasury allocations, capping the speed of major capital projects.

"The biggest challenge we have is the competition we are subjected to when seeking funding with other sectors of the economy," Kimeli said, citing the stalled momentum on multi-lane conversions.

### Stalled Audits and Divergent Diagnoses

KeNHA’s insistence that structural safety depends predominantly on multi-lane overhauls stands in contrast with the position taken by the National Transport and Safety Authority (NTSA). The transport safety regulator has argued that infrastructural engineering cannot be treated as an isolated panacea, noting that several formal safety recommendations it supplied to road authorities remain unexecuted.

![An artistic impression of the Nairobi-Mombasa expressway upon its completion.](https://i.ibb.co/v69VxJdJ/c53cca9245f2.webp)

NTSA Director of Safety Andrew Kiplagat pointed out that traffic incidents are driven by four interdependent elements: mechanical roadworthiness, driver behaviour, road geometry, and emergency medical response systems.

"When a crash happens, you ask yourself what was the issue with the vehicle and the road as well as the road user and the post-crash," Kiplagat said.

While the NTSA confirmed it has completed comprehensive safety audits across key arteries and forwarded specific engineering recommendations—including localized dualling—it noted that state road agencies have yet to operationalize key findings on the ground. 

### Engineering Deficits on Critical Freight Arteries

The policy disconnect plays out across Kenya’s primary logistics links, including the Nairobi–Nakuru, Nakuru–Eldoret, and Mombasa–Nairobi highways. While ongoing construction continues to disrupt the Nairobi–Nakuru route, the Mombasa–Nairobi corridor has suffered repeated multi-vehicle collisions in recent weeks, drawing public alarm over slow infrastructural interventions.

![accident salama](https://i.ibb.co/rGvc9ZST/2b4b60c9b3cf.webp)

KeNHA Director of Development Henry Gakuru noted that complex terrain and specific historical road geometry continue to elevate crash probability along select sections. Pending major fiscal outlays, Gakuru indicated the authority is turning to interim speed-calming installations and localized blackspot engineering fixes, alongside appeals for driver restraint.

Yet regular road users continue to report persistent, low-cost maintenance vulnerabilities that do not require multi-billion-shilling dualling budgets. Highway commuters and freight operators routinely face unlit roadworks, missing regulatory signage, unmarked speed bumps, and disabled heavy-duty trucks abandoned for days within active single-lane carriageways.

### Financing Deadlock Moves to Cabinet

Addressing the mounting friction, Transport Cabinet Secretary Davis Chirchir maintained that capital-intensive corridor expansions remain central to the state’s transport policy, with the Mombasa–Nairobi corridor earmarked as a priority once funding models are finalized.

"We have lost a lot of lives on that road, and we hope that when the issue of dualling is addressed, we shall not have the kind of problems we are currently having," Kimeli noted regarding the coastal corridor.

With both the Nairobi–Nakuru corridor expansion progressing through slow project phases and the Mombasa–Nairobi dual-carriageway dependent on external fiscal structuring, the gap between NTSA’s unexecuted low-cost engineering audits and KeNHA’s multi-billion-shilling dualling demands leaves road users navigating unchanged structural hazards.
