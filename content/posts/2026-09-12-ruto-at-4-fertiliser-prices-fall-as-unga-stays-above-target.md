According to official government performance audits and Ministry of Agriculture directives, President William Ruto’s four-year policy shift toward production subsidies has delivered major cost relief to smallholder grain farmers across the country, even as ordinary consumers wait for retail maize flour prices to drop to the administration’s promised Ksh100 mark.

### What we know
* Subsidised fertiliser prices fell from Ksh6,500 in September 2022 to Ksh3,500, then to Ksh2,500, before settling at Ksh2,000 per 50kg bag following an August 2026 directive.
* The government introduced a 50 percent price reduction on certified maize seeds alongside the latest fertiliser price cut.
* Retail prices for a 2kg packet of maize flour dropped from a peak of Ksh230 in 2022 to approximately Ksh180 in 2023, but have remained above the State's ultimate target of Ksh100 to Ksh120.
* The administration dismantled the previous regime’s consumption-based unga subsidy, which cost taxpayers Ksh7 billion in a single month during the 2022 election period.

According to President William Ruto, addressing congregants during an inspection tour in Taita Taveta, crop growers stand to secure higher yields at significantly reduced operational overheads under the expanded input programme.

“I am making an announcement to all our farmers that from next month, in the next two weeks, we are going to subsidise maize seeds by 50 per cent and reduce the cost of fertiliser by another Ksh500,” President Ruto stated.

The Head of State had anchored his agricultural strategy on this approach from his first day in office, stating during his September 13, 2022 inauguration that farmers, rather than retail millers, required immediate Treasury support: “Our priority intervention therefore is to make fertiliser, good-quality seeds and other agricultural inputs affordable and available. There was an attempt to subsidise unga in the run-up to the election, a programme that gobbled up Ksh7 billion in one month, with no impact. If we had spent Ksh7 billion on fertiliser, it would have been a different ball game.”

Upon assuming office four years ago, the Kenya Kwanza administration inherited double-digit food inflation and a retail maize flour price averaging Ksh230 per 2kg packet. Rejecting consumer subsidies as unsustainable, the President pledged that channeling billions into 1.4 million initial bags of planting inputs would naturally lower shelf prices to between Ksh100 and Ksh120 within one year. By February 2023, shelf prices softened to Ksh180, prompting the administration to cite the drop as initial evidence that its supply-side economics were working.

Four years into the administration, rural farming belts in the North Rift, Western, and parts of the Coast have benefited from predictable, cheap inputs that have slashed direct production expenses by more than 60 percent since 2022. However, urban working-class families who spend a disproportionate share of their daily wage on unga have yet to see retail prices settle inside the promised Ksh100-to-Ksh120 corridor. The gap between cheap fertiliser at the farm gate and stubborn commodity prices in retail outlets underscores the structural challenges of milling charges, transport logistics, and distributor margins that continue to buffer consumer prices from the full benefit of subsidized farming.
