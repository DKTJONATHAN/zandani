In an era where digital footprints are meant to be permanent, a recurring crisis in Kenyan politics suggests that some academic records are far more fluid than others. The latest chapter in this long-running saga involves Kimilili MP Didmus Barasa, whose credentials have come under intense scrutiny following a blunt assessment from Jomo Kenyatta University of Agriculture and Technology (JKUAT). On September 14, JKUAT Registrar of Academic Affairs, Aggrey Wanyama, informed the Consumer Federation of Kenya that Barasa’s name is absent from university alumni records, noting that the institution held no graduation ceremony in May 2008—the date cited on the MP’s purported Electronic Engineering certificate.

The scrutiny surrounding Barasa’s qualifications intensified after he announced in June that he had completed a PhD in Public Administration at the Murang’a University of Technology. This claim arrived despite significant inconsistencies in his official biographical data. Over the years, the MP’s National Assembly profile has undergone notable revisions; earlier versions cited a JKUAT engineering degree and a master’s from the University of Queensland, while more recent iterations have replaced these with a diploma from the Kenya Power and Lighting Training School and a degree from Mount Kenya University. Despite the conflicting records, Barasa has maintained his defiance, insisting he holds multiple undergraduate and master’s degrees.

Barasa is merely the latest figure in a trend that has plagued the Kenyan political class for years, where the legitimacy of academic papers frequently clashes with official institutional records. The Commission for University Education (CUE) famously revoked the recognition of Nairobi Governor Johnson Sakaja’s Team University degree in 2022, citing a complete lack of evidence that the governor had ever attended the institution. This pattern of administrative friction highlights a systemic inability to verify the foundational credentials of those in high office.

The legal landscape for these disputes has proven equally treacherous. Mining Cabinet Secretary Hassan Joho previously faced allegations from the Kenya National Examinations Council (KNEC) regarding a forged KCSE certificate, alongside questions from Uganda’s higher education authority regarding his Kampala University degree. However, the matter was effectively closed in 2018 when a Mombasa High Court judge ruled that investigators had violated Joho’s rights, dismissing the case against him.

Similarly, Kapseret MP Oscar Sudi faced nine forgery charges related to an alleged fake KCSE certificate and a KNEC diploma. His legal battle concluded in 2024 when the court found that the prosecution had failed to provide sufficient evidence to secure a conviction. These cases underscore a recurring theme: while regulators and universities may flag discrepancies, the path to legal accountability is often obstructed by procedural hurdles or a lack of definitive proof.

The list of politicians whose academic histories have been challenged is extensive and varied. Former Taita Taveta Governor Granton Samboja remained embroiled in a dispute with Kenyatta University, which consistently maintained that he was never enrolled, though his case was eventually dismissed on jurisdictional grounds. Meanwhile, Meru’s Mithika Linturi faced a challenge from the University of Nairobi regarding the validity of an Indian degree used for admission. While the High Court eventually ruled the university’s challenge unlawful, the original certificate remains missing from the public record.

Even when cases reach the courtroom, they often end in ambiguity rather than resolution. Former Kiambu Governor Ferdinand Waititu’s Punjab University qualification was flagged in court due to discrepancies between the name on the degree and his official identity documents. As these disputes continue to surface, the persistent gap between the credentials claimed by politicians and the records held by the institutions they cite remains a significant point of contention in the integrity of public service.
