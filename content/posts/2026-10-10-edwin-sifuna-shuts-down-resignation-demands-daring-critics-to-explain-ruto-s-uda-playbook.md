Edwin Sifuna is not packing up his Senate office, and he wants those pushing for his exit to revisit how President William Ruto navigated his own political transitions.

Addressing reporters at his Nairobi office on Friday, October 9, 2026, the outspoken Nairobi senator firmly shut down mounting demands from critics who insist he should relinquish his parliamentary seat after launching a rival political vehicle. Instead of bowing to the pressure, Sifuna turned the spotlight directly onto the country’s top office.

“Ask William Ruto. When did William Ruto resign to join UDA? And we all understand that we have to prepare for the next election,” Sifuna said, questioning why political rules appear to shift depending on who is making the move.

The standoff follows Sifuna’s unveiling of The Equitable Party (TEP), the outfit he intends to use to anchor his presidential campaign in the 2027 General Election. As a leading voice in the Linda Mwananchi faction—a grouping of Orange Democratic Movement figures resisting any alignment with Ruto’s re-election machinery—his vocal dissent has ruffled feathers among party loyalists who believe his continued stay in the Senate under the orange banner is untenable.

Yet Sifuna made it clear that while his ambitions have outgrown the current party structure, his legal status remains intact. He insisted he remains a card-carrying member of ODM who was duly elected by Nairobi residents, framing his position not as party property, but as a binding covenant with the capital's voters.

“I’m still a member of ODM and the people of Nairobi elected me on ODM. We have a contract for five years,” Sifuna said. “I am explaining to the public that I will not run on ODM because ODM now is in the hands of William Ruto. Where is the problem with that?”

Beyond invoking precedent, Sifuna anchored his refusal in practical public finance. Stepping down now, with the 2027 General Election just ten months away, would trigger an unnecessary by-election in Kenya’s largest voting constituency, forcing public coffers to shoulder the logistics of a vote that would be superseded within a year.

He warned that enforcing such resignations on all dissenters would create administrative mayhem, effectively compelling the electoral commission to stage an expensive dress rehearsal across multiple jurisdictions.

“If all of us who have decided that we will no longer seek election on ODM decided to resign our positions and go back to the people for re-election ten months to the general election, where do you think that is going to leave the country and the taxpayer? You would have a mini general election,” he stated.

The senator did not mince words regarding what he termed selective outrage from his political opponents. He pointed out that several leaders previously allied to the United Democratic Alliance crossed floor lines to pursue alternative avenues without facing orchestrated demands to surrender their seats.

“It is hypocrisy that that question is only asked to Sifuna. I’ve not heard any other person being asked that question,” he noted, arguing that ODM's current internal realignments have squeezed out any room for genuine competition from within.

For now, Sifuna plans to hold his ground, maintaining his legislative desk in Nairobi while organizing under TEP. According to the senator, his formal resignation from ODM will only land on the table when the clock winds down toward the 2027 national ballot, leaving his critics to debate the ethics of an arrangement he insists is rooted in existing Kenyan political tradition.
