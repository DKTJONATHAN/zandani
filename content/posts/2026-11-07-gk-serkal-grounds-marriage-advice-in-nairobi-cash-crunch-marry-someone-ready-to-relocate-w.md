GK Serkal has anchored his blueprint for modern marriage on a scenario familiar to many city dwellers: being able to tell your spouse that Nairobi has outpaced your pockets, and having them pack bags without resentment.

Sitting down on the conversational show *Lessons at 30* hosted by Dr Ofweneke, the Kenyan personality bypassed standard romantic tropes to tackle the harsh friction between urban lifestyle pressure and long-term domestic stability. For Serkal, the endurance of a marriage is not revealed in comfortable seasons, but in how a couple negotiates a severe economic downturn.

### The Nairobi Relocation Test

To drive his point home, Serkal presented an unvarnished financial scenario that cuts across Kenya's urban middle class: knowing when to fold your hand in the capital, retreat, and reorganize.

"*Ukisema tunahama Nairobi juu hatuna pesa, bibi akubali aseme tu Twende we fix ourselves ndio turudi*," Serkal stated during the sit-down.

In a city where maintaining appearances often consumes household budgets, Serkal argued that a spouse's willingness to swallow pride and execute a tactical relocation is the ultimate sign of solidarity. Rather than viewing financial strain as a personal failure or an excuse for separation, he framed temporary hardship as a joint project that requires total operational alignment between two people.

### Marrying Without Ego

Central to surviving that level of practical strain is choosing a partner whose self-esteem is not dependent on status or competition. Serkal urged singles to look past superficial attraction and choose their best friends—partners who do not interpret personal drive or ambition as an existential threat.

"In life, just marry your best friend; someone who lets you dominate and be you at all costs. Just have someone who doesn’t feel intimidated by you," he noted.

He explained that a functional household requires an environment where both individuals can navigate their own ambitions without being policed or judged. When one partner feels diminished by the other's confidence, daily interactions turn into quiet rivalries that inevitably crumble under broader external pressures.

### The Best-Friend Buffer

By framing marriage around mutual ease rather than rigid performative roles, Serkal emphasized that friendship provides the only dependable buffer against financial shocks. A partner who acts as a genuine companion eliminates the exhausting demand to keep up a facade behind closed doors.

His remarks reposition marriage not as a high-society display, but as an adaptable partnership structured to withstand real-world economic volatility—where the decision to start over from scratch is treated not as a catastrophe, but as an agreed step toward rebuilding.
