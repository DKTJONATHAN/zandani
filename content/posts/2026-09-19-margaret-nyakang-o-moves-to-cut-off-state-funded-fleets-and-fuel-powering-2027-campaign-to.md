Margaret Nyakang’o is moving to pull the plug on one of Kenyan politics’ most familiar backstage open secrets: the habit of running flashy, high-decibel political tours on the taxpayer’s fuel card.

The Controller of Budget has officially stepped into the early buzz of the 2027 general election cycle, issuing a direct directive to curb the covert diversion of government machinery into partisan rallies. Her crosshairs are trained squarely on political figures and county bigwigs who routinely turn official state-funded 4x4s, institutional drivers, and public-sector staff into ready-made campaign entourages.

According to Nyakang’o, the surge in early partisan activity across the counties is no longer just political theater; it is actively stripping resources away from local service delivery. She warned that public property—ranging from government transport fleets to state-bought fuel supplies and administrative personnel—is increasingly being hijacked to stage partisan gatherings under the radar.

### The Paperwork Cloak on the Ground

The mechanics behind these political spectacles, as Nyakang’o outlines, rely on calculated paperwork sleights. Accounting officers across various administrative units submit formal requisitions for funds under legitimate, routine budget lines—such as community outreach, developmental oversight, or project inspections.

Once the funds clear the requisition desk, the money and logistical assets are promptly redirected to bankroll political ground games, mobilization drives, and VIP logistics for campaign rallies. 

Because standard public finance oversight operates on a retroactive quarterly schedule, offices submit their paperwork months after the convoys have already left the tarmac. By the time quarterly reviews catch the anomaly, the fuel has been burned, the mileage logged, and the crowds dispersed. Nyakang’o acknowledged that this built-in lag has long given savvy political actors a structural free pass to fund their public posturing using official accounts.

### Sending Auditors Past the Paperwork Trail

To break the cycle of reviewing campaign expenses long after the applause dies down, Nyakang’o is changing the enforcement playbook. Her office has ordered dedicated field monitoring and evaluation units into counties nationwide to physically check whether disbursed cash corresponds to real, brick-and-mortar work on the ground.

Rather than waiting for end-of-quarter summaries, these mobile teams are tasked with verifying whether allocated funds actually built the clinics, repaired the feeder roads, or supplied the offices listed on requisition sheets—or simply kept political convoys running from stop to stop.

The ground-level offensive reinforces warnings issued earlier in March by the Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission (EACC). The anti-graft watchdog similarly warned political aspirants and sitting incumbents against treating public payrolls and institutional assets as personal campaign reserves ahead of the next electoral showdown.

### Blind Spots Beyond the Consolidated Fund

Yet even as Nyakang’o tightens the leash on visible fleet movements and local county accounts, her scrutiny extends to deeper institutional blind spots where public cash moves far from the main ledger.

The Controller of Budget underscored that her office continues to encounter major visibility hurdles over massive statutory funds operating outside the Consolidated Fund. She singled out mega-entities—including the Social Health Insurance Fund (SHIF), the Primary Health Care Fund (PHCF), the National Infrastructure Fund, and the Sovereign Wealth Fund—as requiring urgent and rigid oversight structures.

Without direct visibility into these expansive off-budget balances, Nyakang’o noted, the risk of massive public funds operating with minimal accountability remains a persistent danger. 

With early 2027 maneuvering already turning up the volume across the country, Nyakang’o’s deployment of ground monitors serves notice to public officers: the days of disguising political roadshows as official county inspections are facing an abrupt operational audit.
