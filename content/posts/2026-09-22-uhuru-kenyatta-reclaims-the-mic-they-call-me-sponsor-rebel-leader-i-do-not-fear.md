Retired President Uhuru Kenyatta is refusing to stay quiet under the weight of political name-calling, declaring he has absolutely no fear of the "sponsor" and "rebel leader" labels directed at him by the current administration.

Speaking on Tuesday, September 22, 2026, during the formal handover of the Jubilee Party leadership in Nairobi, Kenyatta delivered a sharp, highly personal response to President William Ruto’s persistent critiques. He questioned why his name remains the focal point of the country’s top leadership nearly four years after he stepped down.

"He is now calling me sponsor, rebel leader. I will speak. I do not fear," Kenyatta told the gathered party members. "Let them insult me and do what they want."

The former head of state’s unbothered stance comes amid ongoing accusations from the Kenya Kwanza administration framing him as the financial backing behind opposition movements and a "rebel leader" steering political factions ahead of the 2027 General Election. Rather than retreating quietly into retirement, Kenyatta chose the Nairobi party meeting to address the drama head-on, mocking the state's obsession with his brand.

"And after four years, he is still saying Uhuru this, Uhuru that," Kenyatta remarked, calling out the political scapegoating. "Were you given leadership so that you continue to talk about Uhuru? Or were you given leadership to serve Kenyans?"

Kenyatta pointed to the seamless transition of power in 2022 as proof of his democratic compliance, contrasting his quiet exit with the current administration's ongoing fixation with his tenure. "When court said they won, did you see us fight? We gave them all instruments of power in broad daylight. It was not at night, there was no war," he added.

The public outburst coincided with a significant legal transition for the Jubilee Party. Following a September 14 ruling by High Court Justice Lawrence Mugambi, Kenyatta formally handed over the party's leadership. The court’s decision upheld Section 6 of the Presidential Retirement Benefits Act, which bars retired presidents from holding active executive roles in political parties six months past their retirement. While the ruling forces him out of the official party driver’s seat, it permits him to remain an active ordinary member—a loophole that clearly leaves him room to speak his mind.

In a spirited defense of his own ten-year legacy, Kenyatta acknowledged that while no administration is perfect, his team delivered key infrastructural and socio-economic milestones, even when hit by global setbacks like the COVID-19 pandemic. He also revisited the contested Building Bridges Initiative (BBI), maintaining that its core goal was always to build an equitable framework for distributing national resources.

As the Jubilee Party structures adapt to the new High Court realities, Kenyatta has made it clear that stepping back from an official party title does not mean giving up his voice. By openly challenging the "sponsor" narrative, the retired president has signaled that he is more than ready to answer any shade thrown his way.
