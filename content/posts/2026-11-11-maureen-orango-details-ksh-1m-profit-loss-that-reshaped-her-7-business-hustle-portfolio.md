Long before managing a sprawling portfolio of seven enterprises spanning event management, logistics, construction, and renovation, Maureen Orango learned the harshest lesson of her commercial career through a single seven-figure balance sheet.

After hitting a major milestone by securing Ksh 1 million in net profit, Orango watched the liquidity vanish. Without clear financial boundaries between her personal accounts and her business ledger, personal spending and uncalculated loan repayments systematically drained the capital, turning what should have been an expansion springboard into an operational setback.

Speaking during the Co-op Bank Youth Forum hosted by Violetta Ngina under the theme *Beyond One Hustle: The Art of Business Diversification*, Orango used her own misstep to challenge the conventional rush toward multi-venture entrepreneurship.

For Orango, the journey into multi-sector business began in high school, where she sold small perfume samples for Ksh 20. That initial hustle eventually provided the foundation for an event management firm. Rather than launching secondary businesses in unfamiliar markets, Orango built her subsequent enterprises off the immediate demands of her existing client network. Work in event management revealed client needs in site preparation, leading directly into construction and renovation services, which later created operational demand for transport and logistics.

However, expanding a brand ecosystem across seven distinct verticals exposed the hidden traps of cash flow management. Orango warned young business owners against mistaking gross revenue or high-profile partnerships for actual profitability, emphasizing that opening multiple business lines without understanding operating costs can turn active ventures into financial liabilities.

To prevent capital drain, Orango established a strict internal policy: any funds moved from one performing business to support another enterprise must be recorded and treated as a formal, repayable loan rather than free capital. She also urged founders to master daily operations and establish steady customer demand before taking on payroll obligations, arguing that premature hiring often inflates overhead without driving sales.

Her operational warnings land against a tough backdrop for micro, small, and medium enterprises across Kenya. Data from the 2016 Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS) MSME survey revealed that 2.2 million small businesses closed down over a five-year period, with 29.6 percent citing a shortage of operating capital as the primary driver of failure.

More recent figures from the 2023 FinAccess MSE Tracker Survey paint a similar picture of commercial attrition, showing that only 73 percent of small enterprises monitored from a 2021 operational sample remained active by mid-2023. Uncontrollable working capital constraints and falling customer demand remained the primary pressures killing off young ventures.

Orango noted that business diversification does not always require creating entirely new legal entities or sinking fresh capital into unproven industries. Small adjustments to existing service lines—such as a food vendor bundling complimentary utility items to improve customer retention—can yield immediate revenue growth without driving up fixed operating expenses.

Equally critical is knowing when to cut losses. Orango advised entrepreneurs to run routine financial audits and shut down or restructure persistent loss-making ventures rather than allowing them to pull capital from healthier income streams.

As financial institutions continue hosting weekly advisory sessions for young founders navigating high operating costs, Orango's experience points to a simple baseline for local entrepreneurs: successful expansion depends less on launching new hustles and more on controlling the cash generated by the first one.
