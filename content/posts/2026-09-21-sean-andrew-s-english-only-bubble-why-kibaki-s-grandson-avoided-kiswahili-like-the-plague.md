Sean Andrew has always occupied a distinct niche in Nairobi's social landscape—highly visible, quietly aristocratic, and largely detached from the typical political clamor associated with his family name. But a recent sit-down interview has turned the spotlight onto the cultural and linguistic isolation that often defines Kenya's ultra-elite households.

Speaking during an interview with radio presenter Alex Mwakideu, the grandson of Kenya’s third President, the late Mwai Kibaki, candidly detailed how he grew up shielded from the country's primary national languages, admitting he actively shunned Kiswahili during his school years.

The confession offers a rare glimpse into the linguistic bubble of Nairobi’s high-society suburbs, where English remains the undisputed first language, often at the expense of national and indigenous tongues.

### "Avoided It Like the Plague"

For many Kenyan students, Kiswahili is a mandatory academic hurdle. For Sean Andrew, it was a subject to be actively bypassed.

"I didn’t do Swahili really. I avoided it like the plague because I was never really good at it," Sean told Mwakideu.

The digital creator explained that his immediate social environment left very little room for bilingualism. Raised in an environment where English was the default medium of communication for family, friends, and school peers, the pressure to master Kiswahili simply did not exist.

"I grew up speaking English, and everyone around me did too," he added. "I get that it’s a bit embarrassing. I mean, I know enough to survive, just like I do with Kikuyu."

### The Mother-Tongue Barrier

Sean's linguistic detachment extends to his ancestral Kikuyu language. While many young Kenyans navigate a blend of Sheng, Kiswahili, and mother tongue, Sean’s relationship with Kikuyu is strictly receptive. He understands more than he can speak, with his active vocabulary largely confined to basic greetings.

During the interview, he shared a specific linguistic observation that puzzled him about his heritage.

"Even with Kikuyu, I find it funny that there are no direct words for things like ‘sorry’," he remarked.

While the Kikuyu language relies on contextual phrases or borrowed terms like *pole* (from Kiswahili) to express regret, Sean’s observation highlights the disconnect often experienced by third-generation elite youth trying to parse complex indigenous languages through an English-first lens.

### The "Embarrassment" Debate

The interview took an interesting turn when Mwakideu tried to ease Sean's discomfort. The radio veteran reassured him that his lack of fluency in Kiswahili was perfectly fine and "not embarrassing."

However, that attempt at reassurance has since triggered a wider conversation online. Critics and observers have pointed out the irony of a prominent public figure—descended from a presidency that championed national unity and institutionalized Kiswahili—struggling with the national language.

For many on social media, Mwakideu’s quick absolution missed a broader point about cultural preservation. Critics argued that public figures and citizens alike should take active pride in local languages rather than treating them as optional survival tools.

For Sean, the admission remains a candid acknowledgment of the realities of his upbringing. While his late grandfather addressed the nation in polished Kiswahili and Kikuyu throughout a five-decade political career, the third generation of the Kibaki family is navigating a very different, highly globalized Nairobi—one where "survival-level" local language skills are apparently considered just enough to get by.
