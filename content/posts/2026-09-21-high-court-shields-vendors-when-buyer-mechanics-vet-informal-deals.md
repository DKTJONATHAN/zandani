Purchasing commercial equipment through third parties creates an immediate, enforceable contract even without physical signatures, but using independent mechanics to inspect the purchase strips the buyer of statutory defect protections against the vendor, the High Court has ruled.

The determination by Justice Patricia Gichohi establishes that while principals remain bound by commitments made on their behalf, sellers are shielded from fitness-for-purpose liability once a buyer relies on their own appointed experts rather than the vendor’s representations.

Under the doctrine applied by the court, acts carried out by an authorized agent are treated in law as direct actions of the principal. However, the ruling places the operational risk of mechanical failure squarely on the purchaser whenever an agent exercises independent discretion during the selection process.

### The Ksh 920,000 Engine Dispute

The benchmark clarification arose from a commercial dispute where a lorry owner instructed a relative to locate and purchase a replacement engine. The owner handed over a copy of the lorry’s logbook alongside technical specifications for the required unit.

![An image of an agreement document with an insert of a court gavel](https://i.ibb.co/mVhgjNwT/3f0f4334db10.webp)

Acting on those instructions, the relative engaged independent mechanics to inspect and verify an engine, which was subsequently purchased for Ksh 920,000 and fitted into the vehicle. When the engine malfunctioned shortly after installation, the owner attempted to repudiate the transaction, returned the unit, and demanded a complete refund.

The vendor contested the claim, arguing that the transaction involved no direct contractual nexus with the vehicle owner and that the engine had been chosen, vetted, and approved by the buyer’s technical team prior to exchange of consideration.

### The Cost of Independent Vetting

Justice Gichohi rejected the vendor’s claim that absence of personal contact precluded a valid agreement, applying the common law maxim *qui facit per alium facit per se*—he who does anything through another, does it himself.

Because the relative operated within express instructions and the vendor accepted payment and delivered the machinery, a binding contract legally existed between the vehicle owner and the supplier.

![Court](https://i.ibb.co/DfNdXjQZ/82ccb68b2146.webp)

Yet, the court drew an explicit line between the formation of an agreement and product liability. Justice Gichohi ruled that implied warranties regarding merchantable quality and fitness for purpose did not apply because the buyer’s agent and mechanics exercised their own technical assessment rather than relying on the seller's representations.

With the buyer unable to present concrete technical proof that the unit was inherently defective at the point of sale, the seller could not be held responsible for post-installation mechanical issues.

### Correcting the Lower Bench

The judgment intervened on a contradictory finding from the Small Claims Court, which had initially dismissed the lorry owner's claim on grounds that no valid contract existed, yet paradoxically ordered the vendor to liquidate the engine and remit the Ksh 920,000 back to the buyer.

Justice Gichohi set aside that judgment, noting the lower bench committed an error in law by denying the contract while imposing an equitable remedy tied directly to it.

To resolve the commercial deadlock, the High Court ordered the engine sold within three months to recover the Ksh 920,000 for the buyer. Should the sale fail to materialize within the 90-day window, the vendor must pay the full Ksh 920,000 unconditionally within an additional 30 days.
