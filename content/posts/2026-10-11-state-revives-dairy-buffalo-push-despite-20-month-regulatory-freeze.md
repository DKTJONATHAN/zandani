When President William Ruto instructed the State Department for Livestock to begin sourcing improved breeds of buffaloes for milk production, he reopened an agricultural policy track that has remained stalled in administrative silence for nearly two years.

Addressing delegates at the Agriculture and Food Security Transformation Summit at Jamhuri Park in Nairobi on October 8, Ruto framed alternative livestock as an untapped commercial frontier. He directed Principal Secretary for Livestock Development Jonathan Mueke to investigate procurement channels for high-yielding camel and buffalo stock, pointing to metropolitan camel milk prices of Ksh180 to Ksh200 per litre as proof that non-traditional dairying carries viable margins.

Yet behind the executive ambition lies an incomplete regulatory rollout. The state took its first formal step toward domesticating the animal in January 2025, when then-Agriculture Cabinet Secretary Mutahi Kagwe published Gazette Notice No. 977, invoking Section 2 of the Meat Control Act to classify the Asian domesticated water buffalo (*Bubalus bubalis*) as an official food animal.

That legal step altered statutory classifications, but it stopped short of establishing a working trade pipeline.

### The Missing Pilot Framework

![President William Ruto attending the Agriculture and Food Security Transformation Summit at Jamhuri ASK Showground, Nairobi, on Thursday, October 8, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/DDVjwgw6/99ac82c42a35.webp)

In February 2025, the Ministry of Agriculture indicated that the gazettement would anchor a structured two-year pilot project. The trial was meant to establish protocols for cross-border animal importation, mandatory identification systems, disease surveillance, and specialized slaughterhouse licensing before private farmers could handle the species.

Twenty months into that timeline, the ministry has published no documentation showing whether the trial ever began, whether any breeding stock entered the country, or which quarantine facilities were designated to receive them.

The policy distinction between the Asian water buffalo and Kenya's native African buffalo (*Syncerus caffer*) is biologically straightforward. The water buffalo has been bred for draft power, meat, and high-fat milk production across Asia, North Africa, and southern Europe for millennia. Kenya's indigenous buffaloes remain protected wild game managed strictly under wildlife conservation statutes.

However, turning that legal difference into an operating livestock sector requires administrative machinery that remains unbuilt.

### Lingering Legislative Scrutiny

![Domesticated water buffalo at a ranch in India.](https://i.ibb.co/BKfGS9vx/797ce17b2df0.webp)

The lack of operational details previously drew formal pushback in the Senate. In March 2025, nominated Senator Catherine Mumma petitioned the Senate Agriculture Committee to demand disclosure on the evidentiary basis for the classification.

Mumma sought formal records on whether public participation had been conducted, what baseline scientific research supported introducing the foreign ungulate, and how the Ministry of Agriculture was coordinating biosecurity oversight with the Kenya Wildlife Service to protect indigenous fauna from cross-species pathology.

The committee's questions touched on critical technicalities: Kenya possesses sophisticated veterinary control networks for cattle and goats, but lacks specialized feed-conversion standards, certified artificial insemination straws, or veterinary health codes tailored to *Bubalus bubalis*.

### Implementation Vacuum at Kilimo House

Ruto’s summit directive effectively returns the species to the executive agenda without resolving the procedural gaps identified in 2025. Neither Kilimo House nor PS Mueke's office has released a roadmap detailing how the livestock department intends to finance breed procurement, clear import biosecurity approvals, or manage distribution to local breeders.

Without gazetted livestock handling rules and clear import conditions, commercial herds cannot be legally established by private farmers, leaving the executive mandate anchored to a food declaration that remains inactive on paper.
