When a custom outfit is locked in a designer's studio for two months, getting your cash back at the eleventh hour is little comfort if an event is looming. That is the exact logistical bind singer and entrepreneur Akothee put on display this week after a frustrating experience with a wardrobe commission left her empty-handed and searching for answers.

Taking to her online platforms, Akothee laid bare the predicament of waiting sixty days for an outfit only to be told by the designer that a monetary refund was the final option. For a public figure navigating strict appearance schedules, the hurdle is not simply the money paid upfront—it is the impossible scramble to find a skilled replacement when measurements, fabric selection, and bespoke tailoring cannot be rushed.

![Akothee // Instagram](https://i.ibb.co/q3cFxxzs/a7d272722220.webp)

“So a designer has had my dress for two months and has the audacity to tell me that she can refund the money. If I am in a hurry, how do you handle this one?” she wrote.

Unlike picking a piece off a retail rack, commissioning bespoke clothing involves an intricate chain of consultations, precise measurements, fabric sourcing, and multiple fitting stages. Clients place their trust in a creator’s workshop schedule and explicit delivery timelines, assuming that a custom piece is secure long before any public appearance.

When those timelines collapse near the finish line, a refund acts as a financial reset, but it completely ignores the sunk time and the professional inconvenience. Finding a competent artisan capable of replicating a specific vision at short notice remains a tall order in Nairobi’s competitive fashion circles.

The friction highlights a recurring operational hurdle between clients and independent creators: the need for rigid delivery milestones, penalty clauses, and transparent progress updates. Without strict guardrails on custom orders, the burden of a delayed stitch invariably falls squarely on the client racing against the clock.
