President William Ruto arrived at Jamhuri Park with an unorthodox culinary and livestock pitch for Nairobi, turning directly to his Agriculture Principal Secretary to ensure high-yielding foreign camels and Indian water buffaloes touch down in the country.

Addressing the Agriculture and Food Security Summit in Nairobi on Friday, October 9, 2026, the Head of State bypassed bureaucratic platitudes to issue an open, podium-level reminder to Agriculture PS Jonathan Mueke. Ruto made it clear that their behind-the-scenes strategy to overhaul Kenyan dairy herds was no longer up for internal debate.

“I had directed the Agriculture PS to help us bring in a better breed of camel and bring another buffalo breed. Buffaloes also produce a lot of milk. In India, there are buffaloes,” President Ruto told the gathering. “So PS Mueke, do as we had agreed. Bring in buffalo and also bring in a better breed of camel because we need to diversify our milk sources.”

### The KSh 200 Camel Milk Math

Rather than treating camel farming as a remote dryland trade, Ruto framed it as an untapped urban jackpot. He drew direct attention to the commercial premium camel milk already commands in Nairobi’s retail corridors, urging livestock producers to follow the money.

“Here in Nairobi, camel’s milk is selling at about KSh 190 to KSh 200, beating cow’s milk nearly three times. We need to start thinking,” Ruto argued. He noted that beyond commanding triple the shelf value of standard dairy products, camels possess biological drought endurance that keeps output stable when ordinary pasture vanishes.

To complement the camel drive, the President leaned on international commercial models, pointing to India's thriving water buffalo dairy economy as an operational roadmap for Kenya. By introducing superior breeds capable of matching or outproducing regular dairy cattle, the administration hopes to create an alternative milk stream that shields city consumers from harsh weather shocks.

### Supermarket Squeeze and Vanishing Liters

Ruto’s lively pitch comes against the backdrop of an acute squeeze inside the national milk supply chain. Prolonged dry spells have steadily hammered farm yields, triggering an undeniable slide in volumes flowing through licensed dairy processors.

Official delivery records reveal that formal processors took in 84.4 million liters in June 2026, but volumes fell to 81.3 million liters by July. That downward trajectory persisted unchecked across August and September, leaving formal distribution networks strapped for stock.

The real pain has surfaced directly on supermarket shelves and neighborhood kiosks. In urban centers including Nairobi, Mombasa, and Kisumu, a standard 500ml milk packet that previously retailed for KSh 60 to KSh 70 has climbed to between KSh 75 and KSh 80.

### Farmers Walk Away From Cooperatives

The scarcity has also altered how milk moves from rural paddocks to city teacups. With urban prices climbing, smallholder farmers have increasingly bypassed formal dairy cooperatives, refusing to lock their milk into rigid contracts.

Instead, producers are turning to immediate cash, redirecting their evening and morning yields to informal traders and direct neighborhood buyers who readily pay spot cash at premium rates.

By commanding PS Mueke to deliver on their livestock agreement, Ruto is betting that introducing high-producing buffaloes and resilient camel stocks can tame those wild market swings. All eyes now shift to the State Department for Agriculture to see how fast Mueke clears the logistics to land the first imported herds on Kenyan soil.
