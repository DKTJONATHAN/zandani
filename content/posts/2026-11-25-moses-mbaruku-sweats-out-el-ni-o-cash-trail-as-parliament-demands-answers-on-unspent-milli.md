When Principal Administrative Secretary Moses Mbaruku stepped before the National Assembly Departmental Committee on Administration and Internal Security, he walked straight into a ledger wall. Lawmakers wanted a clean, cumulative tally of every shilling channeled into state agencies for anticipated El Niño downpours. What they got instead was a structural dodge.

Saku MP Ali Dido Rasso pinned down the administration's lingering vulnerability: where did the money go, who is actually holding it, and what happens if the rains never arrive? Rasso pressed officials to lay bare the audit trail governing multi-agency preparedness meetings spearheaded by Deputy President Kithure Kindiki.

"What happens if El Niño does not happen? What happens to those monies? Are they going to be returned? How will those monies be accounted for? And maybe you can share with us how much has been disbursed so far," Rasso demanded during the Tuesday, November 24, 2026 sitting.

Mbaruku offered a sharp administrative distinction in response. He defended the Office of the Deputy President by clarifying that while it spearheaded the national mitigation drive, it never directly held the cash. According to the Principal Administrative Secretary, allocations were wired straight to implementing ministries and departments. On the critical question of what happens to unspent balances if the short rains fizzle out, Mbaruku shifted the responsibility squarely to the National Treasury, noting that financial authorities will determine how any leftover funds are handled.

The parliamentary scrutiny exposes the friction points of Kenya's disaster management machinery. Back in July, Kindiki activated a nationwide preparedness framework directing agencies to stockpile relief supplies, secure medical commodities, map out evacuation routes, and protect critical road infrastructure. Yet months into the readiness push, tracking the domestic financial pipeline remains an opaque exercise for oversight committees.

While domestic disbursements remain largely obscured within institutional silos, external support has flowed with clearer metrics. In October, the World Bank approved Ksh51.8 billion ($400 million) under its Rapid Response Option to shore up Kenya's El Niño readiness, support regional health responses, and mitigate energy costs.

As the committee review continues, the immediate financial paper trail rests between the spending ministries that received the direct wires and a National Treasury yet to rule on potential refunds for unspent emergency reserves.
