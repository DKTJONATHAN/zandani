Asman Ambeta and his fellow youth in Shimoni are pivoting from traditional, small-scale fishing toward the formal blue economy following the completion of the Ksh2.6 billion Shimoni Fish Port. The facility, finalized by the Kenya Ports Authority in 2025, features a 75-metre jetty and advanced cold storage designed to process 50,000 tonnes of fish annually.

The port infrastructure includes an ice-making plant and reefer stations intended to stabilize prices and reduce post-harvest losses. For years, fishermen in the region struggled with decaying catches and limited access to lucrative export markets. The new installation aims to retain the value of fish processing within the local economy rather than exporting raw products to external hubs.

Beyond direct fishing, the coastal region is diversifying income streams. Seaweed farming is emerging as a primary source of employment for women, fueling a local supply chain for soaps and oils. These ventures complement existing tourism operations on Wasini Island, offering residents a financial cushion during the off-peak tourism season.

Concerns regarding the project remain centered on community inclusion. Local residents have pushed for transparent recruitment processes, demanding that the port prioritizes local labor and small-scale operators. This reflects a broader regional effort to integrate economic development with local livelihood protection.

The national government has signaled support for these localized efforts through the KSh156 billion Blue Economy Multi-Agency Action Plan. Launched by the six coastal counties for the 2026-2030 period, the plan seeks to bridge the gap between heavy infrastructure investment and the daily needs of citizens for electricity, improved schools, and vocational training.

### The Nairobi read

The concrete slab of a new jetty in Shimoni means very little if the person holding the landing net is still being squeezed by middlemen. Asman’s shift from boat guide to potential processor is exactly the kind of structural change the coast needs, but Nairobi boardrooms have a bad habit of building monuments that look good in an annual report while leaving the locals on the periphery.

If you are stuck in the gridlock on Waiyaki Way, you might think the coast is just a place for a weekend flight or a holiday house. Think again. The Ksh156 billion regional plan is a massive bet on whether the state can actually execute. The real test is not the capacity of the cold storage plant; it is whether the youth of Shimoni get the contracts or if the tenders go to well-connected firms from the capital. Infrastructure is just steel and cement until it puts money into the pockets of the person actually doing the work. If the local community is sidelined during recruitment, this port will become another white elephant that serves international exporters while leaving the fishermen with nothing but the same old low prices and broken nets. We have seen this movie before. The government needs to prove that local ownership is a priority, not just a line in a manifesto.
