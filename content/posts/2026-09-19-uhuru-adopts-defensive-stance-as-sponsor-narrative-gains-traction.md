Former President Uhuru Kenyatta has signaled a pivot in his public communication, moving away from the spontaneous, off-the-cuff remarks that defined his tenure and early retirement. During a burial ceremony in Gikuu, Kiharu Sub-County on Friday, Kenyatta explicitly stated that he is tempering his public speech to avoid potential misinterpretation in an increasingly volatile political environment.

“These days life is becoming too dangerous for me,” Kenyatta remarked, suggesting that the current political climate has necessitated a more guarded approach to public appearances.

This tactical withdrawal from unscripted dialogue follows a sustained campaign by President William Ruto and his allies to frame the former president as the primary financier of opposition activities. By adopting the ‘sponsor’ label-a term frequently used by the current administration to describe Kenyatta’s alleged influence over opposition figures like Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna-Kenyatta is attempting to neutralize the narrative through irony. However, the shift indicates a deeper concern regarding how his words are being weaponized by the executive branch.

President Ruto has consistently used public rallies to draw a line between his administration’s performance and the legacy of his predecessor. During a visit to Sindo, Homa Bay County, on Thursday, Ruto reframed the political fallout between the two leaders as a breach of a long-standing agreement. Ruto alleged that he and Kenyatta had a pact involving reciprocal support for two-term presidential bids, a promise he claims was abandoned.

While Ruto maintains that he does not begrudge Kenyatta’s decision to withhold support, the persistent public invocation of this ‘broken promise’ serves a specific political function: it positions the former president as a destabilizing force rather than an elder statesman. By labeling Kenyatta the ‘sponsor’ of the opposition, the administration effectively links the former president to the current challenges facing the government, including the ongoing debates over economic policy and public protest.

For Kenyatta, the decision to limit his public commentary is a departure from his usual style of direct, often combative, engagement. Whether this change in strategy will succeed in cooling the rhetoric from State House remains to be seen, as the administration shows no sign of abandoning the ‘sponsor’ narrative as a primary tool for consolidation.
