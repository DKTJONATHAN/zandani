Samuel Ngigi did not sugarcoat the numbers when he stepped up to address a room of aspiring young wealth builders on Friday, November 27, 2026. 

For two years, Kenya’s digital youth treated Money Market Funds (MMFs) like an automated money machine, watching fund apps flash double-digit interest rates that peaked between 14 and 17 per cent. But at the latest Co-op Bank Friday Youth Forum, themed *The Future of MMFs: What’s Changing in Money Market Investing?*, Ngigi stepped in with a cold splash of reality: that golden era was an economic fluke, and anyone treating an MMF as a get-rich-quick trick needs a major reset.

Ngigi, the Head of Business Development and Client Service at Co-opTrust Investment Services, made it clear that money market funds were never engineered to make anyone wealthy overnight.

"Investors should view money market funds as an entry point into investing, not a shortcut to doubling their money," Ngigi told the forum, stripping away the social media mystique that has turned unit trusts into the ultimate millennial side hustle.

### The Vanishing 17 Per Cent Era

The slide from dizzying highs down to the current 9 to 11 per cent band has caught many casual savers off guard. Ngigi broke down why the party slowed down. 

Back in 2024, fund managers were riding an unusual wave caused by an inverted yield curve—a rare economic quirk where short-term government debt instruments paid out higher yields than long-term paper. Because MMFs pool cash specifically into short-term paper, managers capitalized on those inflated rates and passed the fat margins down to savers.

That cycle has closed. The Central Bank of Kenya steadily unwound its benchmark lending rate, trimming it from 12 per cent in November 2024 down to 8.75 per cent by October 2026. As the state pays less to borrow short term, the returns paid to funds have naturally collapsed into single digits.

Ngigi pointed out that the current 9 to 11 per cent range is not a failure of fund managers—it is simply the market returning to its natural resting pulse.

### Parking Lot, Not the Finish Line

For young retail savers who piled in thinking their cash would multiply passively, Ngigi reframed the true purpose of a unit trust: it is an emergency cushion and a starter pack, nothing more.

An MMF pools contributions from small-scale investors to buy commercial paper, treasury bills, and bank deposits under the watchful eye of a professional manager. That setup shields savers from having to master bond auctions or stock picking. However, once a saver hits their primary target, Ngigi advised that the cash should not sit there waiting for a miracle.

The real move, he insisted, is using an MMF to establish financial discipline, gather a war chest, and then graduate into diversified assets that fit longer timelines and bigger risk appetites.

### The Bait of Advertised Rates

Ngigi also took aim at yield-chasing behavior. In an era where fund marketing teams splash bold percentage figures on digital posters, he warned young investors against blindly rushing to whichever fund claims the highest daily yield.

Flashy billboard numbers, he cautioned, often obscure the actual health and risk profile of what lies under the hood. 

Instead of taking promotional banners at face value, Ngigi urged savers to demand and scrutinize the fund fact sheet. That single document exposes the real guts of the vehicle: total fund size, exact underlying asset allocations, institutional service providers, and the track record of the fund manager.

Co-opTrust, which runs the Co-op Money Market Fund as the investment arm of Co-operative Bank, has operated under Capital Markets Authority (CMA) and Retirement Benefits Authority (RBA) licensing since 1998. That institutional longevity, Ngigi implied, matters far more when economic tides turn than an extra 0.5 per cent headline yield from an unvetted portfolio.

The broader regulatory framework is moving in the same direction. In October 2026, the Capital Markets Authority floated proposals to introduce independent investment ratings across the unit trust sector. The proposed system would grade fund managers on asset quality, liquidity buffers, and management capacity, making it harder for risky players to mask shaky balance sheets behind eye-popping promotional rates.

For the young crowd who gathered on Friday looking for the next effortless cash multiplier, Ngigi’s takeaway was unmistakable: the windfall era is over, fundamentals are back, and past performance was never a guarantee.
