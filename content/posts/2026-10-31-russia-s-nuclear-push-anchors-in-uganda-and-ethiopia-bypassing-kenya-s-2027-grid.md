East Africa’s civil nuclear map is taking shape around Russian industrial partnerships, but Kenya remains conspicuously absent from the order book even as its landlocked neighbours lock in technical negotiations with Moscow.

Russian state nuclear monopoly Rosatom confirmed on Friday, October 30, that it has opened formal talks to construct large-scale nuclear power stations in four African states: Uganda, Ethiopia, Ghana, and Egypt. The announcement positions Moscow as the primary technology suitor for East Africa’s baseline power expansion, establishing direct bilateral engagement with Kampala and Addis Ababa while advancing separate talks on floating nuclear power units with Tanzania.

The disclosure came immediately after the third Russia-Africa Summit in Moscow, where President William Ruto held sideline discussions with Russian President Vladimir Putin regarding trade, investment, and bilateral economic cooperation. Yet while Nairobi actively courts foreign direct capital from the Kremlin, Kenya was omitted entirely from Rosatom’s roster of active nuclear commercial negotiations.

### Rosatom’s Segmented Continental Push

Rosatom Chief Executive Alexei Likhachev outlined the state corporation’s continental strategy following the summit, noting that Moscow is preparing customized deployment options rather than standardized utility packages.

![Ruto Putin Kremlin](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_inner/public/images/media/ruto-putin-kremlin.jpg.webp?itok=Z3yWGHSx)

"Our partnership is based on the principle that Africa is diverse, and we have a tailored approach for each country that goes beyond a single project," Likhachev stated. 

Beyond large stationary plants in Uganda, Ethiopia, and Ghana—alongside Egypt, which is already building the multi-unit El Dabaa nuclear station under Russian oversight—Moscow is deploying a parallel maritime strategy. Rosatom is marketing floating small modular reactors across Africa, with nine nations expressing formal interest and detailed discussions already underway with Tanzania, Togo, Namibia, and Egypt. 

The inclusion of Tanzania in the floating reactor pipeline, combined with grid-scale discussions in Uganda and Ethiopia, effectively surrounds Kenya with jurisdictions engaging Russian civil nuclear engineering.

### Kenya’s Independent Build Timetable

Kenya’s exclusion from the Russian pipeline does not signal an abandonment of atomic energy, but highlights an unaligned vendor strategy. Under current national energy blueprints, Nairobi plans to construct a 2,000-megawatt nuclear power station in Siaya County, targeting a construction start in 2027 with commercial grid feeding scheduled for 2034.

![ruto-nuclear-sad.jpg](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_style/public/images/media/ruto-nuclear-sad.jpg.webp?itok=srTvkKZ4)

Parallel to the western Kenya commercial plant, the government has scheduled civil works to commence in 2027 on the country's maiden nuclear research reactor at Konza Technopolis. That facility is designed to anchor domestic isotope production, materials testing, and technical workforce training.

However, both Siaya and Konza require massive capital commitments, complex vendor-financing frameworks, and specialized technological transfers. By conducting high-level Kremlin diplomacy without entering Rosatom's formal project pipeline, Nairobi appears to be keeping its vendor options open or looking toward alternative international nuclear partnerships, even as neighbouring governments formalize their reliance on Russian state backing.

### The Operational Reality Across the Continent

Rosatom's rapid expansion of preliminary agreements highlights a stark reality: despite decades of continental ambition, Africa operates just one commercial nuclear installation. South Africa’s two-unit Koeberg station, commissioned in the mid-1980s using French Framatome technology, remains the sole operational nuclear power supplier on the continent.

While Egypt's El Dabaa facility is actively pouring concrete under Russian execution, the bilateral talks with Uganda and Ethiopia represent early-stage project definitions that require extensive site licensing, grid reinforcement, and long-term financing models.

For Kenya, the next critical test arrives in 2027, when groundbreaking dates for both the Siaya facility and the Konza research reactor will require concrete procurement and technology partnership commitments to be locked in, whether with Moscow or alternative global vendors.
