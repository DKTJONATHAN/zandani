Former President Uhuru Kenyatta has signaled a strategic withdrawal from spontaneous public commentary, citing an increasingly hostile political climate. Speaking at a burial ceremony in Gikuu, Kiharu Sub-County, on Friday, the former head of state suggested that his standard practice of speaking without a prepared script has become a liability.

“These days life is becoming too dangerous for me,” Kenyatta told mourners, framing his decision to curb his remarks as a measure to avoid the misinterpretation of his words by the media and political opponents. He concluded his brief address with a pointed reference to the ‘sponsor’ label that has become a staple of the current administration’s rhetoric against him.

The remarks arrive at a moment of heightened friction between the current and former administrations. President William Ruto has spent the week intensifying his criticism of his predecessor, most notably during a rally in Sindo, Homa Bay County, on Thursday. There, Ruto directly accused Kenyatta of financing opposition figures, specifically naming Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna, and characterized the former president as a man embittered by the current government's economic performance.

Beyond the allegations of financial interference, the President used the Sindo platform to lay bare the grievances surrounding their fractured political alliance. Ruto detailed a long-standing expectation of reciprocal support, claiming there was a prior agreement for a decade-long tenure for each leader. He described a sense of betrayal, asserting that while he stood by Kenyatta through four election cycles, the favor was not returned when he sought the presidency.

While the President stated that Kenyatta held the democratic right to withhold that support, the persistence of the ‘sponsor’ narrative serves a specific purpose in the current political cycle. By framing Kenyatta as the architect of the opposition’s movements, the administration effectively links the former president to the economic challenges the government is currently attempting to address.

For Kenyatta, the shift toward a more guarded public persona marks a departure from his previous, more combative responses to the Kenya Kwanza administration. Whether this tactical silence will shield him from further executive scrutiny remains to be seen, as the government continues to center its political messaging on the alleged failures and lingering influence of the previous regime.
