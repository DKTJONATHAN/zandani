The calendar turned past October 2026 without the planned opening of the Rironi-Naivasha section of the multibillion-shilling Rironi-Mau Summit Highway, as a nationwide shortage of bitumen brought paving operations on the corridor to an unexpected halt.

The missed timeline on the country's busiest transit artery has laid bare an industrial vulnerability that dates back over a decade: Kenya does not manufacture a single barrel of the binding material required to lay highway tarmac.

While public attention on major infrastructure projects routinely focuses on financing structures, heavy machinery, and earthmoving milestones, the final and most critical phase of road surfacing depends entirely on bitumen—a dense petroleum residue that contractors cannot substitute with local materials.

### The 2013 Refining Vacuum

Kenya previously maintained domestic production capacity for bitumen through the state-backed oil refinery in Mombasa, which extracted the heavy substance as an industrial byproduct during crude oil distillation.

![Road construction and tarmac laying along the Rironi-Mau Summit highway near the Ngarariga Interchange.](https://i.ibb.co/wrrWBm6d/49773fe599c7.webp)

That domestic buffer disappeared in 2013 when the Mombasa facility ceased refining crude oil. In the absence of an operational distillation plant, the country was forced into total reliance on external markets for every road surfacing project undertaken over the past decade.

Today, Kenya imports 100 percent of its bitumen requirements. To keep multi-lane expressways, trunk corridors, and rural access routes moving, road builders rely on an import network anchored across the Middle East and Asia.

The supply pipeline depends heavily on shipments originating from the United Arab Emirates, Iran, Oman, China, Kuwait, Iraq, and India. Iran remains the single largest global exporter of the commodity, driven by its vast crude reserves and extensive refining infrastructure.

When disruption hits shipping routes, trade financing, or export allocations across these petroleum-refining hubs, Kenyan paving crews are left waiting on docks and storage depots for the specialized liquid to arrive.

### The Engineering Behind the Bottleneck

In highway engineering, the distinction between bitumen and asphalt defines whether a cleared sub-base becomes an operational highway or remains an unfinished gravel strip.

Bitumen is not finished tarmac; it is the hydrocarbon binder derived from the bottom layers of crude distillation after lighter fuels such as petrol, diesel, and kerosene are boiled off at higher temperatures. Composed primarily of carbon and hydrogen alongside sulfur, oxygen, nitrogen, and trace metals, it serves as the adhesive in road engineering.

![An image of ongoing construction works on the Rironi-Mau Summit captured on September 23, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/kVzgwfHj/43e17366910c.webp)

Asphalt, by contrast, is the finished compound: a precise mixture where heated bitumen coats aggregates of crushed stone, gravel, and sand. Acting as an industrial glue, the bitumen holds these stone aggregates in place. As the compound cools, it hardens into a rigid matrix designed to absorb the crushing axial loads of commercial heavy haulage.

Beyond load bearing, the material provides structural waterproofing. By sealing the aggregate mixture against water penetration, bitumen stops moisture from seeping into underlying gravel layers, preventing road surfaces from softening and collapsing into potholes during heavy rains.

Because no other binding agent offers comparable water resistance and structural elasticity at commercial scale, contractors facing a supply dry-up cannot improvise alternative solutions without compromising road longevity.

### Extended Timelines on the Horizon

The supply crunch that derailed the end-of-October delivery for the Rironi-Naivasha strip highlights the fragility of Kenya's synchronized infrastructure drive, where multiple mega-projects compete for imported raw materials under tight completion schedules.

With domestic refining capacity dormant for more than 13 years, the timeline for completing the remaining segments of the Rironi-Mau Summit corridor hinges less on local site readiness and more on the arrival schedules of maritime tankers discharging heavy crude residues at the port.
