To bypass prohibitive therapy bills and months-long clinic waiting lists, a new digital platform launched in Nairobi is attempting to convert children’s daily screen time into a home-based developmental tracking system.

Developed by Kenyan psychologist and neurodiversity specialist Tabby Wangechi Ngeru, the application—named Qiotter—targets families managing conditions including autism, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, and dyslexia. Rather than attempting to replace professional clinicians, the tool shifts primary observational duties to parents, leveraging daily phone and tablet interactions to generate structured, personalized exercises.

![Qiotter founder Tabby Ngeru](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_inner/public/images/media/tabby-ngeru.jpg.webp?itok=XUl6s6Tl)

Ngeru, who is associated with the educational support organization Beyond Brain Barriers, rolled out the platform following a July launch. The platform addresses a systemic barrier in Kenya's healthcare system: specialized neurodevelopmental evaluations and continuous therapy sessions remain financially out of reach for most households, while clinical care is often poorly coordinated with daily school and home environments.

The app's operational design draws its name from *kiota*, the Swahili word for nest. Under its model, caregivers create a master profile entering initial baseline data about their child's specific developmental needs, communication patterns, and learning styles. The platform then generates daily customized activities delivered through animated, child-friendly modules directly to the child’s interface.

Concurrently, caregivers use a dedicated portal to log behavioral shifts, emotional regulation markers, and cognitive responses over extended periods. This continuous logging is intended to capture gradual developmental changes that brief, periodic clinical appointments routinely miss.

![Qiotter founder Tabby Ngeru, with an inset of social media apps](https://i.ibb.co/G3nfGM5z/4bbd77e458ad.webp)

Central to the platform's automation is an artificial intelligence assistant called NIA. According to Ngeru, NIA is currently undergoing specialized training to process and protect sensitive pediatric behavioral data while tailoring daily activity recommendations to individual learning speeds.

By embedding specialist-guided exercises into routine domestic habits, the system attempts to resolve the operational friction between home routines, classroom instruction, and formal therapy. The platform allows caregivers to export observation logs, creating a unified record that can be shared across school teachers and clinical therapists to align intervention strategies.

As caregiver adoption expands following the platform's initial rollout, the core test for Qiotter will depend on the deployment of its NIA assistant—specifically how effectively its privacy safeguards handle sensitive child data while maintaining reliable daily routines across varying household bandwidths.
