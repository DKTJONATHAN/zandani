Eastern Region Meteorological Services Deputy Director Richard Muita stepped onto the Spice FM studio mic on Wednesday, November 11, 2026, to address a growing sense of public confusion across Nairobi: why the heavy El Niño downpours anticipated by global climate models had yet to flood the capital.

Facing an anxious public primed for severe seasonal rains, Muita used the live radio broadcast to draw a clear line between high-level global phenomena and the immediate atmospheric conditions on the ground. The meteorologist pointed out that nationwide expectations had been raised significantly by global projections from the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), which pointed to a near 100 percent likelihood of El Niño conditions developing worldwide.

However, Muita explained to listeners that a global climate driver does not operate as an automated weather delivery system for East Africa.

“We need to understand what El Niño is,” Muita stated during the Spice FM interview. “It is one of the factors causing changes in weather around the globe. We have not received rainfall as forecast because El Niño is just a factor that drives rainfall patterns across the world, and it is not rainfall itself; that should be clearly distinguished.”

Addressing Nairobi residents who have questioned why seasonal rains remained delayed or lighter than anticipated, Muita broke down the dual-ocean mechanics governing regional weather systems. While El Niño originates thousands of miles away in the warm surface waters of the central and eastern Pacific Ocean, Kenya’s immediate coastal moisture relies heavily on conditions in the neighboring Indian Ocean.

According to Muita, heavy rainfall across East Africa requires a synchronized ocean push. When strong Pacific El Niño warming coincides with elevated sea surface temperatures along the East African coast—a pattern tied to the Indian Ocean Dipole—Kenya typically sees heavy, widespread rainfall. But when Pacific temperatures rise without matching warmth in the Indian Ocean, the atmospheric trigger fails to fire locally.

“For us to achieve the much rainfall that we anticipated, normally it works very well with other factors like the Indian Ocean dipole,” Muita explained on air. “And because its main indicator is temperature, the temperature on the coast of East Africa, once it is kind of in agreement and is higher than normal, then normally it is on the spot. However, when we have strong temperatures around the Pacific, and the Indian Ocean is not as good, then that combination does not lead to much rainfall.”

Muita’s radio intervention comes as commuters, businesses, and municipal planners across Nairobi adjust to a drier reality than early warnings suggested. Rather than an immediate seasonal deluge, local weather outcomes remain bound to how closely East Africa's coastal waters align with broader global shifts.
