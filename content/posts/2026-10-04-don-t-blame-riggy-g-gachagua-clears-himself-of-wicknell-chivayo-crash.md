Rigathi Gachagua took to an American church podium in Boston with an unprompted defensive plea for President William Ruto: do not pin the fatal helicopter crash of flamboyant Zimbabwean tycoon Sir Wicknell Chivayo on him.

Speaking before a gathering of clergy members, the former deputy president turned an overseas address into an unfiltered, high-octane storytelling session. Without producing documentary records, Gachagua spun an intricate tale featuring cross-border dollar drops, private airstrips, and an eye-popping KSh 1.5 trillion fortune that he claimed vanished into thin air when Chivayo’s helicopter went down.

Yet before walking the room through his financial claims, Gachagua made sure to enter his own alibi into the public record.

“William Ruto, please don’t blame Riggy G. I didn’t kill this guy,” Gachagua told the congregation. “I was in the United States. The accident happened in Zimbabwe. Surely I have nothing to do with it.”

### The Airstrip Drops and the KSh 1.5 Trillion Claim

Chivayo, a flamboyant Harare socialite and businessman known across the region for flaunting luxury car fleets and high-value government tenders, died alongside his wife Lucy Muteke and four others when their helicopter crashed in the Marondera Rural area, east of Harare. Zimbabwean President Emmerson Mnangagwa formally confirmed the deaths, with aviation investigators noting that a post-impact fire broke out at the wreckage site.

To Gachagua, however, the fatal crash was not merely a tragedy in southern Africa—it was the sudden collapse of an alleged financial conduit.

Taking advantage of his former station inside the executive, Gachagua told the Boston audience that his insider status gave him direct visibility into what he characterised as clandestine, cross-border operations between Ruto and the late tycoon.

“There was a business partner of his, a crook from Zimbabwe called Chivayo. This man, because I was his deputy, so I know, they were doing business together,” Gachagua claimed. “He is a man he has been using for money laundering. He gets money in dollars, that man comes with a plane, lands in a private airstrip and takes the money to keep for him.”

According to Gachagua's version of events, the sudden air disaster has thrown State House into an unresolvable accounting headache, leaving the head of state unable to recover astronomical personal holdings.

“The man has his money, over 1.5 trillion,” Gachagua asserted. “The man is dead, his wife is dead, he doesn’t know where to begin.”

### Broadening the Script to 2027

Never one to keep his monologues confined to ledger entries, Gachagua quickly widened his narrative from cash storage to Kenya’s upcoming 2027 General Election. From the Boston pulpit, he painted the deceased Zimbabwean businessman as an operative hired to engineer an electoral outcome in Nairobi on behalf of an international technology vendor.

“That guy is an expert in stealing elections, and he is the one who has been burglarising elections in Africa,” Gachagua told the room, alleging that procurement guidelines back home were being massaged to hand election technology tenders to an unnamed South Korean firm fronted by Chivayo. “So this guy was a local agent for those fellows. So this is the man he was to use to steal the vote. The guy is dead.”

Much like the claim involving the KSh 1.5 trillion stash and the private runway deliveries, Gachagua provided no paperwork, tender correspondence, or digital footprints linking Chivayo or foreign election technology companies to Kenya’s electoral agency.

Neither State House nor representatives speaking on behalf of President Ruto have issued any formal response to Gachagua's diaspora remarks, leaving the former deputy president's cinematic script lingering unchallenged across the Atlantic.
