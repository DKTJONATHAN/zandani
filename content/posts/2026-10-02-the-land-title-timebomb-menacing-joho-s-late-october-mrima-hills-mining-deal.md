The Ministry of Mining’s rush to finalize international bidding for the Mrima Hills critical mineral deposits by late October is heading toward a direct collision with a systemic land-tenure crisis in Kwale County. While the government prepares to select a foreign concessionaire to tap into some of the world's most sought-after minerals, hundreds of families in Lungalunga Sub-County remain legally unprotected, living on inherited ancestral land without formal title deeds.

At least six international mining firms from the United States, China, and Australia are currently competing for the rights to develop the Mrima Hill deposit. The site is highly prized for its documented reserves of niobium—a key metal used in high-strength alloys and superconducting magnets—alongside potential deposits of cobalt, copper, and nickel. 

Mining Cabinet Secretary Hassan Joho recently confirmed that the state is in advanced bilateral talks with the United States to establish domestic processing plants and refineries, with a view to exporting refined minerals directly to the American market. A prominent bid has already emerged from an Australian-led consortium featuring RareX and Iluka Resources, which has advanced to the Request for Proposals (RFP) stage, with detailed submissions due this month.

![Mrima Hill](https://i.ibb.co/CdTj5tX/fa61712c4ca6.webp)

Yet, as the state moves at high speed to secure these global partnerships, the legal ground beneath the project remains highly unstable. Underneath the geopolitical interest lies a complex web of unresolved land ownership. 

According to civil society representatives from Haki Madini Kenya, the vast majority of the land surrounding the mineral-rich Mrima Hills has been passed down through generations without formal legal succession. Because these inheritance processes have never been officially completed, local families do not hold the registered titles required to negotiate fair compensation or assert legal rights if the project forces relocations.

This legal vulnerability has triggered widespread anxiety across Lungalunga. Local residents and human rights groups warn that without immediate state intervention to resolve the land registry backlog, any contract signed with a foreign bidder will lead to forced evictions and inadequate compensation.

![A collage of US President Donald Trump (left) and President Willian Ruto (right), with a hand holding rare-earth minerals in the middle.](https://i.ibb.co/9956pthD/e404a72599dd.webp)

Justus Mweo Mwachupa, a Mrima Hills resident, pointed out that the community has been kept entirely in the dark about how the extraction will be executed and what environmental safeguards will be put in place. The lack of transparency has fueled fears of displacement in a region that has historically struggled with land marginalization.

To counter this, local advocacy organizations are attempting to organize the community before the state-imposed October deadline. Mariam Omar, an activist with the human rights group HUDA, stated that the organization is actively working to educate residents on their constitutional rights and the legal roles they must play in any impending mineral deal. The goal, Omar noted, is to ensure that the community has direct access to the decision-makers and a clear understanding of who is legally accountable for the project's local impact.

The immediate test of this standoff will come at the end of October 2026, when the Ministry of Mining is scheduled to conclude its bidding process and announce its preferred partner. Whether the state will pause to address the legal status of the land occupied by its citizens, or press forward with international contracts despite the domestic ownership vacuum, remains the critical question for the families of Kwale.
