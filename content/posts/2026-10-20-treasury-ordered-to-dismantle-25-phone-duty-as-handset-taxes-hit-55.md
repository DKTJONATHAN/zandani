Barely three months after a regional trade agreement pushed total taxes on imported smartphones to an estimated 55.5 percent, the National Treasury has been ordered to draft immediate legislation dismantling the baseline 25 percent customs duty.

The directive, issued by President William Ruto on Tuesday during the 63rd Mashujaa Day celebrations at Kipchoge Keino Stadium in Eldoret, forces an abrupt policy shift on consumer electronics taxation. The targeted 25 percent import duty took effect on July 1, 2026, following the East African Community’s (EAC) revised Common External Tariff, a move that sharply inflated the landing costs of entry-level and commercial handsets.

![President William Ruto attending the 63rd Mashujaa Day celebrations at the Kipchoge Keino Stadium in Eldoret, Uasin Gishu County, on Tuesday, October 20, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/QFFV1HGs/bbe75b52d9c3.webp)

Under the existing revenue structure, the import duty sits at the base of a heavily layered tax stack. Imported devices face a compounding series of statutory charges: a 10 percent excise duty, a 16 percent Value Added Tax (VAT), and a 2 percent Railway Development Levy (RDL). According to prior National Treasury estimates, the combined effect of these charges drove aggregate tax exposure on imported phones to 55.5 percent of their declared value.

Addressing the national gathering, Ruto framed the punitive fiscal barrier as counterproductive to employment and commerce, arguing that mobile technology has ceased to be a discretionary consumer item.

“As you all know, a mobile phone is no longer a luxury,” Ruto said. “It is a classroom, a marketplace, a bank and an office for many young Kenyans and a gateway to employment.”

![File image of Kenyans using smartphones](https://i.ibb.co/kVwj1L53/2d2a0a6b83e2.webp)

The Head of State affirmed that the executive would initiate the formal legal and regional customs adjustments required to strike out the tariff, asserting that lower acquisition costs are vital for expanding participation in the digital economy.

However, unwinding the tariff involves technical and bilateral complexities. Because the 25 percent levy stems from the EAC’s regional customs framework, Kenya must navigate community-level customs derogations or exemptions alongside domestic statutory amendments to avoid trade friction with regional partners.

While the presidency did not release an operational date for when consumers might see lower shelf prices at retail outlets, the calendar for legislative action is immediate. The National Treasury has been directed to table the necessary amendment proposals before Parliament next week, transferring the task of untangling the multi-layered tariff structure to lawmakers.
