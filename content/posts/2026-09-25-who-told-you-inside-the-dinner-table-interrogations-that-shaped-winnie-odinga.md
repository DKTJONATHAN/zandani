Tell Raila Odinga something with absolute confidence, and within five minutes, you would be questioning your own sanity. 

That was the domestic reality painted by East African Legislative Assembly (EALA) Member of Parliament Winnie Odinga on Thursday, September 24, 2026, as she stepped up to the podium at the Nairobi International Book Fair. Representing her family, Winnie was there to accept a posthumous honor for her late father, former Prime Minister Raila Odinga, who was inducted into the Kenya Publishers Association Hall of Fame for his lifelong contributions to governance, political literature, and democratic discourse.

Rather than leaning into standard political platitudes, Winnie pulled back the curtain on what it was like living with a mind defined by relentless curiosity. 

“My father was curious. Extremely curious,” Winnie told the audience gathered at the exhibition grounds. “You could tell him something confidently, and he would ask why. You would explain, and he would say, ‘Who told you?’ You would answer, then, ‘Have you checked?’ By the end of the conversation, you are not sure of something you had been certain about five minutes before.”

That probing, Socratic method was not deployed to intimidate or disarm adversaries for sport, she noted. Instead, it served as a domestic and professional sorting mechanism designed to cultivate independent thought, analytical rigor, and intellectual honesty among his colleagues, advisors, and children alike.

In a political landscape often saturated with unquestioning loyalty and performative agreement, the former premier actively disdained sycophancy. He preferred the friction of engaging with individuals who took the time to verify their facts before drawing conclusions.

The Hall of Fame induction recognizes Raila Odinga's enduring footprint on Kenya's publishing and political literature spaces—a realm where ideas, texts, and ideological battles were documented over decades of opposition and governance.

As the book fair ceremony concluded, Winnie’s recollections left behind a vivid portrait of an icon whose most demanding classroom was not a public stadium or a parliamentary chamber, but the private space where he forced those closest to him to prove they actually knew what they were talking about.
