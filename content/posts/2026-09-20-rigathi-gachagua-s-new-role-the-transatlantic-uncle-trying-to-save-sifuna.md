Rigathi Gachagua has spent much of his public life operating as a fierce, localized political operator. But from his current vantage point in the United States, the former Deputy President is testing out a radically different persona: the concerned, pan-ethnic "political uncle" dishing out survival advice to Nairobi's younger generation of opposition leaders.

Speaking during a Saturday visit to the US, Gachagua revealed that he has taken it upon himself to personally dial into the ongoing internal friction within the Linda Mwananchi movement. His target? Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna and Embakasi East MP Babu Owino, two of the country's most vocal young politicians whose ambitious alliance has recently shown visible signs of strain.

Instead of his trademark aggressive rhetoric, Gachagua described a paternalistic intervention, painting himself as an experienced guide warning his younger counterparts about the realities of statecraft.

"I placed a call to Sifuna from here, had a long chat with him. I did the same with Babu and I told them: 'Look here, you young guys, your movement is being infiltrated,'" Gachagua disclosed, adopting the tone of a seasoned mentor checking in on headstrong proteges.

His long-distance advice comes as the Linda Mwananchi movement grapples with its own internal celebrity drama. While the political vehicle has positioned Sifuna as its preferred presidential candidate for the 2027 General Election, backstage tensions have simmered. Sifuna’s allies have quietly accused Babu Owino of attempting to crowd the Nairobi Senator out of his own spotlight, though Babu has repeatedly denied the claims, insisting his support for Sifuna’s ambitions remains intact.

To hear Gachagua tell it, these internal squabbles are not merely organic rivalry, but a manufactured script. He claimed that a recent Linda Mwananchi street march through Nairobi’s Jacaranda area sparked enough anxiety in high offices to trigger a National Security Council meeting, where he alleges the National Intelligence Service (NIS) was instructed to sow discord. Gachagua offered no public evidence to back up the claim.

Beyond the strategic advice, Gachagua’s sudden transition into a champion of cross-ethnic harmony represents a striking pivot. Once known for declaring that the government belonged primarily to its key "shareholders," he is now using his transatlantic platform to preach broad-based solidarity.

He warned Sifuna and Babu—who represent key Luhya and Luo constituencies—not to let their political rivalry take on ethnic undertones, warning that a "divide and rule" strategy would only benefit State House. He then extended this appeal to a broader reconciliation, calling for a political truce between the Luo and his own Kikuyu community.

"The Luos and the Kikuyus are the same people. They are suffering in Kenya like all other Kenyans," Gachagua argued, a far cry from the exclusionary regional rhetoric that characterized his rise to the deputy presidency.

By framing himself as the protective elder statesman trying to keep Sifuna and Babu from falling into tactical traps, Gachagua is attempting to carve out a new niche of diaspora-based moral authority. Whether Nairobi’s two loudest political stars will actually take relationship advice from a deposed deputy president, however, remains the unresolved question as the road to 2027 begins to narrow.
