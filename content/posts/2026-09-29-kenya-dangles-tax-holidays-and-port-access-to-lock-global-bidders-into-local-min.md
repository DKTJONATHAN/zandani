Kenya is prepared to offer significant fiscal concessions and exclusive infrastructure access to secure a foothold in the global green energy supply chain. 

The government is dangling tax holidays, custom-designed Special Economic Zones (SEZs), and dedicated port access to force international mining conglomerates to process critical minerals locally rather than exporting them as raw ore.

This strategy is the linchpin of advanced negotiations with the United States government, which involve the U.S. Departments of State, Commerce, and Defence. According to Mining Cabinet Secretary Hassan Joho, these bilateral talks are deeply intertwined with a high-stakes bidding process for the development of Mrima Hill in Kwale County.

The Kwale deposit, which holds substantial reserves of niobium alongside indications of cobalt, copper, and nickel, has attracted intense interest. Six firms from the United States, Australia, and China are currently competing for the extraction rights. 

By tying the bilateral talks directly to the Mrima Hill tender, Nairobi is using one of its most valuable mineral assets as leverage to demand domestic refining infrastructure.

### Trading Tax Revenue for Industrial Capacity

To make domestic processing commercially viable for foreign investors, the government is assembling a heavy package of state-backed incentives. Under the proposed framework, winning developers will not just be granted extraction licenses; they will be required to construct and operate local processing plants.

In return, the state will extend significant relief. 

"There has to be all the necessary incentives that the government will provide, such as special economic zones, port access, support infrastructure, and tax concessions," Joho said. 

This shift in policy represents a calculated fiscal trade-off. While the Treasury may lose out on immediate tax revenues from raw exports, the administration hopes that local value addition will catalyze a manufacturing ecosystem around clean energy materials.

### The Geopolitical Squeeze

The diplomacy behind the deal has moved rapidly. The current negotiations follow high-level talks in New York between President William Ruto and U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, where both nations discussed value-addition opportunities.

Washington has actively backed Kenya’s insistence on local refining. U.S. Assistant Secretary of State for African Affairs Frank Garcia recently confirmed that American firms are prepared to support local processing, aligning with U.S. efforts to secure supply chains for critical minerals outside of dominant Chinese processing networks.

However, the presence of Chinese and Australian firms in the Mrima Hill bidding pool ensures that the contest remains highly competitive. The Kenyan government is using this multi-national interest to ensure that whoever wins the concession is fully committed to the local processing mandate.

Currently, the mining sector contributes a mere one percent to Kenya's Gross Domestic Product (GDP). The Ministry of Mining has set an ambitious target to raise this contribution to 10 percent within the next ten years. Officials acknowledge that this target is mathematically impossible if minerals continue to leave the port of Mombasa in their raw, low-value state.

The commercial appetite for Kenya's "processing-first" policy faces an immediate test next month. The government expects to conclude the bidding evaluation for the Mrima Hill project in late October 2026, after which it will enter final negotiations with the preferred bidder to establish the country's first major critical mineral refining partnership.
