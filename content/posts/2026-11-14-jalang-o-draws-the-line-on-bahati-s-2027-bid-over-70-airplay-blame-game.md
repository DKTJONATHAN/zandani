Lang’ata Member of Parliament Phelix Odiwuor, popularly known as Jalang’o, is stripping Kevin Bahati of his celebrity shield, warning the musician that securing an elective seat requires actual constituent delivery rather than studio banter and name-dropping sitting legislators.

The blunt public dressing-down directly addresses Bahati’s renewed political ambitions ahead of the 2027 General Election. Bahati, who is mounting a second bid for the Mathare parliamentary constituency, recently escalated his campaign rhetoric by directly questioning the institutional contributions of creative-industry figures who successfully made the leap from showbiz into the National Assembly.

### The Obinna Show Provocation

The dispute ignited during Bahati’s recent studio appearance on *The Obinna Show*, where the singer sought to frame his upcoming campaign around systemic advocacy for the creative sector. In his sit-down, Bahati pointed fingers at Jalang’o and former Starehe MP Charles Njagua, known as Jaguar, accusing the pair of abandoning the entertainment fraternity once they entered the corridors of power.

Bahati argued that both figures held the legislative leverage to transform the financial fortunes of Kenyan musicians by pushing through mandatory domestic broadcast minimums, but chose not to.

“Jalas and Jaguar were in parliament, and they are the reason I am being shown videos of artists who went to Parliament but failed to support the industry,” Bahati claimed during the show. “What was so hard for Jalang’o or Jaguar to do that in Parliament? Just a bill to say if we want to create other musicians, we must say a local station will have no option but to play 70 per cent local, and that is how an unknown artist in Kisumu will get rotation and will be big because of enough airplay.”

Pitching the statutory quota of 70 to 80 percent airplay as the central pillar of his own legislative ambitions, Bahati attempted to position his 2027 bid as an overdue corrective to the perceived inaction of his entertainment predecessors.

### Jalang'o Counters the Talk-Show Playbook

Jalang’o offered a sharp counter-offensive, dismissing Bahati’s critique as textbook publicity seeking. The Lang’ata lawmaker stated that running an effective parliamentary office cannot be handled like an online influencer rollout, demanding that Bahati halt using established leaders as leverage to raise his personal campaign profile.

“Bahati tried his hands on politics, and the one thing I will tell him is do not use other people’s names for your clout chasing; you do not know what it takes for somebody to be elected a leader,” Jalang’o said. “Do your interviews, and clout chase your ways. Politics is not about clout chasing; it is about serious work being done.”

Pivoting the conversation away from broadcast quotas and onto constituent deliverables, Jalang’o challenged the Mathare hopeful to move out of broadcast studios and take an assessment tour through Lang’ata to observe constituency management at close quarters.

“Come here to Lang’ata and see the work we have been able to do,” Jalang’o said. “People of Lang’ata are very happy about the work we have been able to do, so when he thinks he can come into politics to clout chase, these are people’s lives. These are people who depend on you to deliver.”

### The Shift From Stage to Ballot

The friction exposes the deeper challenge faced by artists attempting to pivot from popular digital followings into representative politics. While Bahati first mounted a high-stakes campaign for Mathare in 2022—navigating party ticket disputes before falling short at the ballot box—Jalang’o successfully captured the Lang’ata seat on his debut run.

For Jalang'o, Bahati’s reliance on podcast critiques underlines a refusal to adapt to how voters evaluate candidates once the initial excitement of celebrity fades. By pointing out the practical realities of winning and maintaining public office, Jalang’o made it clear that entertainment camaraderie ends at the boundary of electoral competition.

“From now, it is no longer just Bahati; it is Bahati, an aspirant running his campaign just like me,” Jalang’o said. “So atafute yake, awachane na yetu.”

With Bahati formalizing his intentions for Mathare three years ahead of the 2027 poll, the debate over whether former creatives in Parliament owe targeted legislative handouts to their former industry—or must focus entirely on their direct geographical constituents—remains a central fault line in his emerging campaign.
