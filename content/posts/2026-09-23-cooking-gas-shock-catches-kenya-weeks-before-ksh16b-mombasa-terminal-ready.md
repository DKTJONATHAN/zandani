Kenyan households are set for an immediate spike in cooking gas prices next month, exposing a sharp timing gap as the country’s massive new Ksh16 billion storage buffer in Mombasa remains weeks away from commercial commissioning.

The price hike, driven by a dramatic collapse in Middle East gas shipments, highlights how vulnerable local kitchen budgets remain to global geopolitical friction, even as regional energy infrastructure projects near completion.

### The Global Price Spike

Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG) is produced by blending propane and butane, two commodities whose global prices are highly sensitive to shipping disruptions. Recent data from Saudi Aramco, a primary supplier for Asian and African markets, shows that butane prices surged by 25.8 percent in August, climbing from Ksh64,620.50 to Ksh81,326 per tonne. Over the same period, propane prices jumped 23.2 percent, rising from Ksh51,929.50 to Ksh63,973.50 per tonne.

![The 30,000-metric-tonne Taifa Gas terminal at Dongo Kundu Special Economic Zone, July 19, 2026](https://i.ibb.co/cKhTBGwS/1a539dba5608.webp)

The cost escalations are directly tied to intensifying US-Iran tensions, which have compromised safety along critical maritime channels in the Strait of Hormuz. This instability has rippled to Saudi Arabia's Yanbu port on the Red Sea, which typically serves as an alternative export channel when the strait is blocked.

The shipping bottleneck has severely choked Yanbu’s export capacity. The port’s LPG shipments fell from 302,600 tonnes in June to 240,300 tonnes in July, before collapsing to just 71,200 tonnes in August. Preliminary industry tracking projects that exports will drop further to around 51,700 tonnes for September, squeezing the supply of gas heading to East Africa.

In Kenyan retail outlets, consumers currently pay between Ksh1,100 and Ksh1,600 to refill a 6-kilogramme cylinder, while a 13-kilogramme cylinder costs between Ksh2,200 and Ksh3,500. Local distributors are expected to pass the higher landed costs directly to consumers beginning in October.

### The Storage Mismatch

![File photo of Gas Cylinders on display at an outlet in Nairobi City](https://i.ibb.co/wF0t6PR4/15c74d6a3c10.webp)

The global price shock arrives at an ironic moment for Kenya’s energy security. At the Dongo Kundu Special Economic Zone in Mombasa, Tanzanian firm Taifa Gas is in the final stages of constructing a Ksh16 billion (€107.5 million) LPG import and storage terminal. 

Designed specifically to shield the local market from volatile international supply disruptions, the facility is set to become East Africa’s largest gas terminal. It features 12 spherical pressurized tanks capable of holding 30,000 tonnes of gas, with a planned expansion footprint of up to 45,000 tonnes across a 30-acre site.

The facility is currently undergoing hydrostatic testing—a critical phase of safety and structural pressure checks before gas can be pumped into the system. While the testing represents the final hurdle before commissioning, the terminal is not yet operational to absorb or buffer the pricing shock hitting the market next month.

Without functioning bulk storage of this scale, Kenya has lacked the strategic reserves needed to negotiate long-term price stability or hold cheaper inventory during periods of global market calm. Consequently, local retail prices remain bound to the immediate weekly fluctuations of Middle East supply terminals.

Once the hydrostatic testing is complete and the Dongo Kundu facility is fully commissioned, the market will watch whether its 30,000-tonne capacity can successfully decouple local retail prices from shipping disruptions in the Red Sea. For now, Kenyan consumers will have to navigate the October price spikes with their pockets.
