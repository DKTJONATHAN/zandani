The Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA) has ordered transporters, importers, and clearing agents to procure their own electronic cargo seals from 15 private vendors, ending years of state-issued tracking hardware that frequently paralyzed clearance lines at the Port of Mombasa.

**What we know:**
* **Deadline set:** All KRA-owned tracking seals will be decommissioned and phased out entirely by October 26, 2026.
* **Scope:** The shift to the multi-vendor model applies to both containerized dry freight (e-seals) and wet cargo (e-fuel tracking).
* **Vendor licensing:** KRA has authorized 15 private technology firms to supply and service the tracking devices directly through commercial contracts with logistics operators.
* **Driver of change:** Chronic equipment shortages at Times Tower previously stranded thousands of trucks at border posts and coastal exit gates, running up millions in demurrage costs.

Under the framework issued on Friday, September 11, the taxman is formally pivoting to what it terms a “Multi-Vendor, User-Owned Seals model” under the Regional Electronic Cargo Tracking System (RECTS). The move unloads the capital-intensive burden of purchasing, maintaining, and replacing malfunctioning seals from the state balance sheet directly onto cargo owners and hauliers.

According to KRA, the change targets both containerized units traversing the Northern Corridor and transit petroleum tankers bound for regional markets.

“To enhance service delivery under the RECTS and in response to evolving business dynamics and increasing demands, KRA has transitioned to a Multi-Vendor, User-Owned Seals model for both dry cargo (e-seals) and wet cargo (e-fuel) electronic seals,” the authority announced in a public notice to industry players.

Cargo handlers, clearing and forwarding agents, and bonded warehouse operators will now have to negotiate individual commercial rates with the 15 licensed suppliers. After the October 26 cutoff, customs stations will strictly turn away any transit or bonded consignment lacking compliance with the private-vendor infrastructure.

For years, the logistics fraternity has decried the tax collector’s inability to maintain an adequate inventory of operational e-seals. Transporters routinely sat idle along the Mombasa-Nairobi highway and the Malaba border point waiting for scarce seals to be recycled from offloaded containers, racking up catastrophic holding fees while regional competitors marketed alternative routes.

By devolving seal procurement to the open market, KRA neatly solves an operational embarrassment that has degraded corridor efficiency. Yet by turning compliance into a recurring private cost, the taxman has once again tackled state inefficiency by shifting the financial overhead straight onto the private sector.
