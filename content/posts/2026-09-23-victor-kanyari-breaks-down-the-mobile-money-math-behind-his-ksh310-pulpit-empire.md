Long before digital mobile-money transactions became a staple of modern Kenyan commerce, a young drop-out running a borrowed hall in Kawangware was busy testing pricing tiers over radio broadcasts. 

Prophet Victor Kanyari’s route to running the Salvation Healing Ministry was built on ledger-style trial and error. Speaking on Radio Maisha, Kanyari pulled back the curtain on the financial mechanics that preceded his national notoriety, laying out how a lack of formal schooling and early hardship in Nairobi guided him straight to the pulpit.

His entry into the ministry was entirely practical. Moving from Loitokitok to Nairobi alongside his mother, Prophetess Lucy Nduta, Kanyari left school in Form Three after running into behavioral hurdles. Assisting his mother as she preached on the streets, he observed firsthand that public donations of food and cash offered a viable exit route from persistent poverty.

“For me, I can’t say was a calling, I just saw, loved it and that’s how I became a preacher,” he recounted during the interview.

Armed with a hall offered by a local landlord in Kawangware, Kanyari began preaching independently. But establishing a steady revenue stream required market testing. Early attempts to raise funds through radio broadcasts met resistance. Initial requests for listeners to send KSh1,000 yielded poor results. Subsequent drops to KSh700 and KSh500 similarly failed to move audiences.

It was only when the financial threshold was dropped to KSh310 that transaction volumes shifted dramatically. Kanyari noted that the lower denomination struck an unexpected chord, triggering a heavy flow of mobile-money transactions across multiple phones that he claimed peaked at over KSh1 million daily during high-traffic periods.

That digital infrastructure later collided with investigative scrutiny. In 2014, Kanyari was thrust into the national spotlight following a KTN *Jicho Pevu* expose titled *Makri ya Injili* (“Gospel Scam”), which featured allegations of staged miracles and the use of potassium permanganate to turn water red during demonstrations.

Despite the intense public backlash and calls for arrest led by figures like former journalist Mohammed Ali, the ministry weathered the storm. Kanyari noted that law enforcement agencies required formal statements from aggrieved congregants before pressing charges, and with no complainants stepping forward to record official statements at police stations, prosecutors did not advance the matter.

Today, the founder maintains that his current services operate on compassion without standard collections, leaving behind the radio-broadcast tiers that originally built his digital-era congregation.
