Former Deputy President Rigathi Gachagua stepped before a crowd of Kenyans in Dallas, Texas, with an old-school political riddle and a brand-conscious pledge: someone among the opposition’s presidential hopefuls is going to throw a tantrum and storm off, but "Riggy G" will definitely not be that guy.

Speaking to the diaspora audience, the Democracy for the Citizens Party (DCP) leader leaned heavily into political theater, forecasting that the delicate unity pact being assembled against President William Ruto will inevitably fracture the minute a single joint flagbearer is unveiled for 2027.

Rather than naming names, Gachagua decided to deliver his warning as an unverified blind item wrapped in historical nostalgia, directly invoking the bitter fallout of the 2002 election cycle.

“Just the way William Ruto’s people are talking, it is the same way Daniel arap Moi’s supporters talked in the 2002 elections,” Gachagua told the Dallas gathering. 

He then walked the room back twenty-four years to the famous opposition assembly that rearranged the country's political chessboard, reminding the audience of the high-stakes friction that erupted when Raila Odinga endorsed Mwai Kibaki.

“At Uhuru Park, there was a meeting and it was declared ‘Kibaki Tosha’,” Gachagua recalled. “There was one opposition presidential candidate and Simeon Nyachae walked away. Nyachae went alone.”

That historical precedent, Gachagua argued, is about to repeat itself on modern timelines. While stopping short of giving away the identity of the prospective modern-day Nyachae, he claimed the signs of an impending departure are already visible to anyone paying attention to backroom positioning.

“I want to prophesy today, not because I am a prophet but based on experience, foresight and understanding Kenyan politics, history will repeat itself,” he told the room. “One presidential candidate will be announced, one person will go away. That one person will go away on his own.”

Yet having stirred up the prospect of an inevitable walkout, Gachagua pivoted swiftly to his own political standing, switching into the third-person pop-culture moniker that has defined his public rebrand.

Though he has already declared his intention to run for State House in 2027, the DCP boss took pains to distance himself from any prospective drama, assuring the room that his personal ambitions will not blow up the larger alliance.

“Riggy G will be part of the winning team,” he stated. “This is because Riggy G will not be that fellow who will walk away; he will stick with the arrangement that has been agreed upon by the people of Kenya.”

The declaration served a double purpose: pitching his own discipline to diaspora audiences while quietly tossing down a gauntlet to other opposition principals eyeing the same presidential ticket.

Gachagua has previously pressed coalition leaders to stop flirting with private exit strategies and publicly commit to backing whoever secures the ticket. In Dallas, he drew a firm line between his own open promise and the shifting postures of his coalition peers.

“I may not talk for other people because people may let you down,” Gachagua said. “I want to speak for myself and say that if it is not me, I will support whoever will be picked as the opposition flagbearer unconditionally.”

To sweeten the loyalty pledge, Gachagua dangled an ambitious electoral forecast before the room, claiming that whichever candidate eventually emerges from the joint consensus will pull in 75 percent of the national vote—a soaring estimate that serves more as morale-boosting rhetoric than grounded electoral math.

For now, the Dallas room was left to chew on the riddle of who Gachagua believes is already packing their bags. With multiple heavyweight contenders circling the same joint ticket, his refusal to identify the impending defector guarantees that every joint appearance, missed handshake, and side consultation back home will be scrutinized for who fits the 2002 silhouette.
