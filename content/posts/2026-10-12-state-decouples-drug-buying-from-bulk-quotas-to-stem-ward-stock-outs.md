Central health authorities are moving to dismantle speculative medicine ordering in public health facilities, instructing county and referral hospitals to tie every replenishment request strictly to verifiable patient consumption data.

The directive, issued during a five-day consultative assembly in Naivasha running from October 11 to 15, marks an operational shift in how the State measures supply chain performance. Rather than gauging success by the monetary value or bulk volume of consignments moved out of central warehouses, the Kenya Medical Supplies Authority (KEMSA) and the Ministry of Health are insisting that availability at the patient’s bedside become the primary benchmark.

Under the new guidelines presented to county health directors and hospital pharmacists, facilities will no longer be permitted to submit estimated bulk orders that fail to account for shelf turnover, prevailing inventory, and verified disease burdens.

### Moving Beyond Warehouse Quotas

Addressing health administrators, KEMSA Chief Executive Officer Dr Waqo Ejersa outlined the systemic breakdown caused by dislocated planning: facilities frequently run completely out of essential frontline treatments while simultaneously holding overstocked batches of other supplies until they pass their shelf life.

![kemsa-ceo.jpg](https://i.ibb.co/svbrX9rp/738c1c01e476.webp)

"Our performance must ultimately be reflected in the ability of health facilities to deliver services reliably and in the ability of patients to access the products they need when they need them," Ejersa said. 

He warned that poor facility forecasting directly drives both avoidable stock shortages and the destruction of public funds through expired commodities. To correct the mismatch, the authority wants hospitals to systematically record patient footfall against exact dispensing registers before generating requisitions.

Medical Services Principal Secretary Dr Ouma Oluga reinforced the position, pointing out that national procurement drives achieve little if local hospital administrators order blind.

"Procuring medicines alone does not guarantee their availability in hospitals," Oluga said, urging administrators to ground requisition cycles in real-time facility demand rather than bureaucratic precedent.

### Empowering Facility Pharmacists

![PS Oluga](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_inner/public/images/media/ps-ouma-oluga.jpg.webp?itok=ahLLFCYM)

Central to the reform is a mandate to strip non-clinical administrators of unilateral procurement discretion. Ejersa instructed hospital boards and county health executives to formally integrate facility pharmacists into resource allocation, demand forecasting, and inventory audits.

Historically, institutional friction between administrative procurement desks and hospital pharmacists has resulted in mismatch orders—consignments delivered without matching patient demographics or seasonal disease spikes, forcing clinical staff to send patients to private commercial chemists.

Oluga tasked pharmacists directly with arbitrating what products enter hospital formularies, setting procurement ceilings, and monitoring distribution chains from store arrival down to clinical administration.

### The Data Challenge Ahead

The Naivasha conference brings together national referral executives, county health leadership, Ministry of Health representatives, and institutional pharmacists to agree on uniform reporting standards before the sessions conclude on October 15.

The durability of the policy shift will hinge on whether devolved hospital networks possess the digital infrastructure and administrative willingness to maintain transparent, daily stock-level reporting—a baseline requirement if KEMSA is to replace ad-hoc emergency deliveries with predictable, consumption-based replenishment.
