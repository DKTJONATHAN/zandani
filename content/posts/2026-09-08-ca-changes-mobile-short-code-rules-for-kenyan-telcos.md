The Communications Authority of Kenya issued a fresh regulatory directive in Nairobi on Tuesday, September 8, 2026, at 9:00 AM EAT. The regulator changed how telecommunications short codes are allocated across the country. Mobile numbers for special services will now depend on the service offered rather than the mobile network operator hosting them.

What we know:
* The Communications Authority of Kenya published the new Telecommunications Numbering, Naming, Addressing and Identification framework on Tuesday, September 8, 2026.
* New short code allocations will group similar services under unified number blocks regardless of whether a subscriber uses Safaricom, Airtel, or Telkom Kenya.
* Existing emergency lines like 112, 999, and child protection lines like 116 will remain unchanged under the official notice.
* The new directive applies strictly to fresh applications for short codes filed after September 8, 2026.

Director General offices at the Communications Authority headquarters along Waiyaki Way confirmed that the enhanced framework aims to treat national numbering resources as a scarce public asset. The regulator noted in its public notice that short codes were previously tied directly to specific network operators, which created fragmented number blocks across different telecommunications infrastructure.

According to the official public notice released by the regulator, allocation will now strictly follow service categories rather than operator systems. "Allocation of telecommunications short codes will be services-based as opposed to the previous framework in which they were telecommunications operator-specific," the notice stated.

Short codes function as abbreviated phone digits used for customer care, mobile transactions, promotional alerts, and public services. Previously, a business operating in Nairobi County had to spend thousands of KSh acquiring separate short codes for Safaricom, Airtel, and Telkom Kenya just to reach every subscriber across the country.

Regulators clarified that companies holding active short codes assigned before Tuesday will keep their current digits. New enterprise applicants setting up offices from Westlands Exit to Upper Hill will be the first group subject to the service-based numbering plan.

### What it costs you

If you are stuck in traffic near the Roysambu drift on Thika Road, typing a USSD code to pay for household power or check a bank balance usually feels seamless. Behind that three-digit text lies a quiet turf war. Network operators previously held lock-key power over short codes. They forced small businesses to pay high setup fees across multiple networks just to reach every customer in Kiambu or Nairobi.

This decision strips that leverage away from big telecom desks in Westlands. Service-based numbering means a delivery startup or local SACCO gets one standard code that works everywhere without playing gatekeeper games. Existing codes are grand-fathered in, so the giant operators keep their easy revenue from old legacy lines. The small player gets a cleaner entrance. It reduces friction for new financial products hitting your phone screen. But until the regulator enforces cross-network pricing limits on these codes, the actual tariff cost per SMS will still bite the consumer sending that text message from a bus stop in Kasarani.
