A forged secondary school certificate presented nearly fifteen years ago ultimately unlocked close to five million shillings in public emoluments before anti-corruption investigators unraveled the paper trail.

At the Milimani Anti-Corruption Court on Thursday, October 22, 2026, Magistrate Isabellah Barasa delivered a judgment that penalized former County Assembly of Kiambu human resource officer Margaret Mbugua after she entered a plea agreement with the Director of Public Prosecutions.

![Undated image of the Milimani Law Courts](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_style/public/images/media/undated-image-of-the-milimani-la-courts-1.jpg.webp?itok=oFrRbMPB)

Investigators established that Mbugua used a falsified Kenya Certificate of Secondary Education (KCSE) document—purportedly issued from Gachoire Secondary School in 1996—to secure a principal human resource officer position. Court records show the document was originally presented on December 16, 2011, at St Paul's University in Kiambu County.

Between November 2020 and August 2025, that recruitment paper allowed her to draw Ksh4,921,953.80 in public salary from the county assembly.

Under the terms of the conviction, Mbugua pleaded guilty to three counts encompassing fraudulent acquisition of public property, uttering a false document, and presenting a forged certificate. The separate charge of forgery was excluded from her guilty plea under the agreement.

![An image of a court gravel](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_inner/public/images/media/court-gravel.jpg.webp?itok=-X9kbCRe)

The magistrate fined her Ksh20,000 on each of the three counts, totaling Ksh60,000, with a default sentence of six months' imprisonment for each count. Beyond the criminal fines, the court ordered her to pay an additional Ksh1 million in restitution to the government as compensation for fraudulently acquiring public property.

The outcome underscores persistent vulnerabilities in institutional vetting processes across devolved units, where academic credentials can bypass internal scrutiny and sustain long-term employment before forensic audits catch up. The Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission welcomed the ruling as a deterrent against individuals utilizing fraudulent papers to siphon public funds.

The case unfolds as state agencies ramp up forensic scrutiny of recruitment records and payroll systems nationwide. Recent audits across ministries, departments, and state corporations have flagged billions in irregular expenditures, keeping the spotlight firmly on personnel management and credential verification across the public service.
