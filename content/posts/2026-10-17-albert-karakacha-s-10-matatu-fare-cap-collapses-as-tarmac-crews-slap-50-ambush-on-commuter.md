When Matatu Owners Association chairman Albert Karakacha stepped forward to sanction a 10 percent fare adjustment, he framed it as a calculated, reasonable defense of private enterprise. Fuel prices had jumped, and operators, he argued, needed to protect their margins. 

Within hours, the crews behind the steering wheels on Nairobi’s streets shredded his formula entirely.

Instead of the controlled ten-shilling bump Karakacha envisioned on standard city stages, commuters boarding Friday morning vehicles walked into an unvetted ambush. Across multiple key arteries, conductors began demanding up to 50 percent more, transforming the association’s boardroom decree into an unruly street-level shakedown that left Karakacha’s authority looking distinctly optional.

### The Math on the Stage

Under the association's 10 percent advisory, a regular Sh100 fare to the central business district was supposed to settle at Sh110. But at pick-up points across town, crews flatly demanded Sh150—an instant Sh50 surcharge per journey.

On the Westlands route via Parklands, vehicles that previously charged Sh50 pushed their prices up to Sh70, locking in a 40 percent jump before morning peak traffic had even cleared. 

For regular commuters, the variance between Karakacha’s press statement and the conductor’s cash pouch was immediate and painful. Passenger Lynnet Anyango voiced the sheer shock shared by dozens stranded on the pavement: “I don’t believe I have been charged Sh150 to town, I feel so bad.”

For an everyday worker completing two trips daily, the unilateral Sh50 jump adds Sh100 to each day’s commute. Across an average 22-day working month, that leak totals Sh2,200—money wrenched directly away from grocery budgets, rent obligations, and school upkeep in an economy already pinched to the bone.

### Open Mutiny at Khoja

At downtown terminals such as Khoja bus station, passenger frustration boiled over into direct verbal confrontations with conductors over who authorized the steep margins.

“Why is it that whenever the prices drop you don’t reduce the fare, but whenever it shoots you hike very fast?” commuter Patrick Kimutai challenged crews loading vehicles at the terminus.

The blunt logic of the price hike also drew scrutiny from passengers doing the math on the spot. Resident Moses Muthama pointed out the flaw in using pump adjustments to justify compounding passenger fares.

“Is it that every passenger is paying more than the 10 percent rise, or it's one passenger paying?” Muthama asked. “Coz the fuel increase is per liter, not per person.”

Karakacha had originally insisted that the industry had consulted before landing on the single-digit benchmark. “Today we woke up, and we found that the fuel has gone up, and as the association, we’ve consulted widely, and we’ve told our members to increase by 10% because we are businessmen and we need to make a profit,” Karakacha stated when green-lighting the review.

Yet the refusal of individual savings and credit cooperative societies (Saccos) to honor that 10 percent ceiling has left city commuters questioning whether the association possesses any operational enforcement power once a conductor pulls the sliding door shut.

### The EPRA Trigger

The chaotic fare escalation follows the contentious petroleum price review released by the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA), led by Director General Eng. Edward Mwirigi Kinyua, which took effect on October 15 and runs through November 14, 2026.

That pump adjustment had already generated fierce pushback before matatu crews ever revised their stage boards. On October 15, the Motorists Association of Kenya (MAK) publicly condemned EPRA’s pricing schedule, highlighting unexplained disparities between international landing costs and what motorists are being forced to pay at retail forecourts.

According to MAK’s analysis of the regulatory data, landed costs climbed by 11.81 percent for super petrol, 11.32 percent for diesel, and 9.41 percent for kerosene. Yet pump prices moved on an entirely different trajectory: petrol rose by 6.04 percent, diesel by 4.54 percent, and kerosene—which recorded the lowest increase in landed cost—spiked by a massive 14.89 percent at the pump.

While lobby groups and regulators trade barbs over refinery invoices and landed shipping manifests through mid-November, Albert Karakacha faces an entirely different credibility test: proving whether his directives can actually control the rogue fares charged on Nairobi’s tarmac.
