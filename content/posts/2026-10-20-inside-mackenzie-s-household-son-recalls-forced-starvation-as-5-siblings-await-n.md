The doctrine that claimed the lives of 190 children inside the Shakahola forest was not merely preached to distant congregants—it was systematically tested inside the household of Paul Mackenzie himself.

Hours after the Mombasa High Court convicted the controversial preacher and 28 associates of 191 counts of murder on Monday, October 19, Mackenzie’s 24-year-old son, Dan Mackenzie, offered a rare window into the internal mechanics of a family built under religious coercion. While his father sits in custody awaiting sentencing, Dan described growing up under the same severe deprivation that defined the forest commune, followed by a total rupture that pushed him out of school and into the informal transport economy of the Coast.

### The Household Regimen

The court’s judgment, handed down by Justice Diana Kavedza-Mochache, legally established that Mackenzie’s movement deliberately starved 190 children and one adult to death. For Dan, that lethal framework was an inescapable childhood reality rather than an abstract doctrine.

![Paul Mackenzie (right) at the Mombasa Law Courts on October 19, 2026, and his son Dan Mackenzie (left).](https://i.ibb.co/67bb5qCv/f0fba26e84df.webp)

At 12 years of age, he was subjected to a continuous seven-day dry fast. Resisting the stringent edicts of the church invited direct physical enforcement; during one episode, church followers stripped him naked and beat him for nearly three hours to compel him to attend a service. While Dan noted his father did not personally administer the physical blows during that assault, the violence functioned as institutional discipline to crush resistance among the preacher's own kin.

The domestic friction deepened as Dan grew older. When he continued to reject the movement's radical trajectory, Mackenzie cut ties completely, informing his son that he was on his own. The fallout forced Dan out of formal education in Class Seven, driving him onto the streets of Malindi to make a living as a motorbike taxi operator.

### Accusations of Betrayal

The separation persisted through his father's arrest, the excavation of mass graves in Kilifi County, and the protracted trial in Mombasa. Following Monday's verdict, a brief exchange between the two highlighted the unbridgeable gulf remaining between the preacher and his eldest son.

According to Dan, Mackenzie confronted him directly, accusing him of betrayal. Dan maintained that he had nothing to answer for, stating he views himself first and foremost as a victim who survived the very system that claimed scores of other young lives.

"Justice has been served for the victims and their families," Dan said outside the courtroom, describing the sweeping guilty verdict as an overdue relief.

![Main accused, Paul MacKenzie (center)](https://i.ibb.co/sSGPnRj/18d4a1f7b0ae.webp)

### Five Children in State Care

While Dan managed to establish an independent livelihood on a motorcycle, the broader generational aftermath of the Good News International movement remains unresolved inside his own family. 

Five of Mackenzie's younger children—three girls and two boys—were extracted from the movement during security operations and currently reside in a designated rescue facility. The minors, shielded from public view, represent a complex rehabilitation challenge for social welfare authorities tasked with de-radicalising and integrating children raised at the centre of an apocalyptic sect.

Dan expressed cautious hope that his young siblings will eventually secure the mental and physical space required to reconstruct their lives outside institutional walls.

For Mackenzie and the 28 co-convicts, legal proceedings now shift toward formal penalties. Justice Kavedza-Mochache scheduled the sentencing hearing for November 6, 2026, where the High Court will determine the custodial terms for the single largest mass murder conviction in Kenya's modern judicial history.
