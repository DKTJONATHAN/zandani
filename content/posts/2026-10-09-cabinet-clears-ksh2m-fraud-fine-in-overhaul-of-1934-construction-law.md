According to an executive dispatch released on Friday, October 9, the Cabinet has approved the Architectural and Quantity Surveying Practitioners Bill, 2026, clearing a sweeping statutory overhaul aimed at shielding property developers and urban tenants from unregistered practitioners.

The proposed legislation hands regulatory authorities substantial punitive power, replacing a legal framework that has governed Kenya’s building sector since 1934. Under the terms cleared by the Cabinet, individuals who secure professional registration or credentials through forged documentation or deceit face a fine of up to Ksh2 million, a prison term of up to two years, or both.

“To strengthen building safety, Cabinet cleared the Architectural and Quantity Surveying Practitioners Bill, 2026, proposing tougher action against unqualified practitioners and penalties of up to Ksh2 million or two years in prison for false registration or licensing,” the Cabinet stated.

### Dual-Tier Penalties and Expanding Oversight

![President William Ruto chairing a cabinet meeting on Friday, October 9, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/NnTHxrqm/e8df5a7aa684.webp)

Parliamentary tracking records for the draft law outline a clear distinction between deceptive entry into the profession and active unauthorized trade. While securing fraudulent paperwork carries the Ksh2 million sanction, individuals caught practising without a valid annual licence face a separate fine of up to Ksh3 million.

The bill also broadens the net of statutory compliance beyond traditional architectural and quantity surveying practices. If enacted, mandatory annual practising licences will extend to landscape architects, interior designers, and construction project managers, bringing previously fragmented disciplines under continuous statutory scrutiny.

The proposed penalties mark an abrupt break from the existing 1934 statute, which caps penalties for the unlawful use of protected professional titles at just Ksh5,000. Lawmakers had previously debated raising impostor fines to Ksh1 million in February 2023, but the latest executive draft doubles that threshold while establishing the steeper Ksh3 million sanction for operating outside the annual licensing register.

### The Shadow of Credential Misuse

The executive push arrives amid mounting evidence that structural failures across Kenya's urban hubs are tied not only to rogue contractors, but also to compromised professional oversight.

Investigative findings into the April 2025 partial collapse of an 11-storey structure in Mombasa revealed that licensed architects and engineers had permitted their professional credentials to be misused on-site. In that incident, investigators cited design discrepancies, insufficient geotechnical soil investigations, and absent professional supervision as direct contributors to the structural failure.

![A photo collage of President William Ruto and an architectural design, October 9, 2026.](https://i.ibb.co/nq81JBcf/ec3ccc4d7c59.webp)

Similar regulatory evasion proved fatal in Nairobi on January 2, 2026, when Manzil Towers collapsed in the South C neighbourhood, killing two people. Post-disaster assessments by the National Construction Authority confirmed that while the project's formally approved blueprints authorized a 12-storey development, the structure had been pushed to 14 storeys before giving way.

### Audit Reality Facing Parliament

The legislative timeline now shifts to the National Assembly, where the bill faces debate against a backdrop of deep structural distress in the capital's housing stock.

Data released on January 9, 2026, by the Institution of Engineers of Kenya, citing inspection audits from the National Building Inspectorate across roughly 15,000 structures in Nairobi, showed that only 15 per cent met safety thresholds. Approximately 8 per cent were rated in fair condition, while the remaining bulk of the audited properties failed safety benchmarks, according to former IEK president Shammah Kiteme.

With the executive branch formally endorsing the bill, lawmakers will determine whether escalating financial liability and mandatory licensing registries can curb the practice of rubber-stamping non-compliant high-rises before more compromised sites break ground.
