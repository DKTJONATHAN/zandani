Edwin Sifuna still holds onto two small slips of printed paper from October 2025: boarding passes for Kenya Airways flights 202 and 203. 

For the Nairobi Senator, who also doubles as the Orange Democratic Movement’s secretary general, those physical ticket stubs are not just travel memorabilia. They are the tangible markers of an unscripted, 24-hour scramble across the Indian Ocean to retrieve the body of former Prime Minister Raila Odinga following his death in Mumbai at the age of 80.

Reflecting publicly on Friday, October 16, 2026, exactly a year after the mission, Sifuna opened up about the behind-the-scenes chaos and the heavy personal toll that defined the immediate hours after the veteran opposition leader passed away.

### The Rush for KQ 202 and 203

When the news broke on the morning of October 15, 2025, that Raila had died while undergoing treatment in India, there was neither time for ceremonial planning nor room for conventional international clearance timelines. 

Sifuna revealed that he preserved the boarding passes from the paired Kenya Airways flights that shuttled him directly into Mumbai and back to Nairobi between October 15 and 16, 2025.

“I kept my passes from KQ flights 202 and 203 to and fro Mumbai on 15/16 October 2025,” Sifuna said, recounting how the sudden logistical reality of coordinating the repatriation took over the moment the news landed.

For the vocal senator, navigating that departure lounge was an exercise in shock management. Looking back, he admitted that the sudden emotional collision of the day left him unable to measure which part of the ordeal took a greater toll: processing the loss itself or walking onto the outbound aircraft to collect his party leader's remains.

“I couldn’t tell you what the toughest part of the day was, receiving the news in the morning or taking that flight to Mumbai,” he confessed.

### Bending the Rules of International Travel

While public eyes were fixed on the political magnitude of Raila’s death, Sifuna’s retrospective brought attention to the intense, quiet diplomatic maneuvering that occurred behind closed doors to get Kenyan officials into India and out again within hours.

Arranging immediate international travel under such emergency conditions required circumventing rigid clearance procedures, visa processing protocols, and aviation clearances that typically take days to authorize. 

Sifuna singled out the Indian High Commission in Nairobi and the central government in New Delhi, acknowledging that the trip would have stalled on the tarmac without extraordinary institutional waivers.

“I want to say ‘Ahsante sana’ to some key people who made everything possible in very difficult circumstances,” Sifuna noted, pointing out how both diplomatic bodies moved mountains to fast-track documentation.

“The Indian High Commission in Nairobi and the Indian Government. I don’t know how many rules of international travel were bent to make that flight happen. Ahsante sana,” he said.

### A Paper Trail of an Unprecedented Day

Preserving boarding passes is often an instinct reserved for milestone vacations or historic personal journeys. In Sifuna’s case, retaining the documents from KQ 202 and 203 serves as a quiet record of one of the most high-stakes operations of his public career.

The senator’s disclosure provides a rare, unvarnished look at the immediate institutional panic and frantic logistical problem-solving that transpires away from television cameras when a towering national figure dies abroad.

One year later, as the country marks the milestone of Raila's passing, those two boarding passes remain Sifuna's personal proof of the day normal diplomatic bureaucracy gave way to an urgent race against the clock.
