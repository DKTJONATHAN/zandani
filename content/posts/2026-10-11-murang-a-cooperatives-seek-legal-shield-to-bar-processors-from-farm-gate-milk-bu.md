Dairy cooperative managers in Murang’a are pushing Parliament to rewrite cooperative regulations and outlaw commercial milk processors from buying supplies directly from individual smallholders.

The push, mounted by societies in Gatanga during the commissioning of newly restored state-funded milk coolers, seeks to erect a legal firewall around cooperative aggregation hubs. Society leaders argue that uninhibited farm-gate off-taking by industrial processors systematically fragments local collection pools, weakening the collective bargaining power required to shield farmers from soaring operational overheads.

Cooperative heads maintain that restored cold-chain infrastructure will sit underutilised if commercial buyers are legally permitted to cherry-pick individual producers at the farm gate, bypassing cooperative pooling mechanisms entirely.

### The Battle for Off-Take Controls

Under current open-market arrangements, milk processors frequently bypass cooperative societies to establish direct contracts with individual farmers, securing volume without adhering to collective society price baselines. 

![A collage of Milk Cooling machines.](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_inner/public/images/media/milk-cooler.jpg.webp?itok=zo60hjDf)

John Waweru, head of Coopertains Dairy Cooperatives, argued that closing this procurement route through statutory enforcement remains the only viable mechanism to keep grassroots societies financially solvent.

“The challenge we have as farmers is that the cost of animal feed is very high; the business has not generated much profit,” Waweru said during the Murang’a commissioning. “We want a law that says people should buy milk in cooperatives if they are from processors, and the cooperatives should deal with the farmers.”

For cooperative managers, an enforced statutory monopoly on primary aggregation would guarantee consistent volumes through local chilling plants and restore their leverage when negotiating per-litre purchase rates with industrial dairies. Without that legislative mandate, societies argue that independent sourcing continues to hollow out member loyalty, leaving cooperatives burdened with the fixed costs of chilling plants while processors harvest the peak milk supply.

### Production Costs Squeeze Farm Margins

The cooperative push comes as smallholders face an acute cash squeeze driven by the taxation structure on livestock nutrition. Dairy farmers in Gatanga noted that elevated duties and levies across processed feeds and raw protein supplements have erased producer margins, turning milk production into a break-even struggle despite persistent retail shortages across the country.

Farmers argue that while milk cooling infrastructure addresses post-harvest preservation, it does not alter the underlying arithmetic of commercial dairy rations. Without targeted fiscal interventions to lower feed input costs, herd nutrition declines, directly suppressing yields per cow and limiting the volume passing through the chillers.

![cows-buffaloes.jpg](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_style/public/images/media/cows-buffaloes.jpg.webp?itok=eLIaUq60)

Local producers are pressing the National Treasury and the Ministry of Agriculture to introduce immediate tax remissions on manufactured dairy meal and essential raw feed ingredients to restore farm profitability.

### Divergent Fixes for National Deficits

The demands from Gatanga highlight a widening gap between grassroots farm-level priorities and executive policy interventions aimed at solving Kenya's milk deficit.

While Murang’a producers are seeking immediate domestic margin relief through feed tax waivers and market ring-fencing, the national executive has turned toward biological diversification. Speaking on Friday, October 9, during the Agriculture and Food Security Summit at Jamhuri Park, President William Ruto directed Agriculture Principal Secretary Jonathan Mueke to begin formal arrangements for importing improved camel breeds and dairy water buffaloes.

Ruto cited commercial water buffalo milking models in India as a template for Kenya to expand its aggregate dairy output and insulate national supplies from climate volatility.

Yet for Murang'a smallholders operating existing dairy herds, the introduction of non-traditional dairy livestock offers no near-term remedy for daily feed receipts. Whether lawmakers take up the proposal to legally compel processors to buy exclusively through cooperative societies remains the central test for dairy leadership seeking to preserve organized pooling in Central Kenya.
