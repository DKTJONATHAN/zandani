President William Ruto did not arrive in Kitale simply to read out another dry administrative schedule. Standing before farmers on Saturday, October 10, 2026, the Head of State bypassed the usual bureaucratic language of agrarian reform to paint a very specific, consumer-facing picture: Kenyan-grown chocolate bars, breakfast beverage mixes, and processed confectionery rolling off domestic production lines.

Using the Trans Nzoia staging ground to announce that cocoa is now officially classified as a scheduled cash crop, Ruto urged growers in mapped ecological zones to look past raw bean exports and reposition themselves for an industrial processing boom.

### The Chocolate Pitch in Kitale

Taking the microphone during the rollout ceremony, Ruto made a direct, practical appeal to farmers, asking them to ready their parcels for a product whose commercial returns stretch far beyond the farm gate into supermarket shelves.

“Start preparing yourselves. From cocoa, we can produce beverage mixes, food items, chocolate, and many other products,” President Ruto said. “For that reason, I urge Kenyans in areas mapped out for cocoa cultivation to take up farming it, because it is also a tree. As we reforest our country, we can simultaneously boost our incomes.”

By explicitly anchoring the crop to the national tree-growing agenda, Ruto offered a dual incentive. Cocoa trees, in the administration's blueprint, are intended to function as both an environmental canopy for climate resilience and an aggressive income driver for rural households looking for alternatives to stagnant earnings.

### Immediate Seedling Deployment

Rather than framing the declaration as a distant legislative promise, Ruto insisted that the groundwork had already been laid in research nurseries. The government moved immediately to distribute seedlings to growers in attendance, tasking state research bodies with scaling up multiplication without delay.

Under the directive, the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organisation (KALRO) and the Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS) have stepped into an operational partnership. KALRO’s primary task is deploying certified, high-yielding planting varieties tailored for specific target belts, while KEPHIS oversees certification and phytosanitary quality control.

“I want us to collaborate in this way. Therefore, today here in Kitale, we are launching cocoa as a scheduled cash crop, and we are distributing seedlings immediately,” Ruto told the gathering. “Our teams at KALRO have conducted research and already have seedlings available, and we will now form a partnership between KEPHIS and KALRO to produce a sufficient supply of cocoa seedlings for everyone.”

### Moving Beyond Tea and Coffee Traditions

The formal elevation of cocoa to scheduled status places the crop within Kenya’s structured commercial framework. The designation allows the state to regulate supply chains, mobilize credit, and court private processing investments needed to build local value addition.

For decades, Kenya's agricultural export balance has relied almost exclusively on tea, coffee, and horticulture. By bringing cocoa into the legal fold and pitching it directly to Western Kenya farmers through promises of domestic manufacturing, Ruto is gambling that the country can nurture an entirely new commercial cash-crop culture from the ground up.
