Administrative friction between private oil marketing companies and state energy regulators delayed the absorption of Ksh10.3 billion in fuel stabilization funds, leaving the State Department for Petroleum struggling to execute its financial obligations during a cycle of heightened pump prices.

Disclosures submitted to Parliament reveal that the petroleum department achieved an 86 per cent absorption rate for its allocated subsidy budget, primarily driven by late claim submissions from oil marketers for the June 15 to July 14, 2026 pricing window. While the Ksh10.3 billion earmarked to buffer pump prices was eventually received in July 2026, processing lags choked institutional cash flow and stalled complementary petroleum projects.

Petroleum Principal Secretary Kello Harsama told lawmakers during an energy budget review in Naivasha that procurement bottlenecks had ripple effects across the sector, delaying key state-backed initiatives such as the Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG) distribution programme.

### EPRA Rebuffs External Pressure

![epra fuel petrol station](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_style/public/images/media/epra-fuel-2.jpg.webp?itok=MpwzHDum)

The financial disclosures emerged as the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA) came under intense questioning from the National Assembly Departmental Committee on Energy over transparency in calculating monthly fuel tariffs.

EPRA Director General Edward Kinyua defended the regulator’s pricing formula, telling MPs that the authority operates independently of commercial or political interests. The defense followed a steep price review mid-month that added Ksh12.93 per litre to Super Petrol, Ksh9.90 to diesel, and Ksh28.49 to kerosene, driven by supply chain volatility in the Middle East.

The increase pushed retail rates in Nairobi to Ksh226.96 per litre for petrol, Ksh227.76 for diesel, and Ksh219.87 for kerosene, triggering widespread public frustration.

### Communication Gap and Quality Oversight

Committee Chair David Gikaria questioned EPRA's public outreach strategy, arguing that reliance on traditional press releases leaves rural consumers uninformed about global crude dynamics and localized pricing structures.

![A photo impression of an oil refinery.](https://kenyans.co.ke/files/styles/article_inner/public/images/media/refinery.jpg.webp?itok=L1jshjUn)

In response, Kinyua outlined an ongoing campaign targeting regional television networks and vernacular radio stations to explain landed costs, taxes, and exchange rate variables to non-English speaking households. EPRA also committed to conducting structured public participation sessions prior to major regulatory shifts.

Beyond price transparency, lawmakers instructed the regulator to tighten border and depot controls to suppress petroleum adulteration. Gikaria warned that unverified blending of kerosene into diesel threatens engine infrastructure and shortchanges retail consumers.

### Private Capital Pivot for Oil Refining

The parliamentary session also confirmed a structural retreat in state infrastructure financing. Harsama informed the committee that the government has formally abandoned plans to independently fund a national oil refinery, opting instead to search for a private strategic developer.

Capital requirements for a modern refining plant exceed available public expenditure caps, Harsama noted, forcing the ministry to structure a public-private framework to handle crude processing. The ministry is now tasked with resolving the LPG supply chain delays while establishing terms of engagement for the private refinery partner.
