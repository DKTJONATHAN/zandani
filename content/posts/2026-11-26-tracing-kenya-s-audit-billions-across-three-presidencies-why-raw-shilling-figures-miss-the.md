Looking at public finance numbers without context is a fast route to political theatre. Every time the Auditor-General flags billions of shillings in government expenditure, the immediate public chorus demands to know who stole what and where the money vanished. But raw financial queries tell only half a story.

A side-by-side comparison of selected financial years across the Kibaki, Uhuru Kenyatta, and William Ruto administrations reveals a counterintuitive reality about how state spending and audit queries scale over time.

On sheer cash amounts alone, the trajectory looks like an escalating curve of concern. Selected financial years from the Kibaki era show queried amounts ranging between Sh303 billion and Sh390 billion. Under the Uhuru administration, those selected figures climbed higher, sitting between Sh450 billion and Sh515 billion. For the Ruto administration, the selected figures land between Sh380 billion and Sh460 billion.

At a glance, those figures suggest that financial controls have steadily deteriorated or that the financial hole has simply widened. However, that view ignores a fundamental reality: the national budget itself has ballooned significantly over the past two decades. Evaluating a Sh400 billion query in a smaller historical budget carries a vastly different weight than evaluating a similar figure against today's much larger government spending blueprint.

That distortion becomes apparent when shifting from absolute shillings to proportional shares of the national budget. 

In selected financial years from the Kibaki administration, queried expenditure hovered around 30 per cent of the national budget. Moving into the Uhuru era, that proportional footprint shifted downward, sliding from 20.6 per cent in the 2017/18 financial year to 14.5 per cent by 2021/22. In the selected figures for the Ruto administration, that downward trend continues, ranging between 10.6 per cent and 11 per cent.

While hundreds of billions of shillings remain under official scrutiny, the share of the national budget represented by those queries has contracted in more recent years. 

Yet these comparisons come with clear asterisks. The available figures draw from selected years rather than a continuous, unbroken annual series. The data also carries a notable gap for the 2015/16 financial year, while figures for 2024/25 remain labeled as preliminary. , financial years that overlap presidential transitions complicate any attempt at neat political attribution.

There is also a persistent misconception that needs untangling: a queried shilling is not automatically a stolen shilling. Auditor-General queries frequently stem from missing paperwork, unsupported transactions, weak internal financial controls, or technical questions over whether funds met their intended vote heads. Some findings expose deep irregularities requiring active criminal investigations, while others are resolved as soon as accounting officers supply the missing records.

The ultimate measure of public finance management is not simply the size of the initial audit query, but the follow-through. It depends entirely on whether accounting officers provide answers, whether flagged irregularities lead to recovery, and whether the same institutional weaknesses repeat in the next cycle of audits.
