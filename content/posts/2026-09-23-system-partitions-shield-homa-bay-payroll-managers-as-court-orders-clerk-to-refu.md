A Nairobi High Court order forcing a former Homa Bay County employee to repay Ksh708,664 in irregular double salaries has exposed the profound structural bottlenecks that prevent anti-corruption watchdogs from holding county payroll administrators legally responsible for systemic leaks.

The judgment, delivered on September 18, 2026, by Justice Benjamin Musyoki, laid bare a critical vulnerability in the devolved unit's financial controls. While the court held the individual recipient accountable for three years of illicit double payments, it simultaneously dismissed the Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission’s (EACC) case against two senior payroll officials accused of facilitating the leak. 

This legal divergence highlights a recurring administrative loophole in Kenya’s counties: the deep divide between automated systems and legacy manual processes.

![Entrance to Milimani Law Courts, Nairobi.](https://i.ibb.co/hxBCKWBs/74a5d6931842.webp)

According to court findings, the irregular disbursements began in February 2015 and persisted through May 2018. The EACC’s case against the beneficiary was solid, anchored by bank transaction logs and a critical self-incriminating email in which she had previously offered to settle the mounting debt in modest monthly installments of Ksh10,000. Justice Musyoki ruled that this email constituted a formal acknowledgment of debt, ordering her to refund the full Ksh708,664 plus interest at court rates, calculated from the date the recovery suit was filed.

However, the regulatory breakdown occurred at the administrative level. The EACC's attempt to pin accountability on the payroll managers collapsed under scrutiny. 

One of the accused officers was cleared after proving his duties were strictly confined to the Integrated Payroll and Personal Data (IPPD) system. Because he lacked access to the manual payroll system—where the double payments were actively processed—the court ruled he could not have authorized or negligently facilitated the irregular transactions. 

![A man counting several one thousand Kenyan shillings bills.](https://i.ibb.co/xSFB0nHq/84732b194ca3.webp)

Similarly, a second payroll manager escaped liability on a timing technicality. He assumed his administrative post in June 2015, several months after the double-pay scheme had already commenced in February. Without definitive proof of authorization or negligence, the court found the EACC’s evidence against the administrators insufficient.

The structural friction revealed in this case complicates the EACC’s broader campaign to secure county payrolls. The commission has been investigating a wider Ksh42 million loss within Homa Bay’s payroll network.

While clawing back funds from end-recipients offers a partial remedy, the systemic partitioning of administrative duties continues to shield high-level operators. By separating manual payroll duties from IPPD workflows and failing to establish clear, unbroken lines of digital custody, county governments effectively insulate administrative staff from liability when irregular payments occur.

The EACC has maintained that it is pursuing the prosecution of public officials who bear the "greatest responsibility" for the wider Ksh42 million payroll fraud. Yet, as the Homa Bay ruling demonstrates, until county administrations fully integrate legacy manual structures into a singular, auditable digital framework, proving administrative negligence in a court of law will remain an uphill battle.
