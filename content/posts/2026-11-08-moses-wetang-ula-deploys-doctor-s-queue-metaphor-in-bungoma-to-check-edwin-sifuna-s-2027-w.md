Moses Wetang’ula went back to the grassroots over the weekend with an unusual diagnosis for the restless political energy bubbling across Western Kenya: sit tight, do not jump the line, and wait for the doctor to call your number.

Speaking at a funeral service in Bungoma County on Saturday, the National Assembly Speaker abandoned formal legislative parlance for raw vernacular theater. Faced with the surging grassroots momentum of younger regional leaders, Wetang’ula cast himself as a patient already seated outside the consultation room door, urging local voters not to abandon his decades-long march toward State House in favor of fresher, louder arrivals.

“When you go to the doctor, he treats one patient then says, ‘Next’,” Wetang’ula told the crowd, deploying a clinical triage analogy to explain national succession. “I’m at the juncture where when they say ‘Next’, I will be in.”

### The Triage Argument

Wetang’ula’s vivid imagery was aimed directly at younger constituents whose loyalties have grown increasingly fluid. Rather than relying on technical arguments about coalition agreements, the veteran politician framed State House access as an unyielding queue where jumping ahead carries institutional penalties.

He warned residents against trading established political mileage for overnight excitement, arguing that walking out of line now would wipe out decades of regional positioning.

“And you are here saying you want to go at the back of the queue and start walking slowly,” Wetang’ula cautioned. “By the time they say ‘Next’, ten people will have gone before you. I can tell you, my people, for free, the easiest route to get to where we want power.”

His calculation is transparent: by endorsing President William Ruto through 2032, Wetang’ula insists the Luhya community holds an uncontested claim on the ruling alliance's eventual succession ticket. In his view, breaking ranks ahead of 2027 resets the clock to zero.

### Invoking Muliro and the Lineage Card

To cement his position at the front of that conceptual waiting room, the Speaker did not stop at modern medical metaphors. He invoked Western Kenya’s historical heavyweight pedigree, drawing a direct line from pioneering giants straight to himself.

“There was Masinde Muliro, Kijana Wamalwa, and I’m the one who’s there now,” Wetang’ula declared, claiming the mantle of undisputed regional dean.

He also leaned on the emotional weight of native idioms, turning to the phrase *shenyu ni shenyu*—roughly translating to *yours is yours*—to demand emotional loyalty from a constituency that watched him rise from Sirisia parliamentarian to Cabinet minister and now head of the legislative arm of government.

“I heard someone say *shenyu ni shenyu*, am I not yours?” he asked the mourners. “You are the people who birthed me and raised me… And I’m not alone in this, I’m with you. You are the reason I’m where I’m today, and I will get where I’m going because of you.”

### The Linda Mwananchi Pressure

The urgency in Wetang’ula’s weekend tone reflects a very real shift on the ground. For months, the Linda Mwananchi Movement has been pulling energetic crowds across the Western belt, powered by an assertive crop of younger politicians who reject the doctrine of waiting until 2032.

The brigade is steered by Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna, whose rapid elevation as a premier opposition voice has placed him firmly inside backroom deliberations regarding the 2027 presidential ticket. Alongside Sifuna, leaders including Trans Nzoia Governor George Natembeya, Saboti MP Caleb Namisi, Vihiga Senator Godfrey Osotsi, and Bumula MP Jack Wamboka have consistently challenged old-guard political deference.

Their rallies have targeted economic grievances and everyday citizen pain points, attracting young audiences who view 2032 as an unnecessarily distant timeline for regional self-determination.

Wetang’ula, however, insisted that national office requires navigating a long institutional apprenticeships rather than riding sudden waves of street fervor. Having established his perch in the National Assembly, the Speaker made it clear in Bungoma that he has no intention of vacating his seat outside the doctor's office—or yielding his spot to newcomers knocking on the door.
