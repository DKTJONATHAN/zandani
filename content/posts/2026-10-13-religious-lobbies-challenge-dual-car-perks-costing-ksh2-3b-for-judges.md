Kenyan taxpayers are simultaneously financing dedicated official chauffeur-driven state vehicles and private automobile purchases for superior court judges, an expenditure model that religious leaders and civil society coalitions are now challenging as fiscally indefensible.

In a joint petition statement released Tuesday, October 13, the civic and faith-based organisations calculated that the Ksh10 million vehicle grant issued to judicial officers consumes roughly Ksh2.3 billion before tax—or Ksh1.61 billion net—across a standard four-year disbursement cycle.

The lobby groups contend that while superior court judges already maintain full access to state-fuelled, driver-assigned institutional transport for official duties, the separate multi-million-shilling grant is allocated entirely for private vehicle ownership. 

"Our analysis indicates that judges have access to official chauffeur-driven transport, while the disputed grant is intended for private vehicle use," the groups stated. "This raises legitimate questions about the justification, proportionality, and overall cost of the benefit alongside existing transport arrangements."

### The Constitutional Shield

The contested perk sits at the center of an entrenched institutional stalemate between the Salaries and Remuneration Commission (SRC) and the bench. 

In May 2024, the High Court determined that taxable car allowances formed an integral component of judicial remuneration, bringing the benefit under the protective umbrella of Article 160(4) of the Constitution. The clause explicitly forbids varying a judge's remuneration or terms of service to their disadvantage during their tenure.

The SRC attempted to overturn that determination, but the Court of Appeal dismissed its challenge in March 2026, reinforcing the precedent that judicial allowances cannot be retroactively curtailed once granted.

Yet the civic and religious groups argue that legality alone does not resolve the broader question of fiscal proportionality. They are urging the Supreme Court to intervene, testing whether judicial benefits can remain shielded when public revenue shortfalls directly compromise essential government operations.

"Every shilling committed to a public benefit carries an opportunity cost for the country," the coalition noted, pointing out that the Ksh2.3 billion four-year outlay could otherwise reinforce primary healthcare services, classroom construction, essential medical supplies, water access, and agricultural support.

### Accountability Across the Public Service

Beyond the judicial docket, the coalition called on the SRC to apply strict fiscal sustainability benchmarks across all arms of government, evaluating whether executive, legislative, and independent office benefits align with economic capacity, skill retention, and institutional transparency.

The groups emphasized that no arm of government should operate above expenditure justification, insisting that non-diminution protections should not transform public perks into untouchable burdens during widespread household hardship.

The matter now pushes an unresolved constitutional dilemma toward the apex bench: whether the Supreme Court will interpret Article 160(4) as an absolute insulation of personal benefits, or whether public interest and economic equity can force a review of how state officials are transported.
