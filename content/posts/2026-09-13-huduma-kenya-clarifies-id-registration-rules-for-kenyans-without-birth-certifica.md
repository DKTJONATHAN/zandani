Huduma Kenya has slammed the door on a decades-old administrative workaround that allowed undocumented Kenyans to acquire national identity cards without primary birth records, declaring the birth certificate an absolute prerequisite for citizen registration.

### What we know

* Huduma Kenya shut the window allowing sworn affidavits and baptismal records to bypass birth certificate requirements for first-time identity card applicants.
* Stranded adults now face formal late-registration procedures costing Ksh700 alongside an exhaustive search for early childhood clinic cards and school records.
* Immigration authorities are standardising civil registries at the source, effectively disempowering local vetting committees that routinely greenlit uncertified identity card seekers.

The directive, issued in response to public inquiries regarding alternative documentation, ends an era where an applicant without a birth certificate could assemble a patchwork trail-a primary school leaving letter, a clinic card, a church baptismal record, and a chief’s affidavit-to stand before a sub-county vetting panel. That administrative escape hatch has been formally shut; prospective applicants must now secure an entry in the national birth register before even queuing for an identity card.

For thousands of unregistered Kenyans turning 18, the practical consequence is an immediate bureaucratic detour. Huduma Kenya directed that those lacking the document must initiate a late birth registration either through the Civil Registration Department or at a Huduma Centre located specifically in their county of birth.

That prerequisite brings heavy procedural baggage. Late birth applications trigger requirements far more rigorous than routine newborn registrations, demanding that citizens unearth decades-old infant clinic cards, school exit certificates, or baptismal entries to convince registrars of their birthplace and parentage.

The financial cost has also crystallized. Under guidelines maintained by the State Department for Immigration and Citizenship Services, a late birth certificate application attracts a Ksh500 late-entry fee alongside an additional Ksh200 issuance levy, forcing citizens to expend at least Ksh700 before their ID paperwork can even be keyed into the system.

While immigration administrators view the hardline stance as a necessary clean-up of Kenya’s foundational citizen registry, the move shifts an immense evidentiary burden onto citizens born outside formal hospital networks. By demanding a primary civil certificate upfront, the state is standardising its databases, but it is also raising the barrier to adult citizenship for those who fell through the administrative cracks at birth.
