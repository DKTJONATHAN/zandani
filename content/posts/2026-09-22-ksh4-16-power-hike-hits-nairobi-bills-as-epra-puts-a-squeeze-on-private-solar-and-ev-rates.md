Power consumers across Nairobi and beyond are staring down heavier utility bills this month following a fresh tariff revision rolled out by the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority.

Detailing the changes in an official Gazette Notice dated September 18, 2026, the regulator pegged the overall increase at Ksh4.16 per kilowatt-hour. For an average household consuming 100 units a month, the adjustment translates to roughly Ksh416 in additional baseline costs, taking effect immediately on all meter readings recorded throughout September.

The largest chunk of the increase traces back to the Fuel Energy Cost Charge, fixed at Ksh3.00 per unit. The levy is designed to offset operational expenses racked up by thermal power plants, including the Kipevu III and Rabai stations. Alongside that fuel component sits a Foreign Exchange Fluctuation Adjustment of approximately Ksh1.14 per unit, meant to buffer utility providers against foreign currency swings tied to Ksh1.32 billion in exchange liabilities. A minor Water Resources Authority levy of 1.48 cents per unit, covering water usage at major hydroelectric installations like Gitaru, Kiambere, and Turkwel, rounds out the addition.

Beyond the raw billing adjustments, the regulatory update introduces significant structural shifts for private power generators and vehicle owners. Under amended rules for net metering, consumers who generate their own renewable energy and export excess power back to the national grid will now be credited for only 50 percent of what they supply. Any unapproved energy feed-ins—termed by regulators as dumping—will see the generator billed at standard base tariffs for the power pushed into the system.

At the same time, the regulator has formalized distinct pricing brackets for electric vehicle charging stations, setting rates at Ksh16 per unit during peak hours and dropping them to Ksh8 during off-peak windows. Domestic consumers have also been re-tiered into three strict bands based on their three-month consumption averages: up to 30 units, between 30 and 100 units, and a broad upper tier stretching from 100 to 15,000 units.

The tariff adjustments land as Kenya Power moves to slow the rapid integration of variable renewable sources. Wind and solar projects currently account for over 21 percent of the national grid's total capacity and supply 34 percent of peak daytime demand, creating operational fluctuations that demand expensive thermal backup. To counter these stability risks, utilities are beginning to mandate that future solar and wind installations incorporate battery storage systems capable of delivering three to four hours of reserve power, while shifting primary focus back toward geothermal and hydroelectric baseload expansion.
