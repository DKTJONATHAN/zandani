Co-op Bank Ridgeways Mall branch manager Peter Ndumia has challenged young Kenyans to abandon the exhausting cycle of endlessly updating academic CVs and instead turn their immediate personal talents into active digital businesses.

Speaking at the latest Co-op Bank Youth Forum hosted by Violetta Ngina on Friday, October 2, Ndumia delivered a blunt reality check to graduates facing a stagnant formal job market. His message was clear: waiting for a traditional corporate paycheck while sending out identical applications is a slow path to nowhere, especially when everyday personal skills can be packaged and sold immediately.

### The Shift from Talents to Solutions

Ndumia argued that the primary obstacle for many young people is not a lack of capability, but a failure to package what they already know into a recognizable commercial service. Rather than searching for new certifications, he urged graduates to look at their existing hobbies, academic strengths, writing skills, or even the tasks friends regularly ask them to handle.

According to Ndumia, the critical pivot lies in moving from having a skill to offering a concrete solution. 

For example, a graduate who excels at mathematics should not merely list the subject as a qualification on a piece of paper. Instead, they should actively market themselves as a one-on-one tutor for students struggling with the curriculum. The same applies to photography, graphic design, coding, writing, or music—each must be framed as a direct answer to a client's immediate problem.

### Turning Social Feeds into Business Fronts

For young entrepreneurs and campus-based creators, the cost of entry into the service market has dropped significantly. Ndumia urged participants to stop viewing platforms like WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn, and Facebook purely as social spaces, and to start treating them as active, professional portfolios.

He challenged the common assumption that a freelancer requires a massive corporate network or a profile on a global gig platform to secure their first client. Instead, he advised looking inward at immediate circles—classmates, relatives, neighbors, and small local businesses.

A student who can write social media copy or design graphics does not need to wait for a multinational agency to hire them. They can approach a local retail shop, offer to improve their digital presence, and build a local reputation from there.

### The Strategic Use of Unpaid Gigs

Securing that initial client remains one of the steepest hurdles for young freelancers. To bypass this, Ndumia offered a piece of advice that runs counter to typical job-hunting wisdom: consider doing early work for free, but only under strict strategic conditions.

If a young professional chooses to take on an unpaid gig, they must document the entire process and use the final output to build their visual portfolio. The goal of unpaid work is not free labor, but acquiring the verifiable proof of capability needed to attract paying clients later on. Without a documented portfolio, the labor disappears without leaving any commercial leverage.

This shift toward self-directed gig work matches a massive macroeconomic realignment in the country. Data from the World Economic Forum indicates that by 2025, approximately 1.9 million Kenyans were already engaged in digital jobs, with roughly 1.2 million of those working directly in the gig economy.

For those still waiting for formal employment doors to open, the forum made it clear that the most reliable path to an income might not be a human resource desk, but the skills already sitting on a smartphone.
