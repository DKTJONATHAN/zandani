According to a preliminary ruling delivered by the Malindi Environment and Land Court on Wednesday, the national government and Nigerian industrialist Aliko Dangote have secured an unhindered path to press ahead with the Ksh 2.2 trillion Dangote Lamu Oil Refinery, following fatal procedural lapses by local litigants that dissolved an active stop order.

The immediate beneficiaries of the decision are the project’s joint developers, who retain an uninterrupted runway to advance site works across thousands of coastal acres, unencumbered by injunctions while the substantive dispute over title deeds and environmental clearances is pushed deep into the final quarter of the year.

Justice Jane Onyango declined to issue fresh interim orders for status quo, formally setting aside an injunction issued on September 25 that had frozen plans for the 700,000-barrel-a-day processing facility.

### Procedural Defects Derail Community Suit

![President William Ruto and Nigerian billionaire Aliko Dangote during the East African Oil Refinery grounbreaking in Lamu on September 30, 2026](https://i.ibb.co/hqrYXhF/7c49fdbcd645.webp)

The court determined that 133 residents challenging the project had failed to comply with fundamental civil litigation requirements, including an explicit directive issued on October 1 to serve legal documents on Dangote and the targeted state agencies.

"You did not serve the respondents as per the October 1, 2026, directions. Serve him and all parties within 14 days before the hearing. Leave is granted to amend the petition within seven days," Justice Onyango ruled.

Beyond the service failure, the court identified a critical defect at the heart of the community filings: the petition named an incorrect parcel of land, compelling the bench to grant the residents seven days to rectify their ownership records before any substantive legal arguments could be considered on merit.

### Competing Claims Over Coastal Acreage

The legal contest pits 133 local families against one of the largest industrial developments ever attempted in East Africa. The petitioners contend that the refinery footprint, spanning between 7,000 and 10,000 acres, encroaches upon ancestral settlements—including the Magogoni villages—without adequate property protections or statutory environmental compliance.

The state, however, maintains that the land administration framework behind the project is legally sound. Lands Cabinet Secretary Alice Wahome had earlier affirmed that the dominant share of the 7,000-acre site constitutes gazetted public land under lawful government stewardship.

![Africa's richest man, Aliko Dangote, at his Nigerian Oil Refinery on June 21, 2025.](https://i.ibb.co/wrzf94MJ/09d0a912fa20.webp)

Addressing the presence of settlement clusters across the demarcated refinery boundary, Wahome noted that while informal populations reside on sections of the site, physical occupation does not confer private ownership rights over public holdings.

### The 40-Month Delivery Race

The High Court’s refusal to renew the status quo order removes the primary legal barrier threatening the project's aggressive construction schedule. The facility was officially launched on September 30, setting off a strict 40-month construction countdown designed to culminate in commercial commissioning by 2030.

Halting ground operations at this early stage would have compromised logistical staging and heavy equipment mobilisation across the Lamu corridor, where the state envisions anchoring crude processing infrastructure connected to the wider maritime transport corridor.

While the petitioners now hold leave to amend their pleadings and serve the respondents, civil proceedings will not reconvene until the matter is mentioned before the court on November 24, leaving earthworks and infrastructure procurement free to proceed on the ground in the intervening six weeks.
