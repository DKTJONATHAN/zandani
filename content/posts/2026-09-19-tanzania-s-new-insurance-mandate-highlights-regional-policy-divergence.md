Tanzania has officially joined the ranks of regional neighbors seeking to formalize inbound travel insurance, but its path differs sharply from the experience unfolding in Kenya. Under the new Insurance (Inbound Travel Insurance) Regulations, 2026, foreign visitors entering mainland Tanzania-by land, sea, or air-are now required to secure a travel policy at a cost of Ksh5,700 ($44).

Unlike Kenya’s attempt to roll out a broad, multi-layered health insurance framework, Tanzania’s policy is specifically streamlined for short-term visitors. The coverage, which remains valid for 92 days and allows for multiple entries, is bundled through the National Insurance Corporation (NIC) or its authorized partners. It covers a defined scope: emergency medical treatment, evacuation, repatriation, and luggage loss.

### The Regional Exception

The policy explicitly carves out a shield for the regional bloc. Citizens from East African Community (EAC) and Southern African Development Community (SADC) member states are exempt from the requirement. This creates a distinct travel corridor where regional integration remains the priority, insulating cross-border trade and movement from the added costs imposed on international tourists.

For those outside these blocs, the administrative threshold is clear: proof of insurance is now a prerequisite for entry. Visitors arriving without a policy face the possibility of being denied entry, mirroring the strict enforcement posture that many regional governments are increasingly adopting to manage the financial risks associated with foreign travelers.

### A Tale of Two Implementations

The rollout in Tanzania arrives just weeks after a similar, yet far more complex, mandate in Kenya faced a sudden judicial halt. In July, Kenya introduced a comprehensive health insurance framework with high benefit limits, ranging from emergency medical expenses to mental health coverage. However, the program was swiftly suspended in August by the High Court, following a petition that challenged the legality of the requirement.

While Tanzania’s model is effectively an extension of the system already in place for Zanzibar-which implemented its own mandatory insurance in late 2024-Kenya’s stalled program remains a cautionary tale of the friction between administrative policy and domestic legal oversight.

For travelers moving through East Africa, the current landscape is increasingly fragmented. While regional citizens continue to enjoy exemptions, the cost of entry for international visitors is becoming a patchwork of state-mandated insurance schemes. Whether Tanzania’s NIC-led model faces similar legal scrutiny or gains traction as a standard regional practice remains to be seen.
