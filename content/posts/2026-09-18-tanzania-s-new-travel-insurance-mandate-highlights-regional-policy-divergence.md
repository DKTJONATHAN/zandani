Foreign nationals arriving at Tanzanian borders now face a new administrative hurdle: a mandatory inbound travel insurance policy costing Ksh5,700 ($44). The regulation, formalized under the Insurance (Inbound Travel Insurance) Regulations, 2026, applies to all visitors entering by land, sea, or air who do not hold citizenship within the East African Community (EAC) or the Southern African Development Community (SADC).

This rollout marks a significant shift in how the country manages the health and security risks associated with international tourism. The policy, which remains valid for up to 92 days and allows for multiple entries, covers essential services including emergency medical treatment, evacuation, repatriation, and luggage loss. Unlike previous localized attempts-such as the mandatory insurance scheme introduced in Zanzibar in October 2024-this mandate covers the mainland and is facilitated through the National Insurance Corporation (NIC) or its registered partners.

For the regional traveller, the policy is notable for its exemptions. By excluding citizens of the EAC and SADC, Tanzania has effectively shielded its immediate neighbors from the additional cost, maintaining a degree of regional mobility that has been a point of contention in other policy discussions.

**A Tale of Two Borders**

The implementation of this mandate highlights an emerging policy divide in East Africa. Just weeks prior, Kenya introduced its own mandatory inbound travel health insurance framework, which aimed to set a high bar for incoming visitors with a cumulative benefit limit of Ksh6.48 million ($50,000). While the spirit of the two policies-securing foreign visitors and reducing state liability-is aligned, their current status in practice could not be more different.

Kenya’s version of the policy has been effectively frozen since August. Following a legal challenge from two petitioners, Justice Francis Rayola Olel issued interim orders that stopped the implementation of the Kenyan requirements pending a full hearing. This has created a scenario where, despite the Kenyan government’s stated intent to enforce the cover, the policy remains unenforceable in the face of judicial scrutiny.

Tanzania, by contrast, has moved ahead with the directive through a Government Notice, suggesting a smoother path to integration than what has been experienced in Nairobi. For international travellers, this means that crossing the border from Kenya into Tanzania now requires navigating two vastly different legal realities: a stalled, contested system on one side and a fully operational, mandatory insurance requirement on the other.

As the policy takes effect, authorities in Tanzania have indicated that failure to produce proof of cover at the point of entry may result in a denial of admission. The long-term impact of these diverging approaches on regional tourism flows remains an open question, particularly as both nations attempt to balance revenue generation with the ease of access required to attract international visitors.
