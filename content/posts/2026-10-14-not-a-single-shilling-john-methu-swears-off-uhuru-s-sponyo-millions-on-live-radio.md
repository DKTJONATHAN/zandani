Nyandarua Senator John Methu has put his hands in the air, swearing before God that retired President Uhuru Kenyatta has not dropped a single cent into his pockets or his party’s political war chest.

Taking his seat behind the studio microphone for a local radio interview on Sunday, October 11, 2026, Methu moved to dismantle the whispered street gossip and high-decibel podium chatter framing Uhuru as the deep-pocketed sponsor pulling strings behind opposition movements ahead of the 2027 General Election.

Rather than offering polite political evasions, Methu escalated the denial into a full personal oath, daring anyone peddling the narrative to produce a single bank transfer slip or transaction trail linking his outfit to the Kenyatta estate.

“I swear before God, I have never seen a shilling from Uhuru Kenyatta,” Methu said during the broadcast. “My party leader has never received a shilling from Uhuru Kenyatta.”

### The Anatomy of the ‘Sponyo’ Tag

For months, the country's political talk tracks have leaned heavily on a single piece of street slang: *Sponyo*—the shorthand label tossed out by President William Ruto and his ruling alliance allies to depict the former head of state as an extravagant sugar daddy covertly financing anti-government operations.

According to Methu, the tag is less of an actual intelligence brief and more of an entertaining podium script designed to feed public imagination while dodging harder questions on national delivery.

Methu argued that the ruling administration simply constructed an oversized political character to take the heat whenever friction boils over. By maintaining the illusion of an all-powerful financier bankrolling dissent, the government buys itself an easy counterpunch whenever rival figures challenge its policies.

“William Ruto must get a bogeyman,” Methu stated, explaining why the catchy moniker was manufactured and circulated across weekend political rallies. The senator insisted that pinning political unrest on a wealthy retired principal serves as an effortless distraction from pressing bread-and-butter concerns facing ordinary Kenyans.

### Playing Along in Murang’a

Methu’s sworn denial arrives just weeks after Uhuru himself decided to turn the government's favorite insult into an inside joke.

During a September 18, 2026, funeral service in Kiharu, Murang’a County, the retired president grabbed the microphone, leaned into the gathering, and playfully adopted the very label meant to discredit him.

Addressing mourners who cheered his appearance, Uhuru signed off his remarks with a grin: “Ni hayo machache kutoka Sponyo… We will meet again and we will continue working together.”

The Kiharu quip instantly turbocharged the moniker across online spaces, leaving commentators and rivals guessing whether the former head of state was mocking his accusers or quietly reminding them of his enduring political heft in the Mt. Kenya heartland.

### ‘Allow Him to Retire’

While Methu was quick to clear his own pockets of the rumored sponsor money, he maintained that his stance on Uhuru's post-State House posture has been consistent from day one.

Reflecting on his past public comments, the Nyandarua lawmaker reminded listeners that he had already advised the fourth president to leave active political brawls to the current crop of contestants and take his rightful rest away from podium altercations.

“Uhuru Kenyatta is a retired president. Allow him to retire,” Methu recalled declaring at a recent public gathering.

Yet with the 2027 electoral countdown tightening and podium barbs flying across both sides of the political aisle, the phantom sponsor tag remains one of the season’s most lucrative rhetorical weapons—even as leaders like Methu go on live air to swear their ledgers remain completely untouched.
