Healthcare facilities across the country face an abrupt clinical dilemma before midnight on Wednesday: sign onto the Social Health Authority’s new three-year contracting framework or begin transferring admitted patients to compliant rivals as their digital billing access is terminated.

The ultimatum, issued by SHA Chief Executive Officer Mercy Mwangangi, establishes an unyielding operational cutoff at 11:59 p.m. on September 30. Facilities that have not executed agreements under the 2026–2029 cycle will be locked out of the authority's provider claims portal starting October 1, immediately stripping them of the ability to verify cover, seek pre-authorisations, or lodge invoices under public health schemes.

Rather than offering an administrative grace period during the handover, the authority instructed uncontracted facilities to clear their wards of scheme beneficiaries by coordinating transfers to certified providers to maintain continuous medical care.

### The Mechanics of the Portal Blackout

![SHA CEO Mercy Mwangangi](https://i.ibb.co/nMFKy2Sn/a08d0b5b23fc.webp)

The enforcement hinges on the total revocation of digital access. Under SHA's operating model, hospital revenue and patient admissions are tied directly to its electronic provider portal. Shutting down that terminal severs a facility's link to four separate statutory financing pipelines: the Primary Health Care Fund (PHCF), the Social Health Insurance Fund (SHIF), the Emergency, Chronic and Critical Illness Fund (ECCIF), and the Public Officers Medical Scheme Fund (POMSF).

Without an active digital handshake, any treatment rendered by an uncontracted facility after midnight on September 30 cannot be processed as a payable public claim, leaving hospitals either absorbing treatment expenses or turning patients away.

The rush to contract comes barely two weeks after SHA unveiled HAKIKA on September 18, a redesigned operating blueprint rolled out alongside an automated E-Contracting Platform. The system was designed to centralise licence verification, contract execution, and application tracking in an effort to remedy widespread billing frictions that marred earlier rollout phases—chiefly rejected claims, pre-authorisation bottlenecks, and persistent payment delays.

According to the Ministry of Health, HAKIKA was structured to introduce predictability, defining explicit clinical quality thresholds, dispute avenues, and standard reimbursement rates for the next three years.

### Provider Pushback on Claim Deductions

Despite the state’s push for a clean transition, the contracting rush is colliding with unresolved grievances among private and faith-based healthcare managers. The friction centers on specific covenants buried within the proposed agreements.

Providers have raised concerns over clauses governing claim adjustments, administrative deductions, and the ongoing obligations demanded of hospitals when statutory reimbursements face prolonged state delays. Under the outgoing arrangements, facility managers shouldered extensive operational burdens while navigating backlogs of unpaid dues. Entering a binding agreement through June 30, 2029, under similar terms remains an uneasy prospect for administrators who feel forced to accept contractual liabilities to avoid losing their patient base.

Yet the authority's stance leaves hospitals virtually no leverage to negotiate side terms ahead of the month-end deadline. With access to all four public funds tied directly to the digital sign-off, providers resisting the clauses must decide whether to yield to the state's terms or manage the logistical and reputational fallout of discharging patients mid-care.

The real operational test begins at dawn on October 1, when facilities log on to determine whether their portals remain active—or whether beneficiaries arriving at reception desks will need to be redirected elsewhere.
