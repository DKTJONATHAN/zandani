A systematic failure to subject power generation contracts to public competition—compounded by conflicts of interest among former energy officials—has locked Kenyan businesses and consumers into some of the highest electricity tariffs in the region, according to an audit by the World Bank.

In its November 2026 *Kenya Economic Update*, the multilateral lender connected the country's uncompetitive energy pricing directly to governance breakdowns in how power purchase agreements (PPAs) are structured and awarded. Rather than reflecting generation scarcity, the high tariffs stem from contractual terms that mandate fixed payments to private energy developers regardless of actual consumption.

### Disparity Across Regional and Benchmark Markets

The financial impact falls disproportionately on local enterprise and domestic buyers. Commercial consumers in Kenya pay an average of Ksh 22.60 per kilowatt-hour (kWh). That rate sits nearly 50 percent higher than the Ksh 15.20 per kWh paid by businesses in neighboring Uganda, and more than double the Ksh 10.10 per kWh rate in Vietnam.

Residential consumers face a similar premium. Households in Kenya pay an average of Ksh 28.40 per kWh, compared to Ksh 22.20 in Uganda, Ksh 15.40 in Morocco, and Ksh 10.10 in Vietnam.

These pricing disparities persist even as Kenya imports supplemental bulk electricity from lower-cost hydro grids in Uganda and Ethiopia to satisfy domestic demand.

### Procurement Bypasses and Revolving-Door Governance

The World Bank’s findings point to long-standing structural flaws in how Kenya Power negotiates supply deals with Independent Power Producers (IPPs). Of 42 active power purchase agreements examined in the report, only 24 percent were procured through open, competitive bidding. The remaining 76 percent were negotiated directly, sidestepping competitive price discovery.

Many of these non-competitive deals incorporated "take-or-pay" clauses. Under these terms, Kenya Power guarantees fixed capacity payments to private generators for available capacity, irrespective of whether the national grid evacuates or distributes that electricity to end users.

The lender also cited clear evidence of institutional conflict of interest within the approval hierarchy. Public officials who held direct responsibility for approving individual energy generation projects subsequently transitioned into employment or equity ownership positions within the very IPP companies they had authorized.

### Grid Leakage Compounds Tariff Overhead

The financial burden of opaque contracting is further exacerbated by operational inefficiencies across the distribution network. The report notes that Kenya loses more than 23 percent of total generated electricity through transmission and distribution leakages before power reaches commercial or domestic meters.

Because baseline PPAs mandate fixed payouts to generators at the point of production, the financial loss from this 23 percent technical and commercial leakage is absorbed into the consumer tariff structure rather than deducted from generator revenues.

The findings place renewed pressure on energy sector regulators and the Ministry of Energy to enforce strict competitive bidding mandates for all future generation capacity and re-evaluate existing take-or-pay structures that bind utility customers to above-market rates.
